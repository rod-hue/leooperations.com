35 Questions to Ask a Wedding Photographer and Videographer Before You Book

The questions that actually separate a safe hire from a risky one, why each one matters, and what to ask at the final meeting.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

The most important questions to ask a wedding photographer or videographer are about risk, not style: Are you available and who exactly will show up? What happens if you get sick or a card fails? What is in the contract about delivery dates, payments and cancellation? Style you can judge from a full gallery. Reliability you can only judge by asking, so bring the list below to every consultation and listen for specific answers, not reassurance.

We have photographed more than 1,000 events, and almost every wedding-day problem we have seen traces back to something nobody asked about in advance. This guide groups 35 questions by topic, explains why each one matters, and ends with the red flags that should make you keep looking.

How to use this list

You do not need to read all 35 questions out loud. Most good photographers and videographers answer half of them on their website, in their contract or in the first ten minutes of a call. Use the list as a checklist: anything still unanswered after the consultation, ask directly.

A few practical tips:

  • Interview at least two or three people before you sign anything. Comparing answers is how you learn what normal sounds like.
  • If you are hiring photo and video separately, ask both vendors the same questions, then ask whether they have worked together. Two teams that have never met can spend the day in each other's shots.
  • Bring whoever is paying. Parents who are funding the photography usually care most about family formals and payment terms, and it is easier to settle that before the deposit.
  • Write the answers down. If a promise matters to you, it belongs in the contract.

If you have not booked yet and are wondering how early to start, see when to book a wedding photographer. For budgets, see how much a wedding photographer costs.

Availability and logistics questions

  1. Are you available on our date, and how long will you hold it? Many vendors hold a date only until the deposit clears. Know whether a verbal hold lasts a day or a week.
  2. Will you personally shoot our wedding, or an associate? Larger studios often send associate shooters. If that is the plan, see that specific person's full galleries, not the owner's, and get their name in the contract.
  3. How many shooters are included, and when would you recommend adding one? One photographer cannot be with both partners getting ready in two locations. A second photographer solves that for photos, and the same logic applies to video: ask how many videographers are included and where each one will be.
  4. Have you worked at our venue, and if not, will you scout it? Familiarity helps with light and rules, but a professional who has never been there can still plan well if they visit or study it.
  5. What are your travel fees, and do you need lodging? Travel outside a set radius is usually extra. For a wedding in another state, ask how airfare, hotel and a rental car are billed.
  6. How many hours are included, and what does overtime cost? Overtime is usually billed by the hour or half hour. Ask whether it can be approved on the day and how it is paid.
  7. Do you need a meal and a break? Most contracts require a vendor meal for an eight-hour day. Plan it for when guests are eating, since nobody wants to be photographed chewing anyway.

Style and portfolio questions

  1. Can we see two or three full galleries from start to finish? A highlight reel shows a photographer's best 40 images from 40 weddings. A full gallery shows what you actually get, including dark receptions and rushed family photos.
  2. Can we see a full gallery from a venue or light similar to ours? An outdoor summer portfolio tells you little about a dim church or a ballroom with low ceilings.
  3. How would you describe your editing, and does it age well? Heavy filters and trendy color grades can look dated in five years. Ask how much of the look comes from editing versus the camera.
  4. How do you direct people, and how much do you step in? Some photographers are mostly documentary and rarely pose anyone. Others direct every portrait. Neither is wrong, but you should know which you are getting.
  5. Have you photographed weddings in our culture or faith tradition? Nikah, tea ceremony, sangeet, money spray, jumping the broom, a Catholic Mass with strict rules. The photographer should know when the key moments happen and where they can stand.
  6. For video: what style are your films, and can we see a full highlight film and a full ceremony edit? Cinematic, documentary and storytelling edits are very different. Our guide to wedding videography styles explains the options.
  7. For video: how do you capture vows and speeches audio? The honest answer involves lapel microphones on the officiant or couple, plus a recorder on the DJ board or podium. Camera microphones alone are not enough.

Contract and payment questions

  1. Can we see the contract before we pay anything? A professional will send it without hesitation. Read it before the call where you sign.
  2. What is the deposit, and is it refundable? Retainers are commonly non-refundable because the vendor turns away other work for your date. Know that going in.
  3. When is the balance due, and how can we pay? Most balances are due before the wedding day. Ask whether credit card fees apply.
  4. What happens if we postpone or cancel? Ask whether a deposit transfers to a new date and for how long.
  5. What happens if you have to cancel? A good contract names a refund or a replacement plan, not just an apology.
  6. Who owns the copyright, and what are we allowed to do with the images? Photographers usually keep copyright and give couples a personal-use license for printing and sharing. Ask what that covers and whether the vendor can use your images for marketing.
  7. What exactly is included in the package? Hours, shooters, number of edited images (or a range), the highlight film length, raw footage, albums and prints. Get it itemized.
  8. Are there any fees not listed? Travel, overtime, rush editing, extra galleries, drone and second-location fees all add up.

Backup and insurance questions

  1. What is your backup plan if you are sick or injured? You want a named plan, such as a trusted associate or network, not "that has never happened."
  2. Do your cameras record a redundant copy to both cards simultaneously, and which photo and video modes support that? Two card slots alone are not proof of a backup. Cameras can also be set to fill one card and then switch to the other, or to sort files (RAW on one card, JPEG on the other, or stills on one and video on the other), as Sony's recording mode guide shows. You want the simultaneous mode, so that if one card fails, the other has the photo.
  3. What backup gear do you bring? A second camera body, spare lenses, batteries and flashes at a minimum. Video teams should bring backup audio.
  4. How do you back up files after the wedding, and for how long? Look for copies in more than one location (for example local drives plus cloud) before cards are cleared.
  5. Do you carry liability insurance, and can you provide a certificate of insurance to our venue? Many venues require vendors to carry it. The venue coordinator will tell you exactly what it needs.
  6. If you are flying a drone, are you FAA certified? Commercial drone flights in the U.S. require the pilot to operate under the FAA's Part 107 rules, which includes a Remote Pilot Certificate. Your venue must also allow flights.

Delivery questions

  1. When will we get our photos and film? Turnaround varies a lot across the industry, from days to several months. Get the dates in the contract.
  2. Do we get sneak peeks? Many teams send a handful of images within a day or two so you can share something right away.
  3. How are the files delivered, and how long will the gallery stay online? Online galleries often expire. Download everything as soon as you get it and keep two copies.
  4. How many final images or minutes of film should we expect? Nobody can promise an exact number, but a range for your hours of coverage is fair to ask.
  5. Do you offer albums, prints or raw footage, and what do they cost? Raw ceremony and speech footage is something many couples wish they had asked for.

Day-of questions

  1. Will you help build our timeline and shot list? The best vendors do. A strong wedding photography timeline and a written wedding shot list prevent most day-of problems.
  2. How do you handle family formals and difficult dynamics? Divorced parents, a sick grandparent, a family member who should not be in certain photos. Tell the photographer privately and ask how they will manage it quietly.

Two bonus questions worth asking: How do you dress and behave around guests? And how do you work with the other vendors, especially the planner, DJ and officiant?

Red flags when hiring a wedding photographer

Most bad hires were warning you in advance. Walk away, or at least slow down, if you see these:

  • No full galleries. Only Instagram highlights and a reel. You cannot judge consistency from a curated feed.
  • No written contract, or pressure to pay before you read it. "We will sort out the details later" protects nobody.
  • Vague backup answers. No second camera, no simultaneous recording to two cards, no plan if they are sick.
  • No insurance when your venue requires it. You will find out three weeks before the wedding, which is too late.
  • A price that looks too good with no explanation. Very low prices sometimes mean no second shooter, no backups or no editing time.
  • Slow, sloppy communication before you have paid. It rarely improves after the deposit.
  • Delivery dates that are not in writing. "A few months" can become a year.
  • Reviews that mention missing family photos, lost files or late delivery. One complaint can be a fluke. Three is a pattern.
  • Shooters who are not the person you met and no galleries from them.

Questions to ask your wedding photographer at the final meeting

The final meeting usually happens two to six weeks before the wedding. Its job is to turn the contract into a plan. Bring your planner's timeline and these questions:

  1. Do you have the final timeline, addresses and every location change?
  2. Who is our day-of point of contact for photos, and who will help gather family?
  3. Have you seen the final family formals list with names, and is it realistic for the time we have?
  4. Where are you parking, and when do you arrive?
  5. What are the venue's rules (flash, where you can stand during the ceremony, drone, candles)?
  6. What time is sunset, and when are we stepping out for golden hour portraits?
  7. Is the ceremony unplugged, and will the officiant announce it?
  8. For video: who is speaking at the reception, and where will they stand? Is the DJ ready for a recorder on the board?
  9. Are there cultural or religious moments, people or objects you must not miss?
  10. Is there anyone who should not be photographed or posted?

Last-minute questions in the final week are usually about weather backups, vendor meals and who has the final payment. Settle those by text and keep the day itself free of decisions.

Frequently asked questions

What are 20 important questions to ask my wedding photographer?

If you only have time for 20, prioritize availability, who will shoot, full galleries, the contract and deposit, cancellation terms, backup cameras and simultaneous two-card recording, sick-day plan, insurance, delivery dates, overtime cost, travel fees, image rights, number of images, timeline help, family formals, venue experience, cultural experience, editing style, sneak peeks and gallery expiration. Those cover most of the risk.

What are common red flags when hiring a wedding photographer?

The biggest red flags are no written contract, no full galleries to review, no backup equipment or sick-day plan, no insurance when the venue requires it, and delivery dates that are not in writing. Slow or careless communication before you pay is also a strong warning sign.

What questions should I ask a wedding videographer?

Ask everything above, plus how they record vows and speech audio, how long the highlight film runs, whether you get a full ceremony and speeches edit, whether raw footage is available, what music licensing they use, and whether they fly a drone legally. Ask to watch a complete highlight film, not just a 60-second teaser.

What is the 30-5 rule for weddings?

It is an informal planning rule, not an industry standard. It says a task that takes five minutes on a normal day will take about 30 on your wedding day, so build generous buffers into every transition. Photographers love it because it protects portrait time.

Should my photographer and videographer be from the same company?

It is the simpler and safer plan. One coordinated team means one timeline, one point of contact and a photographer and videographer who plan their angles together instead of standing in each other's shots. Our guide to hiring a wedding photographer and videographer covers the trade-offs.

How many photographers do I need for my wedding?

We recommend a second photographer when you have two getting-ready locations, want a second angle on the vows, or want candids of guests while family formals are underway, because one person cannot be in two places at once. Many smaller or shorter weddings are well covered by one lead photographer. A third shooter helps with very large or multi-location days. Ask each studio exactly who is included and what each person covers.

What should I ask a photographer at the final meeting?

Confirm the timeline, locations, family formals list, venue rules, sunset time, point of contact and any cultural moments or sensitive family situations. The goal is that nobody needs to ask the couple a question on the wedding day.

How Leo Operations approaches this

Leo Operations is a national field, media and creative firm headquartered in Nashville, and our team has photographed more than 1,000 events. That volume is why we treat the questions on this list as our own checklist: backup gear, audio, written timelines and delivery dates are settled before the day, not improvised on it.

We offer photography, 4K videography or both from one coordinated team, so the couple deals with one timeline and one point of contact. For combined photo-and-video bookings, two camera professionals cover the day, one on photography and one on video, and we add a third when the timeline calls for it, for example when both partners are getting ready in separate locations. Full-day coverage runs up to eight consecutive hours, with drone exterior footage when weather and the venue allow.

Before the wedding, a planning conversation produces a written shot list and timeline, which is where most of the questions above get answered in writing. Afterward, edited photos arrive within three business days as a curated, color-corrected, high-resolution gallery, and the 5 to 7 minute highlight film, with color grading, audio enhancement and licensed music, arrives within 14 business days. We are experienced with multicultural and faith traditions, including Somali, Nigerian, Muslim and Christian weddings, and we travel to weddings in other states.
