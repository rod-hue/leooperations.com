Hiring a Destination Wedding Photographer and Videographer: Costs, Travel Fees and Logistics

Bring your own team or hire locally? How travel fees are structured, what gear and drone rules look like abroad, and how to plan multi-day events, for couples and the families funding the trip.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

A destination wedding photographer usually costs their normal wedding rate plus travel: round-trip flights for each crew member, lodging, ground transport, meals, checked-bag fees for equipment, and often a travel-day fee. For a two-person team flying domestically, travel commonly adds $1,500 to $4,000 to the bill as a market range, and international trips add more. Hiring a local photographer at the destination can trim the travel line, but you give up the in-person relationship and take on the job of vetting someone you may never meet before the wedding.

Destination weddings are not a niche. In The Knot's data on weddings in 2024, 18% of couples had a destination wedding. Among US couples who had a destination wedding, the top locations were Mexico, Italy, Puerto Rico (a US territory, so no passport needed), Ireland and Colombia, per The Knot Worldwide's 2025 Trends to Watch report.

Should you bring your own photographer or hire a local one?

The right choice depends on how many events you are hosting, how much the relationship matters to you and how easy the destination is to reach.

FactorTraveling photographer (yours)Local photographer at destination
CostBase fee plus travelBase fee only, sometimes lower local rates
RelationshipYou meet in person, often do engagement photos togetherMostly video calls
Knowledge of venue and lightLearns on a scouting dayAlready knows it
Style consistency with engagement photosSame eye, same editDifferent
Language and local permitsMay need a local fixerUsually handles it
Drone legalityMay be restricted as a foreign operatorOften licensed locally
Backup if sickStudio's backup may be far awayLocal network nearby
Video and photo working togetherEasy if one teamMust coordinate two vendors

Our take: if the wedding is about family and multi-day events, bring the team you trust. They already know your family's names, your traditions and your engagement-photo style, and that knowledge is worth more than any travel fee on a weekend with three ceremonies. For a small beach ceremony with a handful of guests, a proven local photographer can fit, but only after you have reviewed full galleries from that exact venue or region.

How much does a photographer cost for a destination wedding?

Start with the national baseline. The average US wedding photographer runs about $2,900 to $3,000 according to The Knot's recent Real Weddings Study figures (as reported here), and the average videographer about $2,300 (The Knot). Destination coverage adds travel and usually more hours, because destination weddings are rarely a single eight hour day.

How travel fees are structured

Photographers handle travel in one of four ways. Ask which one, and get it in writing.

  1. Flat travel fee. One number covers everything. Simple to budget; the photographer carries the risk of airfare changes.
  2. Actual costs, itemized. You reimburse flights, lodging and transport at cost, often with a cap. Transparent, but harder to predict.
  3. All-inclusive destination package. A higher package price that includes travel to anywhere in a region or the world.
  4. Couple books directly. You book the crew's flights and rooms (often in the resort block). Works well for resort weddings.

Typical line items, as a market guide:

ItemWhat to expect
AirfarePer crew member, round trip, plus checked bags for gear
LodgingUsually 2 to 4 nights; a room for each crew member or shared if agreed
Ground transportRental car, shuttle or rideshare
MealsPer diem or covered at events
Travel daysMany charge a half or full day rate for days spent flying
Resort vendor feeSome all-inclusive resorts charge outside vendors a fee, sometimes several hundred dollars or more per vendor
PermitsLocation permits for public sites, sometimes drone permits

That resort fee surprises many couples. Read the resort's outside vendor policy before you book anyone.

Logistics: gear, permits and drone rules abroad

Equipment and customs

A two-person photo and video crew can travel with tens of thousands of dollars in cameras, lenses and audio gear. Experienced travelers:

  • Carry cameras, lenses and memory cards on the plane, never checked.
  • Follow the FAA's rules for passengers and batteries: spare lithium batteries go in carry-on bags only, with terminals protected. Spares rated 101 to 160 watt-hours need airline approval and are limited to two per person, and spares over 160 watt-hours are not allowed in passenger baggage at all. Airlines and foreign carriers can be stricter, so check before packing large video and lighting batteries.
  • Register expensive equipment with U.S. Customs (CBP Form 4457) before international trips so it is not taxed on return.
  • Bring duplicate bodies and redundant storage, since replacing gear abroad is hard.
  • Carry liability insurance, and check whether the venue requires a certificate naming them.

Drone rules abroad

This is where many destination films lose their aerial shots. Rules differ sharply by country.

  • United States and territories (including Puerto Rico): FAA Part 107 applies. Commercial pilots need a Remote Pilot Certificate.
  • European Union (Italy, Ireland, Spain, Greece and others): EASA says non-EU residents must register with the aviation authority of the first EU country where they plan to fly, and US training is not recognized. Under EASA's open category rules, requirements scale with the drone's class: a sub-250-gram C0 drone needs only the pilot to read the manual, while C1 through C4 drones require EASA's A1/A3 online training and exam, and A2 operations add a further certificate of competency. Registration is still required for any camera drone that is not a toy.
  • Mexico: Drones over 250 grams must be registered with the aviation authority AFAC, and several guides report that foreign operators effectively cannot get commercial authorization. The usual solution is hiring a licensed local drone operator.
  • Everywhere: Many resorts, historic sites and protected areas ban drones regardless of national law.

If aerial footage matters to you, ask your team to confirm the specific country's rules in writing months ahead.

Work permits and local rules

Some countries require work authorization or a business visa for foreign photographers doing paid work, and a venue's customary practice is not the same as legal permission. A professional team confirms the requirement with the country's consulate or immigration authority before signing a contract for that country, and your planner can help connect you with local requirements for permits at specific sites.

Domestic destination weddings

Many destination weddings never leave the country. Couples pick a city that is fun for guests: New Orleans, Charleston, Savannah, Napa, Asheville, Austin, Scottsdale, the Florida coast, or Nashville, which draws couples for its music, food, historic venues and rolling countryside in nearby Franklin and Leiper's Fork.

Domestic destinations simplify a lot: no passports, no customs, FAA rules for drones, and much cheaper crew travel. You can often hire a team based in the destination city and avoid travel fees altogether, or drive in a team from a neighboring state. For Nashville specifically, a local team will know which downtown rooftops allow photographers, how long it takes to get from a Broadway hotel to a Franklin estate on a Saturday afternoon, and where to find shade in July.

Planning welcome parties and multi-day events

Destination weddings stretch across a weekend, and your coverage should match the events you actually care about.

DayTypical eventsCoverage worth considering
ThursdayArrivals, welcome drinksOptional, 1 to 2 hours
FridayWelcome party or rehearsal dinner; mehndi, henna night or traditional engagement ceremony for many cultures2 to 4 hours
SaturdayWedding day8 to 10 hours
SundayFarewell brunch, beach or city portraits, day-after session1 to 3 hours

A few practical notes:

  • Day-after sessions are one of the best values of a destination wedding. No timeline pressure, golden light, your attire is already there.
  • Multicultural weekends often have more than one ceremony (a nikah and a reception, a traditional Nigerian engagement and a church wedding). Each needs its own plan and sometimes its own outfit-change schedule.
  • Rest for the crew. A team shooting 18 hours over two days needs proper breaks and meals, or the quality drops by Saturday night.
  • Shot list and timeline: share a full itinerary with times, addresses and family names well in advance. Our wedding photography timeline and wedding shot list guides show how to build both.

Questions to ask a destination wedding photographer

  1. Have you shot at this venue, region or country before? Can I see a full gallery?
  2. How do you structure travel fees, and what is the cap?
  3. When will you arrive, and is there a scouting visit?
  4. Who handles flights and lodging, you or us?
  5. What happens if a flight is canceled or you get sick? Who is your backup there?
  6. Are you allowed to fly a drone here, and who is the licensed pilot?
  7. Does our resort charge an outside vendor fee?
  8. How do you back up files while traveling?
  9. Do you cover the welcome party, rehearsal or day-after session, and at what rate?
  10. Do photo and video come from one team, or will we coordinate two vendors?

Frequently asked questions

How much does a photographer cost for a destination wedding?

Expect the photographer's normal rate (the US average is around $3,000 for the wedding day) plus travel. A two person crew flying domestically commonly adds $1,500 to $4,000 in flights, lodging and travel days, and international trips more. Multi-day coverage increases the base fee too.

Is it cheaper to hire a local photographer for a destination wedding?

It usually lowers the travel line, but you give up meeting in person, style consistency with your engagement photos and a team that already knows your family. If you go local, vet the photographer with full galleries from that venue and at least one video call.

Do destination wedding photographers charge travel fees?

Almost all do, either as a flat fee, itemized actual costs, or built into an all-inclusive destination package. Some also charge for travel days. Get the structure and any cap in the contract.

Can my photographer fly a drone at my destination wedding?

It depends on the country and venue. In the US and Puerto Rico, a pilot needs an FAA Part 107 certificate. EU countries require operator registration and, for most camera drones above the sub-250-gram C0 class, EASA's online training and exam, and Mexico effectively restricts commercial flights by foreign operators. Confirm in writing.

How far in advance should I book a destination wedding photographer?

Twelve to eighteen months is a good target, since travel blocks out more days on the photographer's calendar. Book after the venue and date are final. Guests also need time, and save-the-dates for destination weddings often go out 8 to 12 months ahead.

Should our photographer cover the welcome party?

If your family will be together for the first time in years, yes. Welcome parties and rehearsal dinners are relaxed and full of candid moments with relatives you may not see again soon. Two to four hours is usually plenty.

Do we pay for the photographer's hotel?

Usually, yes, either directly through the resort block or reimbursed through the travel fee. Agree on room type and number of nights up front.
