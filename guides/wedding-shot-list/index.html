The Wedding Shot List: Every Must-Have Photo and Video Shot, Plus a Family Photo Template

What to put on your list, what to leave to your photographer, and how to run family photos quickly with a named, ordered list.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

A wedding shot list should contain the photos your photographer cannot guess: named family groupings, special people, heirlooms and cultural or faith moments. The obvious shots (the kiss, the rings, the first dance) are standard priorities for any professional, so confirm them in the interview rather than writing them out. Keep your written list to one page, put names next to every family grouping, and give it to the photographer and videographer at least two weeks before the wedding.

Below is the complete list by part of the day, so you can see what a full day covers and circle what matters to you. After that: a family-grouping template, cultural moments worth adding, and a separate videography shot list.

What to include in a wedding shot list (and what to leave off)

A good list is short and specific. The best ones we receive have three sections:

  • People. Every family grouping with names, plus anyone who matters but is easy to miss (a godmother, a grandfather in a wheelchair, the friend who introduced you).
  • Objects and places. Heirloom jewelry, a grandmother's Bible, a parent's wedding photo, the house you grew up in if you are getting ready there.
  • Moments. Anything unusual or cultural that happens at a set time, like a tea ceremony, a money dance, a choir song or a surprise performance.

Leave off generic shots copied from Pinterest. A 200-line list makes the photographer stare at paper instead of watching your day, and an experienced shooter's instincts will beat it every time. Confirm during the interview that your photographer covers the classics as standard (our list of questions to ask a wedding photographer helps).

Getting ready and detail shots

Details

  • Rings together, plus each band separately
  • Invitation suite with any envelope, menu and program
  • Dress or suit hanging, shoes, veil, tie, cufflinks, watch
  • Jewelry, fragrance, heirlooms, something borrowed
  • Bouquet and boutonnieres
  • Letters or gifts the couple exchanges

Getting ready

  • Hair and makeup in progress, including the final lipstick or the last pin
  • A parent or best friend helping with a dress, buttons, tie or shoes
  • Reading a letter from your partner (reaction shots)
  • Wedding party in matching robes or outfits, toasts with mimosas
  • Final mirror look, alone and with a parent
  • Each partner fully dressed, alone, before leaving the room

If both partners get ready in different places, one photographer cannot cover both. This is one of the strongest reasons to book a second photographer, or to plan with a coordinated photo and video team how to split the rooms. If a photographer and a videographer split up, each room is covered in one medium at that point, so decide in advance which room gets stills and which gets video, or stagger the dressing times so both can cover the key moments together.

First look shots

  • The walk up, from behind the person waiting
  • The moment of turning around, wide and tight
  • Reactions from both partners, then the embrace
  • A wide shot showing the setting
  • First looks with a parent, grandparent or the wedding party
  • Private vow or letter reading, if you do one

The first look guide covers how to set this up, including first looks with dad and the bridal party.

Ceremony shots

  • Venue wide before guests arrive, plus the altar, aisle and decor
  • Guests arriving and finding seats
  • Officiant and partner waiting at the front
  • Wedding party processional, then flower girl and ring bearer
  • The walk down the aisle, and the waiting partner's reaction at the same moment
  • Giving away or escort moment, with parent or parents
  • Wide shots from the back showing the whole room
  • Readings, songs and any unity ritual
  • Vows and ring exchange, close
  • The kiss, wide and tight
  • Recessional, plus guests' reactions
  • The couple's first minute alone after the ceremony

Ask your officiant to step aside for the kiss so they are not standing in the middle of it. It sounds small. It makes the photo.

Family formals: a family grouping template

This is the section that most needs to be written down. Use names, not roles, and order the list from biggest group to smallest so people can leave as you go. Here is a template that covers most families. Once you expand it (each parent alone counts as two photos, the Partner B repeat doubles the first block, and special people can add several), most lists land somewhere around 15 to 25 groupings, so count your own final named list:

#GroupingExample names to fill in
1Couple with Partner A's full extended familyGrandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins
2Couple with Partner A's immediate family plus grandparentsParents, siblings, siblings' spouses and kids, grandparents
3Couple with Partner A's grandparentsNames
4Couple with Partner A's parents and siblingsNames
5Couple with Partner A's parentsNames
6Partner A with each parent aloneNames
7Partner A with siblingsNames
8Repeat 1 to 7 for Partner BNames
9Couple with both sets of parentsNames
10Couple with both immediate families togetherEveryone from 4 and Partner B's version
11Special peopleGodparents, guardian, mentor, officiant

Rules that keep this fast:

  • Start with the largest group and subtract. Elderly relatives go first so they can sit down.
  • Do the families back to back, one side then the other, rather than alternating.
  • Plan two to three minutes per grouping, then multiply by your final count. Fifteen groupings take 30 to 45 minutes and twenty-five take 50 to 75 minutes, and our timeline guide shows where that fits (wedding photography timeline).
  • Assign a family helper who knows everyone and has the list on their phone.
  • Ask the officiant to announce which family members should stay after the ceremony.
  • Note sensitive situations privately: divorced parents who should not stand together, a stepparent who should be in some photos, a recent loss.

Extended family beyond the template (third cousins, great-aunts' husbands) is better captured as candids at the reception or in one large group photo.

Wedding party and couple portraits

Wedding party

  • Full wedding party, posed and candid
  • Each side with their partner
  • Each partner with every attendant individually
  • Flower girl and ring bearer, ideally early before they melt down
  • A fun or walking shot

Couple portraits

  • Classic full-length and close portraits
  • Walking shots and candid laughing shots
  • Details: hands with rings, the back of the dress, the veil in the wind
  • A wide shot showing the venue or city
  • Golden hour portraits, 15 to 20 minutes before sunset
  • A night shot with lights, sparklers or the venue lit up, if timing allows
  • Drone exterior views, if your team offers them, the venue allows flights, the weather cooperates and the airspace is legal to fly in (commercial drone pilots operate under the FAA's Part 107 rules, which include airspace authorization near airports)

Reception shots

  • Room reveal before guests enter: tables, centerpieces, place cards, cake, the sweetheart table
  • Grand entrance of the wedding party and the couple
  • Toasts, and the reactions of the couple and parents during each one
  • First dance, parent dances and any special dances
  • Cake cutting and the first bite
  • Table candids and guests' reactions
  • Band or DJ and the dance floor at full energy
  • Bouquet and garter toss, if you do them
  • Grandparents on the dance floor
  • Late-night food, send-off with sparklers, bubbles or a getaway car

What are the classic traditional wedding shots? Most people mean the walk down the aisle, the kiss, the recessional, the family group, the wedding party, the cake cutting, the first dance and the send-off. These are standard priorities for every professional. Venue rules, a blocked angle or two events happening at once can still cost a frame, so name your true must-haves and confirm them before the day.

Cultural and faith moments to add

Many of the moments couples remember most are not on any generic checklist. If your wedding includes them, add them by name and note when they happen. A few examples we see:

  • Nigerian weddings: the traditional engagement ceremony, gele tying, family introductions, the money spray during dances, and outfit changes for the reception.
  • Somali and other Muslim weddings: the Nikah, signing of the marriage contract, and in some families separate celebrations for men and women, which affects who can photograph each. Ask the family who may be photographed and where.
  • Christian ceremonies: communion, a blessing from the pastor, a choir or praise team song, and church rules about flash and movement during the service.
  • Jewish ceremonies: ketubah signing, the chuppah, the breaking of the glass and the hora.
  • South Asian ceremonies: mehndi, haldi, baraat, pheras or the Anand Karaj, and multiple outfit changes over several days.
  • Chinese and Vietnamese ceremonies: tea ceremony with each elder, door games, and red-envelope traditions.
  • African American traditions: jumping the broom, libation ceremonies and family prayers.
  • Latin American traditions: the lazo, arras and padrinos.

The single most useful thing you can do is tell your photographer who leads each tradition and what to watch for. Faith venues often have strict rules about where photographers can stand, so confirm those in advance.

Videography shot list

Video needs its own list because the priorities are different. The most important item is audio, and nobody can fix bad audio later.

Audio (most important)

  • Vows and ring exchange, recorded on lapel microphones on the officiant and one partner, plus a backup recorder
  • Every toast and speech, recorded from the microphone or DJ board
  • Readings, songs and prayers during the ceremony
  • Letters read aloud during getting ready or the first look

Moments on camera

  • Getting ready, the dress going on, final reactions
  • First look, with sound if you are exchanging private words
  • Processional, full ceremony from at least two angles, the kiss and recessional
  • Grand entrance, every toast, first dance and parent dances in full
  • Cake cutting, bouquet toss, peak dance-floor moments, send-off

Atmosphere and B-roll

  • Venue exteriors and interiors, decor and details in motion
  • Guests arriving, hugging, laughing
  • Drone exteriors of the venue, when weather, venue permission and airspace rules allow
  • Short interviews or messages from parents and friends

Ask your videographer whether you receive the full ceremony and full speeches as separate edits, not only a highlight film. Many couples wish they had. Our guide to wedding videography styles explains what each edit type includes.

Frequently asked questions

What to include in a wedding shot list?

Include named family groupings, special people, heirlooms and any cultural or religious moments, plus anything unusual that happens at a set time. Skip writing out generic shots like the kiss or the rings, since those are standard priorities, but confirm in the interview that they are covered. One page is the right length.

What shots to get at a wedding?

The core shots are details, getting ready, the first look if you have one, the processional and vows, the kiss, family and wedding party groups, couple portraits, toasts, dances, cake and the send-off. Add cultural moments and important people by name.

What are some classic traditional wedding shots?

The walk down the aisle, the waiting partner's reaction, the ring exchange, the kiss, the recessional, the family group, the full wedding party, the cake cutting, the first dance and the send-off. These are the frames most families will print and frame.

How do I organize family photos at a wedding?

Write a numbered list with names, starting with the largest group and getting smaller. Do one side of the family, then the other, assign a helper who knows everyone, and ask the officiant to announce who should stay after the ceremony.

What is the 30-5 rule for weddings?

It is an informal planning rule that says a five-minute task takes about 30 minutes on your wedding day. For shot lists, it means a 40-item family list will take far longer than you think. Trim the formals and capture extended family candidly.

What should be on a wedding videography shot list?

Start with audio: vows, ring exchange, toasts and any letters read aloud. Then list the moments you want in full (ceremony, toasts, first dance, parent dances), plus atmosphere shots and any messages from family. Confirm you will receive full ceremony and speech edits.

Should I give my photographer a shot list?

Yes, but keep it to the things they cannot know: names, family dynamics, heirlooms and traditions. A long list of generic poses usually makes the photos worse, because the photographer is reading instead of watching.

How Leo Operations approaches this

At Leo Operations, the shot list is not a form you fill out alone. A planning conversation with the couple (and often the parents) produces a written shot list and timeline, with every family grouping named, every sensitive family situation noted privately, and every cultural or faith moment placed on the clock. We are a national media and creative firm headquartered in Nashville, we have photographed more than 1,000 events, and our wedding team travels to couples in other states.

Photography, 4K videography or both come from one coordinated team. Two camera professionals cover most weddings. On photo-only bookings that means two photographers, so one can run family formals while the other catches candids. On combined photo-and-video bookings it means a photographer and a videographer, and we add a third camera professional when the timeline needs formals and candids at the same time. Coverage runs up to eight consecutive hours, with drone exterior footage when weather, the venue and airspace rules allow. We have shot many multicultural and faith weddings, including Somali, Nigerian, Muslim and Christian celebrations, so we know to ask who leads each tradition and where cameras are welcome. Edited photos arrive within three business days, and the 5 to 7 minute highlight film, with color grading, audio enhancement and licensed music, within 14 business days.
