How to get endorsements for a political campaign, from the first ask to the last mail piece

Who to ask, in what order, what to say, the legal rules on who can endorse, and how to turn a name on a list into votes.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

You get political endorsements by asking for them in a deliberate order. Lock in a handful of credible local names before you announce, use them to prove you are viable, then go after the unions, issue groups and elected officials who wait to see who can win. The candidate makes each ask personally, with a specific request and a signed permission form ready. The endorsements that move the most voters are the surprising ones: a study of newspaper endorsements by economists Chun-Fang Chiang and Brian Knight found that unexpected picks persuaded about 3 percent of readers to switch, and that credibility drove the effect.

Do endorsements help a political campaign win?

Yes, and the reason is information. Most voters in a city council, judicial, school board or state legislative race know very little about either candidate. A name they trust on your mail piece or door hanger does work that months of paid advertising would otherwise have to do. In a primary or nonpartisan race, with no party label to guide people, an endorsement is often the most useful fact a voter gets.

Credibility is what makes an endorsement persuasive. Chiang and Knight found that endorsements of a Democrat by left-leaning papers carried less weight than the same pick from neutral or right-leaning papers, and the pattern held in reverse. Endorsements were also more influential among moderate voters. Translate that to a local race: a retired police chief backing a reform candidate tells undecided voters something they did not already assume.

Endorsements bring resources too: a union's member mail and volunteers, an official's donors, a neighborhood leader's network of people who will hear you out.

Know how big endorsers operate. In 2026 President Trump issued 315 Republican primary endorsements with a 96 percent success rate, according to Ballotpedia data, and the Deseret News noted that the rate may be skewed by races where his candidate ran unopposed. Endorsers want winners. You have to look like one before the biggest names commit.

How to build an endorsement target list

Start with your voter math, not your contact list. Figure out how many votes you need, which groups of voters you have to win, and who each group already trusts. If you need older homeowners in two precincts, the person who matters might be a neighborhood association president nobody downtown has heard of.

Sort targets into tiers:

  • Tier 1: endorsers who bring real resources (unions with field programs, groups with mail budgets, officials with large followings). Usually five to fifteen names.
  • Tier 2: validators who speak to a specific bloc you need, such as a respected pastor, a coach or a veterans' group leader.
  • Tier 3: community names that make your public list long and local. They matter in aggregate.

For every target, record who on your team knows them, who they endorsed last cycle, what they care about and who they are feuding with. Keep it in one shared spreadsheet with columns for tier, connector, ask date, status, permission level (name only, title, photo, quote, video) and assets collected. Then hunt for cross-pressured validators, because the endorsement that surprises voters is the one that persuades them.

Endorsement strategy: the order and timing that works

Endorsements build on each other. The first ten names are the hardest because each endorser is taking a risk on an unproven candidate. After that, every new name lowers the risk for the next one. Most campaigns start this too late and announce with a bare list.

PhaseWhat to do
2 to 6 months before launchQuiet meetings with your closest allies. Secure 5 to 15 credible names for launch day.
Launch dayRelease the list with your announcement. A long, local list signals viability.
First 90 daysHit fundraising and field benchmarks you can show Tier 1 targets. Request every group's questionnaire.
Questionnaire and screening seasonSubmit on time, prepare for interviews, follow up within 24 hours.
Final 6 weeksRoll out held endorsements one at a time to drive news cycles. Put your strongest validators on mail, video and door scripts.

Get each organization's calendar in writing early, because many decide in batches. Run for Something, for example, makes endorsements roughly every 6 to 8 weeks, and its deadline for the next class of 2027 local and state legislative candidates was Sunday, November 8, 2026. Miss a window and you may wait months.

Hold two or three strong names for October, when everyone else has run out of news.

How to ask for an endorsement

The candidate asks. Staff can schedule and follow up, but endorsers want to look the candidate in the eye.

  1. Get a warm introduction from someone the endorser trusts. A cold email to a sitting official rarely gets a yes.
  2. Request a 20 to 30 minute meeting, in person when possible.
  3. Bring a one-page brief: why you are running, your path to victory, money raised, field plan and current endorsers.
  4. Listen first. Ask what they need from the next person in the seat.
  5. Make a direct, specific ask: "I'd like your endorsement, and I'd like to use your name and photo on my website and mail."
  6. If the answer is "not yet," ask what they need to see and when you can come back. Then come back.
  7. Send the endorsement form and a thank-you within 24 hours of a yes.
  8. Make a second ask: host a house party, record a short video or knock doors with you on a Saturday.

The second ask is where most campaigns leave value on the table. An endorser who hosts a fundraiser or brings ten volunteers is worth far more than a name on a list.

Sample letter asking for an endorsement

Use a letter or email after a meeting, or for Tier 3 endorsers you cannot meet in person. Keep it under 250 words. The names and numbers below are placeholders.

Dear Ms. Johnson,

Thank you for your years of work on the Eastside Neighborhood Association. I am running for City Council in District 5 because our streets, parks and bus service have not kept pace with the families moving here, and I want to fix that.

I am asking for your endorsement. With your permission, I would list your name and title on my website and campaign materials, with titles listed for identification purposes only.

So far, 42 neighbors, two former council members and the District 5 teachers' association have endorsed our campaign. We have knocked on 3,100 doors and raised $38,000 from 410 donors.

I would welcome 20 minutes to hear what you want from the next council member. I will call your office Thursday to find a time.

Sincerely, Jordan Lee, Candidate for City Council, District 5

What to put on an endorsement form

Get every endorsement in writing before you publish it. The form should capture the endorser's name exactly as they want it shown, title and affiliation, a line confirming the title is for identification only, an approved quote, a photo or permission to take one, and checkboxes for where you may use it (website, mail, digital ads, TV, door literature). Add a signature and date. Never list a name you do not have in writing, and never reword an approved quote without asking again.

Endorsement questionnaires, screenings and interviews

Unions, issue groups and party clubs usually screen candidates with a written questionnaire, and many add an interview or a membership vote. Treat it like debate prep.

  • Answer honestly and consistently. Assume members, reporters and eventually your opponent will see your answers. Keep a master answer file so every form says the same thing.
  • Show your path to victory: budget, money raised, voter contact plan and door counts.
  • Expect field requirements. Run for Something requires endorsed candidates to run a field program with the candidate knocking doors and calling voters. A real canvassing program wins endorsements and races.
  • Do not over-promise. Commitments made in a screening follow you into office.
  • Rehearse the interview with a staffer playing the most skeptical committee member.

Who can legally endorse a candidate?

Several kinds of endorsers face legal limits. Know them so you never put an ally in a bad position.

Corporations and labor unions

In federal races, a corporation or labor organization may endorse a candidate and announce it to its restricted class and the general public, and it may talk with candidates while deciding. Communications to its restricted class may be coordinated with the candidate. Under 11 CFR 114.4, spending to announce an endorsement to the general public stays outside contribution rules only if it is not coordinated with the candidate. Once express advocacy spending to the restricted class tops $2,000 for an election, the organization reports it on FEC Form 7. State and local races follow state law.

Churches, pastors and charities

As of November 2026, the Johnson Amendment still bars 501(c)(3) organizations, including churches, from endorsing candidates. In July 2025 the IRS agreed to a proposed consent judgment that would have let houses of worship endorse candidates to their congregations, but a federal court rejected it on March 31, 2026, and the rule stayed in force for the November 3, 2026 election. The Justice Department has since argued on appeal that the ban should not apply to churches.

Pastors and nonprofit leaders can still endorse personally. IRS Publication 1828 describes a candidate-paid ad listing ministers who personally endorsed, noting that titles and affiliations are for identification purposes only, and says it is not campaign intervention by the church. Use that exact language. Our guide to churches and political candidates covers candidate visits and forums.

Federal employees and judges

Under the Hatch Act, a less restricted federal employee may endorse a partisan candidate but must do so in a personal capacity and may not identify their federal position. The ABA Model Code of Judicial Conduct, Rule 4.1, bars judges and judicial candidates from publicly endorsing candidates and from seeking or using endorsements from a political organization, except as law permits. Each state adopts its own version, so judicial candidates should read their state code first.

Newspapers

By Nieman Lab's count, roughly three-quarters of major American newspapers did not endorse for president in 2024. Where your local paper still endorses, prepare seriously. Fewer competing voices make each one count.

How to use endorsements once you have them

An endorsement only works if voters see it. Build each one into every channel:

  • Website: an endorsements page with photos, titles and short quotes, updated weekly on your campaign website.
  • Social and digital: one graphic or short video per endorser, plus digital ads targeted to the voters that endorser speaks to.
  • Video: a 30 to 60 second testimonial from a trusted validator often outperforms anything the candidate says about themselves. See our political video production work.
  • Mail: dedicate at least one piece to your strongest validators, sent to the households that trust them. Our direct mail guide covers costs.
  • Field: put endorsements in canvass scripts and on door literature. "Your neighbor Ms. Johnson is supporting me" opens doors.

Then send endorsers a short monthly update on doors and dollars. Endorsers who feel included recruit the next ones.

Frequently asked questions

How do you get endorsements for a political campaign?

Build a tiered target list based on the voters you need, secure 5 to 15 credible local names before launch, then pursue unions, issue groups and officials on their calendars. The candidate makes each ask personally, with a one-page brief and a written permission form. Show viability with fundraising and field numbers.

How do I write a letter asking for an endorsement?

Recognize the person's work, say in a sentence or two why you are running, and ask directly for their endorsement and permission to use their name. Add proof of momentum, such as current endorsers, doors knocked and dollars raised. Close with a specific follow-up, like a call on a named day.

How do you request an endorsement from an elected official?

Get a warm introduction from someone the official trusts, then request a short in-person meeting. Bring your path to victory and fundraising numbers, and come back if the first answer is "not yet."

What is a good sentence for an endorsement?

Keep it personal and tie it to one specific detail. For example: "I have worked beside Jordan Lee on our neighborhood association for six years, and nobody fights harder for District 5's parks and streets." The endorser must approve the final wording in writing.

Can a church or pastor endorse a political candidate?

A church as an organization still cannot endorse under the Johnson Amendment as of November 2026, after a federal court rejected a proposed IRS settlement in March 2026; an appeal is pending. A pastor can endorse in a personal capacity, outside official church functions and church publications, with any title listed for identification purposes only.

Can a business or union endorse a candidate?

Yes. Under FEC rules a corporation or labor union may endorse a federal candidate and tell both its restricted class and the public. Communications to its restricted class may be coordinated with the campaign, while public announcements must not be. State and local races follow state law.

When should a candidate start seeking endorsements?

Start two to six months before you announce, with quiet meetings to secure a launch-day list. Ask organizations for their screening calendars as soon as you decide to run, and hold a few strong endorsements for the final six weeks.

Do endorsements actually help candidates win?

Yes, especially in primaries, nonpartisan races and down-ballot contests where voters have little other information. Research on newspaper endorsements found that surprising, credible endorsements changed votes, with the biggest effect among moderates. Endorsers also bring volunteers, donors and member communication.
