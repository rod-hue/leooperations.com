Signature Gathering for a Ballot Initiative: What It Costs and How It Works

How to get a measure on the ballot, from state signature requirements and cost per signature to circulator pay rules, validity padding, legal challenges and the campaign after you qualify.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

Putting a citizen initiative on a statewide ballot usually means collecting tens or hundreds of thousands of valid voter signatures before a hard deadline, and in most states that now costs millions of dollars. Ballotpedia found that paid initiative drives in 2024 averaged a record $14.87 per required signature, with $172 million spent on signature drives for 53 initiatives in 18 states. Circulators themselves were being paid $12 to $15 a signature in parts of California in 2026. Budget for far more raw signatures than the legal minimum, because a large share will be thrown out, and remember that qualifying is only the first half of the campaign.

How do you get a measure on the ballot?

Ballotpedia counts 26 states that allow some form of citizen-initiated ballot measure, plus many cities and counties with their own processes (Ballotpedia News, 2026). The steps are broadly similar everywhere:

  1. Draft the measure. Work with lawyers who know your state's single-subject and form rules. Drafting errors are the most common reason measures get knocked off the ballot later.
  2. File it with the state. Most states require a filing, a fee in some cases, and an official title and summary written by the attorney general or secretary of state. Ballotpedia tracks filing fees by state.
  3. Get approval to circulate. Some states require the petition form itself to be approved before a single signature is collected.
  4. Collect signatures within the state's circulation window and distribution rules.
  5. Submit, then survive verification by county or state election officials.
  6. Defend against legal challenges, which can come before, during and after verification.
  7. Run the campaign to win the vote.

Signature requirements vary by state

Requirements are usually a percentage of the votes cast in the last governor's or presidential race, which means the number changes every few years. NCSL keeps a state-by-state table and Ballotpedia has a page for each state. A few examples show the range:

Distribution requirements are the hidden cost driver. When you must collect a minimum in many counties or districts, you cannot just work the big-city sidewalks where signatures are cheapest. Rural signatures cost more per name and require travel and lodging for crews.

How much does it cost to put something on the ballot?

Ballotpedia measures this with cost per required signature (CPRS): total spent on the signature drive divided by the number of signatures legally required (Ballotpedia_analyses)). Because campaigns collect far more than the minimum, CPRS is higher than what anyone pays per raw signature. It is still the best apples-to-apples way to budget.

Other 2024 figures from Ballotpedia's 2024 signature cost analysis:

  • $172.15 million spent on signature drives for 53 initiatives in 18 states, the most since at least 2010.
  • The average successful drive cost $3.25 million, down from a record $4.08 million in 2022.
  • The highest CPRS was Montana's CI-128 at $48.21. Arkansas had the highest state average at $41.34, then Montana at $27.42.
  • The single most expensive drive was Florida's Amendment 3 at $23.09 million.
  • Volunteer drives for Idaho's top-four primary measure and a South Dakota referendum spent nothing on paid gathering.

New America's analysis of the same data found the median statewide signature drive cost about $5.8 million in 2024, up from roughly $315,000 in 2012, and that signature gathering typically eats one-fifth to one-quarter of a measure's total support spending (New America).

For a quick estimate, multiply your required signatures by a CPRS in the range your state has seen recently. A 100,000-signature requirement at $10 to $15 CPRS means a $1 million to $1.5 million drive. A California constitutional amendment at those rates is a $9 million to $13 million drive before any persuasion spending.

How much do petition circulators get paid per signature?

It depends on the state, the season and the competition. Older NCSL figures put typical per-signature pay at $1 to $3, occasionally up to $10. Those days are gone in busy states. In early 2026, circulators on dueling California measures were paid $12 to $15 per signature, up from about $4 when the drives started, according to data cited by the San Francisco Standard (San Francisco Standard, 2026). When several well-funded measures are on the street at once, they bid against each other for the same crews and prices spike.

What circulators earn is not the same as what campaigns pay. The petition company's price also covers management, travel, lodging, validation checks, office staff and margin.

Most statewide measures that qualify use paid circulators, often with a volunteer program alongside. Volunteer-only statewide drives do succeed, but usually with a large existing membership organization behind them and a long runway.

State rules on paying circulators

In Meyer v. Grant (1988), the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously struck down Colorado's ban on paying petition circulators, calling petition circulation core political speech. States can still regulate how circulators are paid.

Ten states now ban paying circulators per signature: Arizona, Arkansas, Florida, Montana, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Dakota, Utah and Wyoming. Oklahoma added its ban in 2025 (Ballotpedia News, 2025). In those states, circulators are paid hourly or salaried, and firms manage productivity through quotas and supervision instead of bounties.

Other common circulator rules include residency requirements (six states, per Ballotpedia), registration with the state, mandatory training, badges disclosing paid or volunteer status, and identifying information on each petition. Florida's 2025 law (HB 1205) added circulator registration, training, a 10-day deadline to turn in signed forms and new penalties; parts of it have been challenged in federal court (Ballotpedia News, 2025). Rules changed in Arkansas, Florida, Oklahoma and Montana in 2025 alone, so check current law before you hire anyone.

Validity rates and padding

Election officials reject signatures for many reasons: the signer is not registered, is registered in another county, signed twice, the signature does not match, the address is wrong, or the circulator made an error that voids the whole sheet.

How many extra signatures you need depends on your state's verification method and your crew's quality. In our experience, paid statewide drives commonly target 25 to 40 percent above the requirement, and sometimes more where validity has been poor or challengers are aggressive. That is an experience-based planning range, not a published standard.

Know your state's verification math. California, for example, uses random sampling: if the sample projects more than 110 percent of the required valid signatures, the measure qualifies; below 95 percent it fails; in between, every signature is checked (California Elections Code 9030). A thin cushion can mean months of full count.

How to protect validity:

  • Check signers against the voter file in real time where allowed, or validate samples daily in the office.
  • Track validity by circulator and cut people whose rates fall.
  • Train hard on the technical rules for each sheet (one county per sheet in many states, circulator affidavits, dates).
  • Never pay or offer anything to people for signing. California opened an investigation in 2026 after video showed gatherers offering money for signatures (KTLA/AP).

Florida shows what is at stake. In 2026, no citizen initiative qualified; the marijuana measure finished with 783,592 validated signatures against 880,062 required after a large number were tossed (The Capitolist, 2026).

Start earlier than you think. Many states' deadlines fall many months before the election (Florida's is February 1 for a November vote), and petition firms book up in big years.

Legal challenges

Expect them if anyone with money opposes you. Common lines of attack:

  • Single-subject or separate-vote violations.
  • Misleading title or summary.
  • Circulator rule violations (residency, registration, affidavits, pay).
  • Signature validity challenges, line by line.
  • Fraud allegations, especially against paid crews.

Budget for lawyers from day one, keep clean records of every circulator and every sheet, and hire a firm that can document its own quality control.

The campaign after qualification

Qualifying is not winning. New America's data found the median total spending on a 2024 initiative campaign was about $10 million, and the largest campaigns spend hundreds of millions; California's Prop 27 in 2022 drew $403 million combined (New America).

The post-qualification campaign looks like any other: research and polling, paid media, mail, digital and a field program. One advantage: the volunteers and voter contacts you built during the signature phase are your best early supporters, so keep that data clean and in your own hands. Some states also require supermajorities or have other passage rules, so check the threshold. See political campaign budget and canvassing cost and effectiveness.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to put something on the ballot?

For a statewide initiative, usually millions of dollars. Ballotpedia found paid drives averaged $14.87 per required signature in 2024 and the average successful drive cost $3.25 million. Local measures with small signature requirements and strong volunteers can qualify for far less.

How much do petition circulators get paid per signature?

Rates vary by state and season. Historically $1 to $3 was common, but in competitive 2026 California drives circulators were paid $12 to $15 per signature. Ten states ban per-signature pay, so circulators there are paid hourly.

What is the cost of petition signature gathering per signature?

Ballotpedia's cost per required signature averaged $14.87 in 2024, with individual measures ranging from zero for volunteer drives to $48.21. That figure includes all drive costs divided by the legal minimum, not by total signatures collected.

Is it legal to pay petition signature gatherers?

Yes. The Supreme Court's 1988 Meyer v. Grant decision protects paying circulators. But ten states ban paying them per signature, and many states regulate residency, registration and training. Paying voters to sign is illegal everywhere.

How many extra signatures should a petition drive collect?

Enough to survive your state's verification. In our experience, paid statewide drives commonly target 25 to 40 percent above the requirement, more if validity has been weak or opponents plan line-by-line challenges.

How long does it take to get an initiative on the ballot?

Usually 9 to 18 months from drafting to certification for a statewide measure, longer where deadlines fall early. Signature collection alone typically takes two to six months.

Should we use paid or volunteer signature gatherers?

Volunteers work for local measures and organizations with big memberships. For statewide measures with high thresholds or distribution rules, most campaigns that qualify use paid circulators, ideally alongside volunteers.

What are petition drive management companies?

These firms recruit, train and manage circulators, handle logistics and validation, and are paid per signature, per hour or with a management fee. Ballotpedia keeps a list of petition drive management companies and their past costs.
