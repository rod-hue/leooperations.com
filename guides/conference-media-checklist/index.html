The Conference Media Checklist: What to Plan, Shoot and Deliver

A practical checklist for photo, video, livestream, headshots, social and print, with a shot list by session type, sponsor deliverables, consent signage and delivery timelines.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated November 2026 · 11 min read

A conference media plan comes down to three lists: what you settle before the event (vendors, shot list, sponsor commitments, internet and power, consent signage), what you capture on the day (keynotes, breakouts, the expo, networking, sponsors, VIPs and headshots), and what you deliver afterward and by when (same-day social selects, a full gallery, a highlight reel, session recordings and sponsor packages). The checklist below covers all three for photo, video, livestream, headshots, social and print. Hiring benchmarks start around Thumbtack's October 2026 average of $150 an hour for event photography, a market figure, so the real cost driver is how many rooms and deliverables you commit to.

The biggest mistake we see on conference media is treating it as a booking instead of a plan. A photographer with no shot list will come back with nice pictures. A photographer with your sponsor contract in hand will come back with the pictures that renew those sponsors.

Start with what the media has to do after the event

Before anyone writes a shot list, decide what the photos and video are for. Most conferences use event media in five places, and each one changes what you capture:

  • Next year's marketing. Full rooms, energy, recognizable speakers, the expo at its busiest. This is the registration page and the save-the-date.
  • Sponsor renewal. Every logo, every activation, every sponsored session, documented and delivered back to the sponsor.
  • Member and attendee value. Session recordings, slides and on-demand video for people who missed a room.
  • Press and social. Fast selects of headliners and announcements, in vertical and horizontal formats.
  • The archive. Board photos, award winners, honorees and the people who run the organization.

Write those uses down and attach them to the vendor brief. They decide crew size more than the schedule does.

Pre-event checklist (8 to 12 weeks out through load-in)

Vendors and scope

  • Book photography, video, livestream and design together if you can, so one team plans positions and timing instead of three vendors bumping into each other.
  • Define scope by room and hour: which rooms get a photographer, which sessions are recorded, which are streamed.
  • Get usage rights in writing. Conference media gets reused for years in ads, reports and sponsor decks, so the license should cover commercial and promotional use.
  • Confirm insurance paperwork early. Venues often require a certificate of insurance from every contractor well before the show, and Music City Center, for example, asks for the certificate 30 days prior (MCC Event Planning Guide).

Information to hand your media team

  • Final run of show with room numbers and times.
  • Floor plan with stage, screens, registration, the expo and sponsor activations marked.
  • Speaker list with headshots so the photographer can recognize them.
  • VIP list with photos: board, top sponsors, elected officials, honorees, keynote speakers.
  • Sponsor contracts or a sponsor benefits sheet showing exactly what each level was promised.
  • Brand guide, logo files and hashtag for social.
  • A day-of contact who can find a specific person in five minutes.

Technical needs

  • Internet. For a livestream or session uploads, order hardwired internet at the encoder position. Venue Wi-Fi is not built for broadcast. Music City Center, for one, calls its free Wi-Fi an unmanaged service and tells users needing more to buy wired service (MCC technology).
  • Power. List every station that needs power: streaming table, headshot lights, editing station, charging.
  • Audio. Ask the AV company for a clean board feed into every recorded or streamed room. Camera-mic audio of a keynote is not usable.
  • Positions. Agree on camera risers and center-aisle positions with AV so cameras do not block screens.
  • Credentials. Get crew names onto the venue's call list and badge list.

Consent and release signage

Attendees should know they are being photographed and filmed before they walk into a room, not after. Standard practice is a professionally printed notice at registration and at the entrance to every recorded room, plus a line in the registration terms. A clear, simple notice reads something like: "This event is being photographed, filmed and livestreamed. By attending, you consent to the use of your image by [Organization]. If you prefer not to be photographed, please let our media team know."

Plan the rest of the system too:

  • A visible way to opt out, such as a lanyard sticker or a different badge color, briefed to the photographers.
  • Signed releases for anyone featured individually in marketing video: testimonials, speakers interviewed on camera, attendees quoted in a sizzle reel.
  • Written consent from a parent or guardian before featuring any minors.
  • Speaker agreements that cover recording and on-demand distribution of their session.

Your counsel and your registration terms set the exact wording for your organization. The point is that the signage is designed, printed and placed as part of the sign program, not taped up on show morning. At venues with strict sign rules (Music City Center does not allow handwritten signs or anything taped to walls), the notice belongs on an easel with the rest of the event signage.

Print and design

  • Program or pocket guide, session signs, directional signs, sponsor boards, step-and-repeat, badges and lanyards.
  • Final files to the printer with enough lead time for a proof.
  • Check every sign against venue rules before it prints.

Day-of checklist

Before doors

  • Crew badges picked up, call list confirmed.
  • Internet and power tested at every station. Stream test to a private link.
  • Audio feed tested in every recorded room.
  • Signs checked against the plan, including consent notices.
  • Empty-room and detail shots of the stage, signage, sponsor boards and the expo before the crowd arrives. These are the only clean shots of the set you paid for.

During the event

  • Work from the shot list and run of show, with a media lead tracking what has been captured.
  • Hit every sponsor obligation and check it off.
  • Send social selects to the comms team during the day, not at night.
  • Keep a second shooter or videographer on the expo floor and networking areas while the main crew covers the stage.

After doors close

  • Back up every card to two drives before anyone leaves the venue.
  • Log any missed shots and plan to get them the next morning.

Conference photography shot list by session type

This table is the core of a conference shot list. Adjust it to your program, then add the names.

Session or areaMust-have photosMust-have video
Registration and arrivalLines moving, badges, branded check-in, greeters, first-morning energyB-roll of arrivals and signage
Opening general sessionFull-room wide from the back and from the stage, welcome speaker, audience reaction, branding on screensFull recording, wide and tight angles, audience cutaways
KeynoteSpeaker tight and medium with screen behind, gestures, wide of a full house, applause, any VIP in the front rowMulti-camera recording with board audio, 30 to 60 second social clip
Breakout sessionsSpeaker, room wide, audience taking notes, slide on screen with sponsor creditLocked-off recording with board audio if sessions go on demand
PanelsEach panelist individually, full panel, moderator, audience questionsWide plus a tight camera that can follow the speaker
Expo or trade show floorHigh wide of a busy floor, every sponsor booth with logo legible, demos, conversations, product close-upsWalk-through B-roll, booth interviews, highlight reel footage
Sponsor activationsLogo, people engaging, sponsor representatives with guestsShort sponsor clip
Networking and receptionsSmall groups in genuine conversation, VIPs together, food and bar details, room wideB-roll for the highlight reel
Awards and recognitionEvery honoree on stage, handshake and award, honoree with presenter, group of all winners, family and table reactionsFull award segment, each acceptance
Headshot loungeEach attendee's headshot, plus wide of the lounge with sponsor brandingShort clip of the lounge in action
Board, staff and leadershipGroup photo, individual photos with key partnersShort interviews for the recap

Two notes from the field. First, recognition beats volume: a gallery that shows every sponsor and every honoree is worth more than one with ten thousand frames of the crowd. Second, the wide of a packed room is the single most valuable photo at any conference. Plan when you will take it (usually the opening keynote, just before the speaker starts) and who will be on the riser to take it.

Sponsor deliverables checklist

Sponsors renew when they can show their boss what they bought. Build a sponsor package into the media plan from the start.

  • A folder of photos for each sponsor: booth, logo placements, activation, sponsored session, their team with guests.
  • Their logo visible in context (stage screens, signage, step-and-repeat) with at least one clean shot of each placement.
  • A short sponsor clip or a mention in the highlight reel for higher levels.
  • Session recordings for sponsored sessions.
  • Social posts that tag the sponsor, with the images they can reshare.
  • Delivery within a few days of the event, while the sponsor is writing their own internal recap.

If you sell booths and sponsorships for next year, the highlight reel is also a sales tool. Our guide to trade show booth content covers how to build that footage on the floor.

Headshots, livestream and social: the extras that need their own plan

Headshot lounge

A headshot lounge gives attendees something they take home and use for years, and it is a natural sponsor placement. It needs its own space, power, a backdrop, a line management plan and a way to send each person their photo. Plan the throughput, the hours and the delivery method before you sell it. Our conference headshot booth guide covers the setup in detail, and our headshot photographer page explains on-location headshot service.

Livestream

A livestream is a broadcast, and it deserves a broadcast checklist: a hardwired line, a backup connection, a clean audio feed, graphics (lower thirds, sponsor slates, holding slides), a producer watching the stream, and a plan for captions where you need them. Decide in advance which sessions stream and which are recorded only. Speaker and sponsor agreements should cover both.

Social coverage

  • Agree on how many social selects you want per day and when they arrive.
  • Shoot vertical (9:16) video for Reels, TikTok and Stories, and capture both orientations of key moments.
  • Have speaker handles ready so every post can tag the right people.
  • Plan a same-day recap edit for the end of day one. It drives attendance on day two.

Post-event delivery timelines

Write delivery dates into the contract. These are the standards a professional conference media team should meet.

DeliverableTypical timing
Social selectsSame day, during the event
Same-day recap editEnd of each show day
HeadshotsSame day to a few days, depending on retouching
Sponsor photo foldersWithin one week
Full edited galleryOne to two weeks
Session recordingsOne to three weeks, depending on volume and captions
Highlight reelTwo to three weeks
Sponsor clips and testimonial editsTwo to four weeks

Ask for galleries organized by day, session and sponsor so your team can find the photo a sponsor asks for in thirty seconds, and keep the raw footage archived for next year's promo.

Frequently asked questions

What should be on a conference photography shot list?

Registration and arrivals, the full room at the opening keynote, every keynote and panel speaker, breakout rooms, the expo floor, every sponsor booth and logo placement, networking and receptions, every award and honoree, the headshot lounge and leadership group photos. Attach names and photos of VIPs and sponsors, because the shot list is only as good as the photographer's ability to recognize the people on it.

What should you capture at a conference?

Capture the content (speakers and sessions), the community (attendees learning and talking), the commerce (sponsors and exhibitors) and the recognition (honorees and leadership). Shoot empty-room details before doors open, then get the full-house wide during the opening session. Those two sets of photos sell the next event.

How many photographers do you need for a conference?

Plan one photographer per simultaneous area you need covered: the main stage, the expo and any breakouts on the shot list. A single-room event can work with one photographer. A multi-track conference with an expo usually needs at least two plus video, and a headshot lounge needs its own photographer.

How much does it cost to hire a photographer for a conference?

Thumbtack's October 2026 data puts the average wedding and event photography rate at $150 an hour and commercial photography at $198 an hour. Multi-day conferences with several rooms, video and fast delivery price higher. See our event photographer cost guide for more market ranges.

Do you need photo release forms at a conference?

Use notice signage at registration and every recorded room, a consent line in registration terms and a clear opt-out method for general coverage. Get signed releases for anyone featured individually in marketing video and parental consent for minors. Your counsel should approve the wording.

How long does it take to get conference photos back?

A professional team delivers social selects the same day and a full edited gallery in one to two weeks. Sponsor folders should arrive within a week, while sponsors are still building their own recaps. Put every date in the contract.

What is the difference between recording and livestreaming a session?

A recording is edited and published later, so problems can be fixed in post. A livestream goes out in real time, so it needs a hardwired internet line, a backup connection, a clean audio feed and a producer watching it. Many conferences stream the keynotes and record the breakouts.

Should one company handle conference photo and video?

For most conferences, yes. One team can plan camera positions so the photographer and videographer do not block each other, work from the same shot list and deliver a consistent look across photos, video and design. It also gives your staff one contact on show day.
