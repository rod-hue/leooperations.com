Why a professional website matters for candidates, campaigns and businesses

The research on first impressions and credibility, what a campaign site and a business site each need, the legal basics, market cost ranges and a launch checklist.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

A professional website matters because people decide whether to trust you almost instantly, largely on how the site looks and works. Researchers found visitors form a reliable opinion of a web page's visual appeal in about 50 milliseconds, and in a Stanford study of 2,684 people, the site's look and design was the factor mentioned most often (46.1% of comments) when they judged credibility. For a candidate, it is where donations, sign-ups and press checks happen. For a business, it is where a stranger from Google decides whether to call. A small professionally built site commonly costs $1,000 to $10,000 in the market, and a cheap, slow or broken one costs more in lost trust.

This guide is part of our series on looking clean, professional and polished in public. The pillar piece is why professional photography and video matter.

Why is a professional website important?

Your website is the one place online you fully control. Social platforms change their algorithms, news stories describe you in someone else's words, and review sites show whatever customers post. Your site is the record you write.

It is also where people go to check you out after hearing about you somewhere else. A voter gets a door hanger, a customer sees a yard sign or an ad, and the next move is a search for your name. If what they find looks abandoned or amateur, the door hanger and the ad just lost most of their value. Every paid ad, email, text and Google listing points somewhere, and that somewhere has to hold up.

What the research says about first impressions and credibility

People judge in a fraction of a second

In a 2006 study published in *Behaviour and Information Technology*, Gitte Lindgaard and colleagues showed people web pages for just 50 milliseconds and asked them to rate visual appeal. Those snap ratings closely matched ratings people gave after looking longer, as Nature reported at the time. The authors concluded designers have about 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression, and that impression tends to color how people rate usability and trust.

Design is what people cite when they decide to trust you

The Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab, led by B.J. Fogg, asked 2,684 people to evaluate the credibility of real websites in 2002. The "design look" of the site came up more than anything else, in 46.1% of comments, according to the Consumer Reports WebWatch summary of the study. Information structure was next at 28.5%. People say they care about expertise; what they react to is presentation.

That is not a reason to put style over substance. It is a reason not to hide your substance behind a site that looks like 2011. Stanford's companion Web Credibility guidelines give practical advice that still holds: show there is a real organization behind the site, make it easy to contact you, keep content updated, and avoid errors of every kind, including broken links and typos.

Speed is part of the impression

Google's research found that as mobile page load time goes from one second to three seconds, the probability of a visitor bouncing increases 32%, per Think with Google (2017 data). Google also publishes Core Web Vitals thresholds that count as "good": the main content loads within 2.5 seconds, the page responds to a tap within 200 milliseconds, and the layout does not jump around (a layout shift score of 0.1 or less).

The usual speed killers are huge uncompressed photos, piles of plugins, autoplay video and cheap hosting.

Mobile, accessibility and security: the non-negotiables

Mobile

In the United States, roughly 40% to 43% of web traffic came from phones in mid 2026 snapshots from StatCounter, with desktop still carrying the rest. Campaign and local business sites usually skew more mobile, because traffic arrives from social posts, texts and QR codes. Design for the phone first.

Accessibility

The WebAIM Million 2025 report found detectable WCAG accessibility failures on 94.8% of the top one million home pages, with low-contrast text and missing image alt text among the most common problems. The Department of Justice has said in its 2022 web accessibility guidance that the ADA applies to the websites of businesses open to the public, and it points to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) as a helpful standard. Build to WCAG 2.1 AA. It is the standard DOJ points to, the one plaintiffs' lawyers test against, and the practical design target that makes your site usable for the widest range of visitors. Meeting it is strong evidence of good faith, but it is a design standard, not a guarantee that every visitor's needs or every legal question is settled, so have your lawyer assess your specific exposure.

For elected officials, note that official government sites are a separate matter. DOJ's 2024 Title II rule requires state and local government websites to meet WCAG 2.1 AA, and an April 2026 interim rule moved the compliance dates to April 26, 2027 for public entities serving 50,000 or more people and April 26, 2028 for smaller entities and special district governments. Those deadlines apply only to Title II governments. They do not set a deadline for campaign committees or private businesses, whose sites fall under the general ADA guidance above.

The basics are not hard: color contrast, alt text, real text instead of text baked into images, captions on video, labeled forms and a site that works with a keyboard alone.

Security

Chrome began labeling every plain HTTP page "Not secure" in July 2018, as Search Engine Land reported. Any site that collects a name, email or donation needs HTTPS, which most good hosts include free. Turn on two-factor authentication for the site, the domain registrar and the payment platform. Campaign sites get targeted because they are visible.

Do candidates need a website?

Yes, even for city council or school board. Reporters and endorsing organizations will look for it, voters who search your name should find you before your opponent's framing of you, and every ad and mailer needs a destination you control.

What should a political campaign website include?

  • A home page with one clear message and one main action. A strong photo, your name, the office and district, and a donate or volunteer button above the fold.
  • Donate flow. Link directly to your processor (ActBlue, WinRed, Anedot or similar) with suggested amounts, and test it on a phone.
  • Volunteer form. Name, email, phone, ZIP and a few check boxes. Move sign-ups into your CRM the same day; see recruiting and managing volunteers.
  • Bio page. Real story, real photos, specific accomplishments. Not a resume.
  • Issues page. Three to six priorities in plain language. Voters skim. Reporters read closely.
  • Photos and video. Current, professional, with actual constituents. Stock photos of generic smiling families undercut everything else.
  • Endorsements. With permission. See our guide on testimonials and reviews.
  • Events calendar. Kept current or removed. An event from four months ago says the campaign is asleep.
  • Press page. Short and long bio, high-resolution headshots, releases and a press contact.
  • Language access. In 2018 to 2022 Census data, Spanish accounted for 61.1% of people who speak a language other than English at home. If your district has significant Spanish speakers or other language communities, translate the core pages properly (with a human reviewer, not just a machine) and link the translation from the header.
  • Disclaimers and compliance text.

Paid-for-by disclaimers and solicitation notices

The FEC requires disclaimers on political committee websites available to the general public, and on email blasts of more than 500 substantially similar messages, per its advertising and disclaimers guidance. The disclaimer must be clear and conspicuous, meaning easy to read and not placed where people will miss it. For an authorized campaign the wording is along the lines of "Paid for by Sam Jones for Congress," the example the FEC uses on its fundraising notices page.

That same FEC page says solicitations must explain that the campaign uses best efforts to collect contributor information, and that committees whose gross annual receipts normally exceed $100,000 must include an IRS notice that contributions are not tax deductible. Your donation platform handles part of this, but your site's footer and donate page still need the right language. State and local races follow state law, so check with your state elections office.

What a business website needs

The logic is the same: prove you are real, make the next step easy.

  • Services pages. One page per main service, in the words customers search. "Kitchen remodeling in Franklin" beats "Solutions."
  • Proof. Reviews, testimonials, project photos and recognizable clients, with permission.
  • Contact that works. A tappable phone number, a short form, hours and service area.
  • About page with real people. Professional photos of the owner and team. Customers hire people, and professional photography of your actual team does more for trust than any stock image.
  • One primary action per page. Book, call or get a quote.

How the website ties into Google Business Profile

For local businesses, your Google Business Profile often gets seen before your website. Google says its local results are based on relevance, distance and prominence, and that review count, review score and information from across the web (links, articles, directories) feed prominence. Your website is one of those signals. Keep your business name, address and phone number identical on the site, the profile and every directory. Link the profile to the right page on the site, and put your service area and city names in your page titles and headings where they naturally belong.

Local SEO basics that move things: a unique title and description on every page, honest location pages with real content if you serve several cities, fast mobile loading, real photos compressed for the web, and Google reviews requested from every customer. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 97% of consumers read online reviews for local businesses.

A campaign site that cannot be updated on a Saturday morning is a liability, which is why our website design work includes a plan for who makes changes and how fast.

Builder platform or custom build?

A builder platform can carry a professional site when it is professionally designed, filled with professional photos and kept current. Template sites assembled in a weekend show it, and they break down when you need speed under deadline, multiple languages, CRM or voter file integrations, or a look distinct from everyone using the same template. The most common mistake we see is not the platform. It is a site built quickly at launch and never touched again. Whatever you choose, own your domain and admin login. Campaigns lose access to sites all the time when a volunteer or vendor leaves.

Frequently asked questions

Why is a professional website important?

It is usually the first thing people check after hearing your name, and they judge it in about 50 milliseconds, with design the factor they cite most for credibility. A professional site turns that attention into donations, volunteers, calls and sales.

Do candidates need a website?

Yes, even in small local races. Voters, reporters and endorsing groups will search your name, and the site is where every ad, mailer and text sends people to give or volunteer.

What should a political campaign website include?

A clear home page with a strong photo and one main action, a fast donate flow, a short volunteer form, a bio, an issues page, endorsements, an events calendar kept current, a press page with headshots, translations where the district needs them, and a clear and conspicuous paid-for-by disclaimer. Federal committees also need the FEC and IRS solicitation notices on donation pages.

How much does a professional website cost?

Market ranges run from a few hundred dollars a year for a DIY builder, to roughly $1,000 to $10,000 for a professionally built small site, to $3,000 to $30,000 or more for a custom agency build. Hosting and maintenance are extra, so get two or three written quotes.

Is a Facebook page enough instead of a website?

No. You do not control the algorithm, the layout or whether the account stays up. A dedicated website gives you more control over your contribution path, press resources, accessibility and compliance presentation, and it stays reliable when a platform changes its rules. Use social to send people to the site.

Does my website need to be ADA compliant?

The Department of Justice says the ADA applies to websites of businesses open to the public and points to WCAG as a helpful standard, though some questions are still being litigated. Building to WCAG 2.1 AA is the safest practical approach. State and local government sites have a separate WCAG 2.1 AA requirement under Title II, due April 26, 2027 for entities of 50,000 or more people and April 26, 2028 for smaller entities and special districts; those dates do not apply to campaign or business sites.

How long does it take to build a campaign website?

A simple site can launch in one to three weeks if photos, bio and issue copy are ready. Waiting on content slows most projects. Launch home, bio, donate and volunteer pages first, then add the rest.
