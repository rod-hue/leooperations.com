Political Direct Mail and Text Messaging: What It Costs, What the Law Says, and What Works

A practical guide to campaign mail (USPS political mail, postage, per-piece costs, how many drops) and political texting (P2P vs broadcast, TCPA consent, 10DLC registration, opt-outs), plus a short look at phones and IVR.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

Political direct mail usually costs about $0.50 to $0.70 per piece all-in (design, printing, postage and list) for a standard oversized postcard in volume, and more for small runs, letters or large-format pieces. Peer-to-peer (P2P) political texting is far cheaper per contact: published platform rates run roughly 2 to 4 cents per SMS segment and 5 to 8 cents per picture message, plus registration and staffing. Mail reaches nearly every targeted household and has modest, well-studied turnout effects; texts are fast and cheap but tightly regulated by the TCPA, carrier 10DLC rules and opt-out law. Most serious campaigns use both, timed to when voters actually cast ballots.

How much does political direct mail cost?

Per-piece cost has four parts: design, printing, postage and data (list, targeting, address hygiene). The all-in range below assumes a full-color oversized postcard, the workhorse of campaign mail.

Volume per dropTypical all-in cost per piece (market range)
Under 5,000$0.70 to $1.25+
5,000 to 25,000$0.55 to $0.85
25,000 to 100,000$0.45 to $0.70
100,000+$0.40 to $0.60

These are market ranges, not Leo Operations prices, and published figures line up with them: one political guide puts typical political mail at $0.50 to $0.70 per piece (GoodParty.org), and Seattle's Democratic party guidance in 2023 allowed consultants to charge $0.40 to $0.53 per piece on batches of 15,000 or more (The Stranger, 2023).

That same Stranger investigation found one consultant charging $0.53 for a piece that cost $0.19 to produce. Markups are normal; hidden markups are not. Ask your mail vendor to break out print, postage and fee on every drop. See our guide on how to hire a political consultant.

Letters in envelopes, large tri-folds and pieces with personalization cost more. Short print runs are expensive because setup cost is spread over fewer pieces, so printing one 10,000-piece run is cheaper per piece than two 5,000-piece runs.

Postage: what campaigns actually pay

Postage is often the biggest single line. USPS raised mailing prices by an average of about 4.8 percent on July 12, 2026, and the Forever stamp went to $0.82 (USPS final rule, 2026). Current commercial prices are in USPS Notice 123. The USPS political mail page lays out three main options (USPS Political Mail):

  • USPS Marketing Mail. The standard for campaign postcards and flyers. Cheapest per piece when presorted to targeted addresses. Not time-guaranteed.
  • First-Class Mail. Faster and forwardable; used for personalized letters, fundraising and late pieces.
  • Every Door Direct Mail (EDDM). Mails every address on a carrier route. No list needed, but no targeting either, so a lot of postage lands on people who cannot or will not vote for you.

Can a campaign get nonprofit postage rates?

Usually not. Nonprofit Marketing Mail prices are available to national and state committees of political parties (and the four congressional campaign committees), but a USPS ruling makes clear that county and local committees are ineligible, and an authorized party committee cannot lend its permit or mail a candidate's material at those prices (USPS Customer Support Ruling PS-055). A state party can mail its own pieces supporting candidates at nonprofit rates if it pays for them itself. Candidate committees and most PACs cannot claim nonprofit rates simply because the mail is political, so they pay commercial rates. Other organizations can qualify under separate USPS nonprofit categories in DMM 703, but cooperative mailings for ineligible senders, including candidates, are not allowed at those rates.

Delivery timing and USPS political mail handling

USPS lists Marketing Mail delivery at 3 to 10 days, compared with 1 to 5 days for First-Class Mail (USPS Publication 632), so build that window into every drop date. USPS asks political mailers to complete its online reporting form at least two days before dropping a mailing at a Business Mail Entry Unit. Inside USPS, political mail can be marked with a red Tag 57 so facilities log and expedite it; the green Tag 191 is reserved for ballots (USPS Link).

Plan in-home dates backward from when people vote, not from Election Day. In states with early and mail voting, your last persuasion piece should land before most ballots are returned.

How many mail pieces and drops does a campaign need?

Repetition is what makes mail work, because most pieces get a few seconds of attention at the mailbox. The planning ranges we use:

  • Local races: 3 to 5 pieces to likely voters, sometimes fewer to strong supporters.
  • Competitive legislative and congressional races: 5 to 10 pieces to persuadable targets, spread over the last 4 to 6 weeks.
  • GOTV mail: 1 to 3 pieces timed to ballot arrival, early voting start and the final weekend.

Budget example: 20,000 targeted households, 5 drops, at $0.55 per piece is 100,000 pieces and about $55,000.

Does political direct mail work?

The field experiments are clear about how mail earns its place. Gerber and Green's landmark New Haven study found door-to-door canvassing raised turnout substantially, direct mail slightly, and phone calls not at all (American Political Science Review, 2000). Later experiments found partisan mail did little for turnout, and negative mail could even dampen it (Yale ISPS).

The big exception is social pressure. A Michigan experiment found mailers telling voters their neighbors would see whether they voted increased turnout about as much as a door knock (Gerber, Green and Larimer, 2008). Those tactics can annoy voters, so many campaigns use softer versions (thanking past voters, showing a voter their own history).

On persuasion, Kalla and Broockman's review of 49 field experiments found the average persuasive effect of campaign contact in general elections was close to zero, with exceptions when campaigns target real persuadables on unusually lopsided issues or contact voters early (APSR, 2018). Primaries and down-ballot races, where voters know less, are where mail persuasion has the most room to work.

Our practical take: mail is reliable reach and repetition, especially to older voters, and it works hardest as reinforcement for a door-to-door program. See canvassing cost and effectiveness.

Political texting: P2P vs broadcast

Peer-to-peer (P2P)Broadcast (A2P mass text)
How it is sentA person sends each message, one click at a timeSoftware sends to a whole list at once
Federal TCPA autodialer consent rule onlyGenerally no consent required if the technology is not an autodialerPrior express consent required if sent with an autodialer
State law, carrier and provider rulesCan be stricter; check separatelyCan be stricter; check separately
Two-way repliesYes, volunteers or staff answerLimited or automated
Typical useVoter contact, persuasion, GOTV, IDSupporter lists, fundraising, event reminders
Number type10DLC local numbersShort code or toll-free, or registered 10DLC

The consent row describes the federal TCPA autodialer distinction and nothing more. State texting and telemarketing laws, carrier standards and your provider's acceptable-use and opt-in rules can be stricter, and registering on 10DLC is not consent from anyone. A product being marketed as "P2P" also does not settle the autodialer question; what matters is how the specific technology actually sends messages. Confirm the platform's sending process and have counsel review the program for each state you text into.

Political texting rules: TCPA, consent and opt-outs

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act treats texts as calls. The FCC says autodialed texts to cell phones require the recipient's prior express consent, but political texts can be sent without prior consent if they are not sent with autodialing technology (FCC political campaign rules). In a 2020 declaratory ruling, the FCC said a platform that requires a person to manually send each message one at a time is not an autodialer (Holtzman Vogel). The Supreme Court then narrowed the autodialer definition in Facebook v. Duguid (2021) to equipment that uses a random or sequential number generator (Gordon Rees).

That is why P2P exists. It is also why TCPA lawyers still file suits: statutory damages are $500 to $1,500 per unlawful text, and several states have their own stricter laws. Get a compliance review for your state.

Other rules every campaign texting program should follow:

  • Opt-outs. FCC rules effective April 11, 2025 say consumers can revoke consent by any reasonable means, replies like STOP, QUIT, END, REVOKE, OPT OUT, CANCEL and UNSUBSCRIBE count, and requests must be honored within 10 business days (Manatt). Best practice is to honor STOP immediately and across every platform you use.
  • Identify yourself in the first message (candidate or committee name).
  • Disclaimers. Federal disclaimer rules turn on the type of communication. The FEC's rules cover public communications (including internet ads placed for a fee), mass mailings and phone banks of more than 500 substantially similar pieces or calls, emails of more than 500 by political committees, and committee websites (FEC advertising and disclaimers). In Advisory Opinion 2002-09, the FEC applied the small-items exception to SMS text ads because of their length limits. So not every political text carries the same federal disclaimer requirement, but many states have their own text disclaimer rules, and identifying the sender is a carrier expectation regardless. Have counsel review the exact texting program, and include a clear paid-for-by identification as a standard practice.
  • Do Not Call. The FCC says political campaign calls are exempt from the National Do Not Call Registry, but the TCPA still applies.
  • CTIA guidelines. Carriers enforce CTIA's Messaging Principles and Best Practices, which treat political texts as application-to-person traffic, expect clear STOP handling, and prohibit using purchased or shared opt-in lists for consent-based programs (Twilio summary of CTIA guidance).

10DLC registration and Campaign Verify

Almost all political P2P texting runs on 10-digit long codes (10DLC), and carriers require every sender to register a brand and campaign through The Campaign Registry. Unregistered 10DLC traffic has been blocked by major U.S. carriers since early February 2025 (Prompt.io).

Political committees use the political use case, which requires a token from Campaign Verify. Providers list the Campaign Verify fee at $95 (Vonage), and Scale to Win notes it can take up to two weeks to get a token depending on your public filings (Scale to Win). Expect smaller brand and campaign registration fees from your platform as well. Start registration at least a month before you need to text. Many first-time campaigns lose a week of GOTV waiting on approval.

How much do political text messages cost?

Platforms charge per segment (160 characters of plain text, fewer with emoji) and per MMS (picture message). Published or example rates:

PlatformSMS per segmentMMS
RumbleUp (entry tier)$0.03$0.08
ThruText (example rates in help docs)$0.035$0.06
Scale to Win (2024 published)$0.02$0.05

Sources: RumbleUp, ThruText help center, GoodParty.org review, 2024. Confirm current pricing directly; volume tiers lower the rate.

Add the people. P2P requires humans to click send and answer replies. Volunteers cost nothing but need training and supervision; paid texters or vendor-managed programs add labor costs that often equal or exceed platform fees.

Budget example: 50,000 voters, 3 texts each, mostly two-segment SMS with some MMS, comes to roughly $10,000 to $15,000 in platform fees before labor and data. Bad numbers, wrong-number replies and opt-outs grow with every send, so clean the list after each round.

Phones and IVR, briefly

Live calls and automated (IVR or robocall) programs still have a place, especially for older voters, tele-town halls and quick polling. The FCC rules:

  • Prerecorded or autodialed political calls to cell phones need prior express consent.
  • Prerecorded political calls to residential landlines are allowed without consent, limited to three within any 30-day period.
  • Every prerecorded message must clearly identify who is calling at the start.
  • AI-generated voices count as "artificial" voices under the TCPA.

Source: FCC political campaign robocalls and robotexts guide. On programs we run, live calls with real conversation outperform recorded messages, and both work best as support for a knock on the door.

Frequently asked questions

How much does political direct mail cost?

Usually about $0.50 to $0.70 per piece all-in for oversized postcards at campaign volumes, more for small runs or letters. Postage is the biggest piece, and USPS prices rose about 4.8 percent in July 2026. Ask your vendor to break out print, postage and fees.

How much do political text messages cost?

Platform fees typically run about 2 to 4 cents per SMS segment and 5 to 8 cents per MMS, plus 10DLC registration (including a $95 Campaign Verify fee for political committees) and the cost of people to send and reply.

Is it legal for political campaigns to text you without permission?

Under the federal TCPA, the FCC says political texts to cell phones can be sent without prior consent if they are not sent with an autodialer; autodialed texts require prior express consent. That is only the federal rule. State laws, carrier standards and texting-provider rules can be stricter, and 10DLC registration is not consent. Campaigns must honor opt-out requests.

How do I stop political text messages?

Reply STOP. Under FCC rules effective in 2025, senders must honor that request within 10 business days, and reputable campaigns stop immediately.

What is the best political text messaging service?

The best service is the one that fits your list size, staffing and timeline. Most campaigns use P2P platforms built for politics (several publish per-segment pricing), and choose based on price, list tools, reply management and how fast they handle 10DLC and Campaign Verify. Ask about deliverability rates in your state.

Can campaigns use nonprofit postage rates?

Among political committees, the qualifying ones are national and state committees of political parties (including the congressional campaign committees), and they can mail only their own material at those rates. Candidate committees, county and local party committees, and most PACs cannot claim a nonprofit rate just because the mail is political, so they pay commercial prices. Separately, organizations that qualify under other USPS nonprofit categories (such as certain labor, educational or religious organizations, listed in DMM 703) can be authorized for nonprofit rates, but they cannot lend that authorization to a candidate or mail for an ineligible committee. Check eligibility with USPS before you budget on it.

How many mailers should a campaign send?

Most local races send 3 to 5 pieces to likely voters. Competitive legislative and congressional races often send 5 to 10 to persuadable targets over the final month or more, plus GOTV pieces timed to early voting.

Does political direct mail work?

Yes, when it is used for what it does well. Mail delivers reliable reach and repetition, builds name recognition, and has the most persuasive room in lower-information races like primaries and local contests. Social-pressure mail has raised turnout about as much as a door knock, and standard mail works best as reinforcement for a canvassing program.
