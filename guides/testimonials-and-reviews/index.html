Testimonials and reviews: how to capture credible ones and use them everywhere

Why supporter, customer and validator voices persuade, how to film testimonials that look and sound real, the consent and FTC rules, and how to ask for and answer reviews.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

Testimonials and reviews work because people trust other people more than they trust anything an organization says about itself. Nielsen's global survey found 83% of people trust recommendations from friends and family, and BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 97% of consumers read online reviews when choosing a local business. For campaigns, a nurse, a small business owner or a neighbor saying why they support you does work your own ad cannot. To be persuasive, testimonials must be real, recorded well (good light, a lavalier mic, a quiet room, the person's own words), backed by written consent, and, for businesses, compliant with the FTC's 2024 rule and each platform's own policy. Those are separate tests. The FTC bans fake reviews and any incentive conditioned on a positive or negative review, and requires material connections to be disclosed; a genuine, clearly disclosed paid testimonial can be lawful, but disclosure never cures a payment that required five stars. Google is stricter still and bars incentivized reviews on its platform entirely.

This guide is part of our series on looking clean and professional in public. The pillar guide is why professional photography and video matter.

Why testimonials and reviews persuade

People trust people like them

Nielsen's 2015 survey of 30,000 people in 60 countries found recommendations from people you know were the most trusted form of advertising, and consumer opinions posted online also ranked near the top, ahead of most paid ads.

For local businesses the evidence is direct. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey reported that 41% of consumers "always" read reviews when looking at businesses, up from 29% the year before, and that more shoppers now limit themselves to businesses rated 4.5 stars or higher. Google itself says review count and review score factor into local search ranking. Reviews affect both whether people find you and whether they choose you.

Validators in politics

Voters do not have time to study every race. Political scientists have shown they lean on trusted shortcuts instead. In a well-known study of California insurance ballot measures, Arthur Lupia found that voters with access to widely available information shortcuts, such as knowing which groups stood where, voted much like well-informed voters (American Political Science Review, 1994). Endorsements and testimonials from credible people and groups are exactly that kind of shortcut.

That is why a retired teacher talking about a school board candidate can be the most effective content a campaign produces. Choose messengers the persuadable voter sees as someone like them, or someone they respect.

Specific beats glowing

The most credible testimonials are specific. "She's great" persuades nobody. "She called me back the same day when the city towed my car by mistake" does. Real details, real hesitations ("I wasn't sure at first...") and real places make a testimonial believable. Scripted lines make it sound like an ad, and audiences can tell.

How to film a video testimonial that looks and sounds credible

Credible testimonials come from a disciplined setup, and on the shoots we run, the same five things decide whether a clip is usable. Bad audio ruins more testimonials than anything else.

Light

Use soft light from the front or slightly to the side. A large window works well if the person faces it. Avoid overhead fluorescent light, which creates harsh shadows under the eyes, and never put a bright window behind the person. If you are bringing lights, one large soft key light plus a little fill is plenty.

Sound

Clip a lavalier microphone to the person's shirt, about a hand's width below the chin, and hide the cable. Record a backup if you can. Do a short test and listen back on headphones. Phone mics and on-camera mics pick up the room, not the voice.

Room

Pick the quietest room available. Turn off HVAC and fans, silence phones and close doors. Soft furniture and rugs reduce echo. Outdoors, plan for wind and traffic.

Framing

Frame from mid chest up, with the eyes about a third of the way down from the top of the frame. Have the person look at the interviewer, who sits just beside the camera, not into the lens (unless it is a direct-to-camera piece). Choose a background that says something true: their shop, classroom, porch or neighborhood. Shoot a vertical version or frame wide enough to crop to 9:16 for Reels, Stories and TikTok.

Real words, not scripts

Do not hand people a script. Ask open questions and let them talk:

  • How did you first meet the candidate or business?
  • What was going on in your life or work when you needed help?
  • What specifically happened?
  • What changed afterward?
  • What would you tell a neighbor who is on the fence?

Ask people to answer in full sentences that include the question ("The first time I met Maria was...") because the interviewer's voice will be cut out. Ask the same question twice in different words if the first answer is stiff. The second answer is almost always better.

B-roll

Film 10 to 20 minutes of the person in their real environment: working, walking, talking with the candidate or owner, details of their hands and their space. B-roll lets you cut between answers smoothly and shows the viewer the life behind the words. For campaigns, this is standard practice in political video production, and our campaign video production guide covers how to plan a shoot day that captures many of these at once.

Light editing and captions

Edit for length and clarity, not meaning. Never splice words together to create a sentence the person did not say. Keep natural pauses where they add weight. Burn in captions: a 2019 Verizon Media and Publicis Media survey found 69% of viewers watch video with the sound off in public. Spell the person's name and title correctly in the lower third, and show it to them before it airs.

Written quotes and photos

Written quotes with a professional photo work well on websites, quote cards, mailers and proposals.

  • Quote people in their own words. Light cleanup for grammar is normal, but send the final text back for approval.
  • Use full name, plus a town, title or relationship when they agree ("Dana Ellis, small business owner, Hermitage").
  • Photograph them well. A professional portrait makes a quote far more believable than a cropped selfie.
  • Keep a log: who said it, when, where it was collected, and what they approved.

Consent, release forms and the rules

Get a written release every time

A signed release is our standard for every testimonial we record. It is sound practice rather than one legal formula that applies identically to every quote and photo: what the law requires depends on the state, the medium and the use (commercial advertising generally needs consent to use a person's name or likeness, while other uses may not). The release removes the guesswork. It should say who the person is, what you recorded, where you may use it (website, social media, paid ads, print, TV), for how long, whether they were paid or given anything, and whether they can withdraw future use. For anyone under 18, a parent or guardian signs. Digital signature tools work fine. Laws on use of a person's name and likeness vary by state, so have a lawyer review your standard form once and then use it consistently.

Special cases for campaigns: public employees, uniformed personnel and some licensed professionals have limits on political activity. Department of Defense rules, for example, restrict political activity by service members, including appearing in uniform in connection with campaigns. Ask before you film anyone in a work uniform.

FTC rules for businesses

For commercial businesses, the FTC's rules are clear and now carry real penalties.

The FTC's final rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, announced in August 2024 and effective October 21, 2024, prohibits:

  • Creating, buying or selling fake reviews or testimonials, including AI-generated ones or reviews from people with no real experience of the business.
  • Offering compensation or incentives conditioned on a review being positive or negative.
  • Posting reviews by officers, managers or employees without clearly disclosing the connection.
  • Running a company-controlled "independent" review site.
  • Suppressing negative reviews with groundless legal threats or intimidation.
  • Buying fake followers or views to look more influential.

The FTC can seek civil penalties against knowing violators.

The FTC's Endorsement Guides guidance adds that incentives for reviews must be disclosed and cannot depend on the review being positive, that you should not ask only the customers you expect to be happy, and that if a testimonial shows unusual results, the ad must make clear what people can generally expect.

Campaigns fall mostly under election law rather than these commercial rules, but follow the standard anyway. A fake supporter testimonial is a scandal waiting for a reporter.

How to ask for Google reviews properly

Google's own rules, in its prohibited and restricted content policy, bar reviews that were paid for directly or in kind, incentives such as discounts or free services in exchange for reviews, and businesses that "selectively solicit positive reviews from customers" or discourage negative ones. Reviews from current or former employees count as a conflict of interest.

A clean process:

  1. Ask everyone, not just happy customers. That is both the Google rule and the FTC guidance. Asking selectively is called review gating, and it can get reviews removed.
  2. Ask at the right moment. Right after a job is delivered, an event ends or a problem is solved.
  3. Make it one tap. Use the review link from your Google Business Profile in a text or email. QR codes at the counter work for walk-in businesses.
  4. Keep the ask simple. "We'd appreciate an honest review of your experience." No suggested wording, no offers.
  5. Never write, buy or trade reviews. Not from friends, not from family, not from a vendor that promises results.

How to respond to negative reviews

A page of only five-star reviews can look curated, and a calm reply to a complaint shows future customers how you handle problems. BrightLocal's 2026 survey noted that slow or generic replies are increasingly read as a red flag.

  • Reply within a day or two. Speed shows you are paying attention.
  • Thank them, acknowledge the issue, and take it offline. "Thank you for telling us. That's not the experience we want anyone to have. Please call me directly so I can make it right."
  • Do not argue or share private details. Future customers are reading, not just the reviewer.
  • Fix the real problem if there is one, and it is fine to ask afterward whether they would update their review. The FTC warns against pressuring people to delete negative reviews.
  • Report reviews that violate the platform's policy (fake, spam, off-topic, conflict of interest) through the official process, rather than threatening anyone.

For campaigns, the parallel is negative comments. Moderate spam and abuse according to a posted policy, answer real questions with facts, and do not delete fair criticism.

Frequently asked questions

Why are testimonials and reviews important?

People trust other people more than they trust an organization talking about itself. Surveys show most consumers read reviews before choosing a local business, and voters use endorsements and trusted messengers as shortcuts when deciding. A real, specific testimonial does persuasion work your own messaging cannot.

How do you get good video testimonials?

Get a signed release, film in a quiet room with soft light and a lavalier mic, and ask open questions instead of using a script. Capture b-roll, edit lightly without changing meaning, add captions and let the person review the cut.

Is it legal to pay for reviews or testimonials?

Buying fake reviews is illegal for businesses under the FTC's 2024 rule, and so is offering incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review. Paying someone for a genuine testimonial or giving a small thank-you can be legal if the connection is clearly disclosed and the reward does not depend on what they say. Disclosure does not fix an incentive tied to a positive review. Google separately prohibits incentivized reviews on its platform, disclosed or not.

Can I ask customers for Google reviews?

Yes. Asking is allowed and encouraged. What Google prohibits is offering incentives, writing reviews yourself, having employees review you, and asking only customers you expect to be happy. Ask every customer with a simple, honest request and a direct link.

Do I need a release form for testimonials?

Make it your standard every time. The exact legal requirement depends on your state and how you use the testimonial, and a written release settles the question in advance. It spells out where and how long you can use the person's name, words, voice and image, and whether they received anything in return. Minors need a parent or guardian's signature, and state laws on likeness vary.

How should a business respond to a bad review?

Reply quickly and calmly, thank the person, acknowledge the issue, and offer to resolve it directly offline. Do not argue or share private details. Fix the underlying problem, and report reviews that break platform rules through the official process.

Can campaigns use supporter testimonials in ads?

Yes, with written consent and accurate wording, and the ad needs the required paid-for-by disclaimer. Check special cases before filming, such as public employees, people in uniform, and anyone whose job limits political activity.

What makes a testimonial credible?

Specific details, the person's own words, a real setting, clean audio and a real name on screen. Scripts and vague praise sound like ads.
