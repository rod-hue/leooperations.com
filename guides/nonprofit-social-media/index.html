Nonprofit social media strategy: the right platforms, posts that work, Facebook fundraisers and how to measure results

A working plan for executive directors, development staff and communications teams, with current platform data, post ideas, the rules for Meta fundraisers, a policy checklist and a measurement plan.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

A nonprofit social media strategy works when it is built on the mission: real stories of the people and places you serve, posted on a steady schedule to the two or three platforms your supporters already use, with a clear next step in each post (give, sign up, volunteer, share). For most nonprofits that means Facebook and Instagram first, since nearly every organization in the M+R Benchmarks 2026 study kept a presence on both in 2025, with YouTube (74%) and TikTok (43%) added where video is a strength. Facebook fundraisers can add to that. They run through PayPal Giving Fund, carry processing fees, and are best treated as one channel alongside your own donation page.

Why social media matters for nonprofits

Social media is often where supporters first meet your work and where they see it again between appeals. It is how a volunteer shows friends what Saturday's food drive looked like and how a donor sees what last year's gift built.

What is the best social media platform for nonprofit organizations?

The best platform is the one your supporters use daily. Two data sets help.

First, the general public. Pew Research Center's 2026 survey, published in October 2026, found that 84% of U.S. adults use YouTube and 70% use Facebook, with about half using Instagram (Pew Research Center). Age makes a big difference: 79% of adults 18 to 29 use Instagram and 65% use TikTok, while among adults 65 and older, 59% use Facebook and 11% use TikTok. If your donors skew older, Facebook carries more weight. If you recruit young volunteers, Instagram and TikTok matter more.

Second, what nonprofits actually use. The M+R Benchmarks 2026 study, based on 2025 data from participating nonprofits, found:

PlatformShare of participating nonprofits with a presence (2025)
FacebookNearly all
InstagramNearly all
YouTube74%
X (Twitter)54%, down from prior years
TikTok43%
Bluesky37%
Threads32%

Source: M+R Benchmarks 2026.

The same study reported average Facebook followers up 3% in 2025, while X was the only platform where nonprofit followers declined (down 3%), and 31% of nonprofits on X planned to leave or wind down their accounts.

A practical plan for most nonprofits:

  • Facebook for donors, older supporters, events and local community groups.
  • Instagram for visual storytelling, volunteers and younger supporters.
  • YouTube if you have video worth keeping: program explainers, gala films, testimonials.
  • TikTok if you can produce short, authentic vertical video every week.
  • LinkedIn for corporate partners, board recruitment and staff hiring.

Do two or three well before adding more.

Nonprofit social media strategy: a simple framework

  1. Pick one or two goals per quarter. Grow the email list, recruit volunteers for a specific event, raise money for a campaign, or build awareness before a big announcement. Every post should support one of them.
  2. Define the audiences. Donors, volunteers, people you serve, partners, local media. Each wants something different.
  3. Choose your platforms based on those audiences, using the data above.
  4. Set a rhythm you can keep. A steady three to five posts a week across your main platforms is easier to sustain than daily posting for two weeks followed by silence.
  5. Build a monthly calendar around your real schedule: programs, events, giving days, awareness months and board-approved campaigns.
  6. Make every post useful or moving, and give people a clear next step when it fits.
  7. Measure monthly and adjust (see the measurement section below).

Nonprofit social media post ideas

These are the post types that tend to work for mission-driven organizations. Rotate through them across the month.

Show the mission

  • A day in the life of a program, from setup to the last person served.
  • Before and after: the empty lot and the community garden, the classroom before and after a renovation.
  • A short video of one volunteer explaining why they show up.
  • A staff spotlight with a photo and two sentences in their own words.

Prove the impact

  • One number, one photo: "312 families received groceries in March."
  • Year-in-review highlights with real photos, not stock images.
  • A thank-you post that shows exactly what a recent gift paid for.

Invite people in

  • Volunteer shifts with dates, times and a sign-up link.
  • Event announcements, then live Stories during the event, then a recap the next day.
  • A clear, specific ask during a campaign: "We are $4,000 from our goal for winter coats."

Educate

  • Explain the issue you work on in plain language, in a short carousel or video.
  • Answer common questions about your services or eligibility.
  • Share credible research or news related to your cause, with a sentence on what it means locally.

Storytelling: the core of nonprofit social media

Most of a nonprofit's best posts are stories about a person, a place or a moment. A good story post usually has three parts: a real person or situation, what changed, and what the reader can do.

A few rules that protect people and make stories stronger:

  • Get consent in writing before posting anyone's photo, name or story, and explain where it will appear. For minors, get permission from a parent or guardian.
  • Protect dignity. Show people as capable participants, not as objects of pity. Let them tell the story in their own words when they are willing.
  • Change identifying details on sensitive subjects when the person prefers privacy.
  • Use your own photos and video. Real images of your work show supporters what their support actually does.
  • Caption your videos and add alt text to images so people with hearing or vision loss, and people scrolling with the sound off, get the full message.

Strong visuals carry much of this work. A professionally shot set of program photos and a short mission video can supply months of posts. See our guide on brand video for how organizations plan that kind of film.

How to set up a Facebook fundraiser for a nonprofit

Meta's charitable giving tools let supporters create fundraisers and add donate buttons that benefit your charity on Facebook and Instagram, according to PayPal Giving Fund. Here is how the system works as of 2026.

How donations are processed

Meta partners exclusively with PayPal Giving Fund to process donations for nonprofits, per Facebook's Help Center. The change took effect on October 31, 2023, when Meta stopped processing charitable donations through Meta Payments. At the same time, Meta stopped covering processing fees and ended recurring donations, as reported by The Chronicle of Philanthropy (2023).

ItemHow it works
ProcessorPayPal Giving Fund, for nonprofits in the U.S., U.K., Canada and Australia
Processing fee1.99% of the gift plus $0.49, which donors can choose to cover (Chronicle of Philanthropy, 2023)
Recurring giftsNo longer supported on Meta since October 2023
Payout, enrolled charitiesDeposited to the charity's PayPal account 15 to 45 days after the donation, at the end of the month (PayPal)
Payout, unenrolled charitiesTypically by check within 45 days, distributed monthly (PayPal)

Steps for the nonprofit

  1. Set up your Facebook Page correctly. Use the official organization Page, set the category to nonprofit or charity, and list your address in the About section, as outlined in GoodUnited's sign-up guide (updated December 2023).
  2. Confirm eligibility. In the U.S., organizations generally need 501(c)(3) status, a tax ID and an account with a licensed bank, per the same guide.
  3. Gather documents. Expect to provide your organization's legal name, address, tax ID, leadership details and a recent bank statement.
  4. Apply for Meta's giving tools through Meta's charitable giving sign-up from an admin account on the Page. GoodUnited notes approval can take about three weeks.
  5. Enroll with PayPal Giving Fund. Enrolled charities receive funds to their PayPal account. Charities must enroll by the 15th of the month to receive the first payment within that month, per PayPal.
  6. Add a Donate button to your Page and to posts once approved.

Steps for a supporter creating a fundraiser

Per Facebook's Help Center, a supporter first confirms that charitable giving is available in their country, then starts a fundraiser, selects your nonprofit, enters a goal amount and currency, and clicks Create. Make this easy by posting simple instructions before birthdays, giving days and campaign kickoffs.

Where fundraisers fit

Treat Facebook fundraisers as one channel, not the center of your online giving. In the M+R Benchmarks 2025 study, Facebook Fundraiser revenue fell 42% in 2024 and accounted for 0.2% of participants' total online revenue. Your own donation page is where you can build long-term relationships and offer recurring gifts, which Meta no longer supports. Use social posts to send people there, and use Facebook fundraisers to let supporters raise money among their friends.

Influencers and partners

Partners can extend your reach. In the M+R Benchmarks 2026 study, 58% of participating nonprofits worked with outside influencers at least once in 2025, and every one of those programs included Instagram, with 72% including TikTok. The most common goals were narrative and persuasion content, advocacy or volunteer calls to action, and direct fundraising asks.

For many local nonprofits, strong partners are already close: board members, volunteers, local businesses and community leaders. Give them ready-to-post photos, videos and captions.

Nonprofit social media policy: what to include

Every nonprofit should have a written social media policy approved by leadership and shared with staff, board and volunteers. A practical policy covers:

  • Account ownership. The organization owns every account. At least two staff members hold admin access, and passwords are stored securely and changed when people leave.
  • Who can post and approve. Name the roles that can publish, and which posts need sign-off (campaigns, statements, anything sensitive).
  • Consent and privacy. Written consent for photos and stories, extra protection for minors and vulnerable clients, and no sharing of private client details.
  • Political activity. Section 501(c)(3) organizations are prohibited from participating in any political campaign on behalf of or in opposition to any candidate, according to the IRS. Nonpartisan voter education and voter registration are allowed when conducted in a nonpartisan way. Apply this to posts, shares and comments.
  • Comment moderation. What gets hidden or removed (spam, threats, personal attacks, private information) and how criticism is answered.
  • Crisis response. Who speaks for the organization, how quickly, and on which channels.
  • Staff personal accounts. Guidance on identifying as an employee and keeping personal opinions separate from the organization's voice.
  • Accessibility. Captions on video, alt text on images and plain language.

How to measure nonprofit social media results

Measure what connects to the mission. A monthly report should track:

MetricWhat it tells you
Reach and impressionsHow many people saw your content
Engagement rateWhether the content resonates (reactions, comments, shares, saves divided by reach)
Follower growthWhether your community is growing on each platform
Link clicksWhether posts drive people to your site
Email sign-ups from socialWhether social builds your email list, a long-term asset you control
Donations from socialGifts traced to social links (use tracking links) and Facebook fundraiser totals
Volunteer and event sign-upsWhether calls to action work
Response timeHow quickly you answer comments and messages

Use tracking links (UTM codes) on every link to your donation and sign-up pages so your website analytics can show which posts drove action. Each month, note why your top and bottom posts performed the way they did.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best social media platform for nonprofit organizations?

Facebook and Instagram are the core platforms for most nonprofits; nearly every participant in the M+R Benchmarks 2026 study kept a presence on both in 2025. YouTube (74% of participants) suits organizations with strong video, and TikTok (43%) helps reach younger supporters. Choose based on where your donors and volunteers spend time.

What is a good nonprofit social media strategy?

A good strategy sets one or two goals per quarter, focuses on two or three platforms your audiences use, posts real stories of the mission on a steady schedule, and gives each post a clear next step. Review results monthly and adjust.

What are good nonprofit social media post ideas?

Strong ideas include a day in the life of a program, volunteer and staff spotlights, before-and-after photos, one-number impact posts, thank-you posts that show what a gift paid for, event Stories and recaps, and plain-language explainers about your issue.

How do I set up a Facebook fundraiser for a nonprofit?

The nonprofit sets its Facebook Page category to nonprofit or charity, applies for Meta's charitable giving tools, and enrolls with PayPal Giving Fund, which processes donations for Meta. Once approved, supporters can create fundraisers by selecting your nonprofit, setting a goal and currency, and clicking Create. Approval can take a few weeks, so start well before a campaign.

Does Facebook charge fees for nonprofit fundraisers?

Since October 31, 2023, donations are processed by PayPal Giving Fund with a fee of 1.99% plus $0.49 per gift, which donors can choose to cover, as reported by The Chronicle of Philanthropy. Meta stopped covering the fees at that time.

How long does it take to get Facebook fundraiser money?

Enrolled charities receive donations in their PayPal account 15 to 45 days after the donation, at the end of the month, according to PayPal. Unenrolled charities are typically paid by check within 45 days.

What should a nonprofit social media policy include?

It should cover account ownership and admin access, who can post and approve, consent for photos and stories, protection for minors and clients, the IRS limits on political campaign activity for 501(c)(3) organizations, comment moderation, crisis response, staff personal accounts and accessibility.

How often should a nonprofit post on social media?

Consistency matters more than volume. Three to five quality posts a week across your main platforms, plus Stories during events and campaigns, is a sustainable rhythm for most small and midsize nonprofits. Increase frequency during giving days and major campaigns.
