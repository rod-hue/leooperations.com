Professional headshots: what they cost, what to wear, how to pose and how to run a team session

A working photographer's guide to the one image people see more than any other, from pricing and wardrobe to backgrounds, posing and on-location team days.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

A professional headshot in the U.S. typically costs $216 to $427, with a national average of about $304, according to Thumbtack's headshot cost data (updated October 2026). ProShoot's 2026 guide puts specialists in major markets at $500 to $800 and personal branding sessions at $800 to $1,500 or more, with team headshots on location usually priced per person, from roughly $50 to $200 each depending on group size. What you get is an image people will see often: on LinkedIn, your company site, press releases, speaker bios, campaign literature and email signatures.

What does a headshot do?

A headshot is often the first impression someone has of you before they read a word. It signals whether you look credible, approachable and serious about your work. A hiring manager, a donor, a reporter or a prospective client often sees your face before your résumé or your pitch.

LinkedIn's own help center says members with a profile photo receive up to 2X more profile views and 3X more connection requests than members without one. That figure is for having any photo at all. The quality of the photo shapes what people take from that extra attention. A cropped wedding picture, a dim selfie or a years-old image does not present you as well as a current, well-lit portrait.

For organizations, consistency matters too. A leadership page where every executive was photographed with the same light, background and crop looks unified and organized. A page of mismatched snapshots looks less polished, even when the people are excellent.

Prices depend on the photographer's experience, your market, how many outfits you want, how many retouched images you receive and whether the shoot happens in a studio or at your location. These are market ranges from published 2026 data, not any one studio's rates.

What moves the price up: hair and makeup, multiple locations, travel, rush delivery, extra retouched selects and usage rights for paid advertising. Retouching is worth keeping in the budget. Correcting skin tone, color balance and small distractions (a stray hair, a lint spot on a lapel) is what makes a headshot look finished.

What to wear for a professional headshot

Clothing in a headshot should disappear. People should remember your face and your expression, not your shirt.

  • Solid colors over patterns. Fine stripes, small checks and tight herringbone can create a shimmering moiré effect on camera. Solids and subtle textures photograph more cleanly.
  • Mid-tone and jewel colors. Navy, charcoal, deep green, burgundy, soft blue and plum flatter most skin tones. Pure white can lose detail and pure black can swallow the shape of your shoulders against a dark background.
  • Fit matters. A jacket that fits through the shoulders photographs better than an expensive one that does not. Press everything the night before.
  • Necklines frame the face. V-necks and open collars lengthen the neck. Very high or very low necklines distract.
  • Bring options. One formal look and one more relaxed look covers most uses, from a board bio to a conference program.
  • Match your field. A litigator, a pastor, a startup founder and a city council candidate each have a different visual vocabulary. Dress the way your best clients or constituents expect to see you on your best day.
  • Glasses on or off. If you wear glasses every day, wear them in the photo. A good photographer will angle the light and your head to remove glare.

Keep grooming to your normal routine. Many people get a haircut about a week before rather than the day before, and avoid trying new skin products in the days before the shoot.

Headshot poses that work

A common worry about posing for headshots is looking stiff. A short list of small adjustments, directed by the photographer in real time, usually solves it.

  1. Turn the shoulders, square the face. Angle your body about 30 to 45 degrees from the camera and turn your face back toward the lens. This slims the frame and looks more natural than standing square.
  2. Forward and down with the chin. Push your forehead slightly toward the camera and drop your chin a touch. It defines the jawline. It may feel odd, but it usually looks natural in the photo.
  3. Weight on the back foot. It relaxes the shoulders and stops you from leaning in like you are about to argue.
  4. Lean for warmth. A small lean toward the camera reads as engaged. Leaning back reads as distant.
  5. Hands, if they are in frame. Arms crossed loosely, a hand on a table edge, or a hand on a jacket button for three-quarter-length shots. Keep hands relaxed rather than gripping.
  6. Seated variations. Sitting on the front third of a stool with a straight back gives speakers and executives a commanding but relaxed look.

Good headshot photographers tend to keep a conversation going. Natural expressions come more easily from conversation than from being told to "look natural."

Can I smile in a headshot?

Yes. For most business, nonprofit, ministry and political uses, a genuine smile works well, because it signals approachability and confidence at the same time. The question is which smile.

A strong session captures a range: a closed-mouth smile, a full smile with teeth, and a serious, confident look with a slight lift in the eyes. You then choose by use. A real estate agent, a pastor or a customer-facing director will usually lead with the full smile. An attorney's firm page, a CFO's board bio or an actor's dramatic headshot may lean on the composed look. Candidates generally need both, because the same face appears on a friendly door hanger and on a serious debate graphic.

A forced smile is the one to avoid. A photographer who can get a real laugh, and catch the moment just after it, makes a visible difference.

Choosing a headshot background

The background sets the tone of a headshot. There are three main options.

  • Studio solid or gradient. Gray, white, navy or a brand color. Clean, timeless and easy to match across a whole team. This is the standard for corporate directories, government and legal bios.
  • Environmental. Your office, a lobby, a production floor, a sanctuary, a downtown street with soft blur behind you. Environmental headshots tell people where you work and what you do, which is why founders, creatives and candidates often prefer them.
  • Branded. A background color or set built to match your website and print materials, so the headshot drops into your design system without editing.

Two rules apply to all three. The background must separate cleanly from your hair and clothing (dark hair against a black wall disappears). And for a team, the background has to be identical for everyone, which means the same light, distance and color every time. That consistency is a major reason companies hire a professional for group headshots instead of letting each employee submit their own.

Lighting, lenses and the best focal length for headshots

Photographers usually shoot headshots with a short telephoto lens, roughly 85mm to 135mm on a full-frame camera. Longer focal lengths flatten facial features in a flattering way and let the photographer stand at a comfortable conversational distance. Wide lenses used up close (including the main lens on most phones) enlarge the nose and forehead and push the ears back, which is one reason close-up phone portraits can look slightly off even in good light.

Light shapes the face. A professional setup typically uses a large soft key light, a fill or reflector to control shadow depth, and often a hair or rim light to separate you from the background. Light and exposure are also adjusted for every skin tone. Automatic camera settings can misjudge exposure and color for deeper skin tones, and a skilled photographer sets these deliberately for each person.

Team headshots on location

For organizations, an efficient approach is to bring the studio to you. A photographer sets up portable lights and a backdrop in a conference room and photographs people in short slots, so nobody leaves the building.

On-location team pricing is usually per person, with lower rates for larger groups. These are 2026 market ranges from ProShoot:

Group sizeMarket range per person
1 to 5 people$150 to $200
6 to 15 people$100 to $150
16 to 30 people$75 to $125
30+ people$50 to $100, often with a half or full day rate

How to run a team headshot day that goes smoothly:

  1. Pick the look first. Background color, crop (head and shoulders or waist up) and dress code, written down and sent to everyone a week ahead.
  2. Book a room with space. About 15 by 15 feet minimum, away from foot traffic, with power and a door.
  3. Schedule in five to ten minute slots. Build a sign-up sheet, leave a buffer every hour, and assign one internal coordinator.
  4. Plan for the people who missed it. A make-up session (same lights, same background settings, noted by the photographer) keeps new hires matching the rest of the team for years.
  5. Agree on delivery and naming. File names by employee name, sizes for the website and LinkedIn, and a turnaround date.

A major benefit of a professional team session is consistency over time. When the photographer records the light positions, distances and background, future hires can be photographed to match.

How headshots are used

A single session should produce files for every place your face will appear. Plan for these from the start.

  • LinkedIn and professional networks. A square, tightly cropped head and shoulders image that still reads at thumbnail size.
  • Company websites and team pages. Consistent crop and background across the whole staff.
  • Speaker bios, press kits and media. Reporters and event organizers need high-resolution files, often in both vertical and horizontal crops.
  • Campaign and elected official materials. Mail, yard signs, digital ads, debate graphics, ballot guides and official portraits. Candidates need extra room above the head and to the sides so designers can place type.
  • Ministry and nonprofit use. Staff pages, program covers, event flyers and annual reports.
  • Email signatures, directories and badges. Small, but seen daily by everyone you work with.

Ask your photographer to deliver multiple crops of your selects rather than a single file. It saves designers hours and keeps the image consistent everywhere.

What a professional photographer adds

A headshot is often used for several years and in many places, so the cost is spread across a long period of use. A professional controls the things that shape how you come across: flattering light for your face and skin tone, a lens that keeps your features accurate, direction that gets a real expression, a background that fits your brand, and retouching that looks like you on a good day rather than a filtered version of you.

A photograph also gives a true likeness. When a reporter, a voter or a client meets you in person, your face should match the photo. Heavily altered or generated images can create a mismatch, which matters for organizations that depend on trust, such as law firms, campaigns, health systems, churches and schools.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a pro headshot?

In 2026, a professional headshot in the U.S. averages about $304, with most sessions between $216 and $427, according to Thumbtack's national data. ProShoot's 2026 guide puts specialists in major markets at $500 to $800 and personal branding sessions at $800 to $1,500 or more. Team headshots on location are usually priced per person, from about $50 to $200 each.

What does a headshot do?

A headshot helps build trust before you say anything. It shows employers, clients, voters and reporters that you are credible and approachable, and LinkedIn reports that members with a profile photo receive up to 2X more profile views and 3X more connection requests. A strong headshot helps that first impression work in your favor.

Can I smile in a headshot?

Yes, and for most business, nonprofit, church and campaign uses a genuine smile works well. A good session captures a full smile, a closed-mouth smile and a confident serious look so you can pick by purpose. The key is a real expression, which comes from a photographer who keeps you talking.

What are the best headshot poses?

Angle your shoulders 30 to 45 degrees from the camera and turn your face back to the lens. Push your forehead slightly forward and drop your chin a touch to define the jawline, and keep your weight on your back foot. A slight lean toward the camera reads as warm and engaged.

What is the best headshot background?

A solid gray, white, navy or brand color background is the most versatile and the easiest to match across a team. Environmental backgrounds (your office, a lobby, a city street with soft blur) add context and work well for founders, creatives and candidates. Whichever you choose, it has to separate cleanly from your hair and clothing.

What is the best focal length for taking headshots?

Many professional headshots are made with a short telephoto lens between about 85mm and 135mm on a full-frame camera. These focal lengths keep facial proportions accurate and flattering. Wide lenses used close to the face, including most phone cameras, can distort the nose and forehead.

How do team headshots on location work?

The photographer sets up portable lights and a backdrop in a room at your office and photographs employees in five to ten minute slots. Everyone gets the same light, background and crop, so the team page looks unified. The photographer records the setup so new hires can be photographed to match later.

How often should I update my headshot?

A common guideline is every two to three years, or sooner after a noticeable change in hairstyle, weight, glasses or role. Candidates and executives often refresh before a campaign launch, a promotion announcement or a major speaking season. The goal is simple: people should recognize you when they meet you.
