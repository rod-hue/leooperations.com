How to Hire a Political Consultant (and What They Actually Charge)

A practical guide to the kinds of political consulting firms, how each one gets paid, the questions to ask before you sign, and the red flags that cost campaigns money.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

Hiring a political consultant means hiring a specialist (general strategy, field, media, digital, mail, polling or fundraising) and agreeing up front on how they get paid. Most are paid through some mix of a monthly retainer, a commission or markup on what they buy for you (the traditional media commission is 15 percent of the ad buy, often negotiated lower), per-unit field pricing, and sometimes a win bonus. A local race might spend a few thousand dollars a month on consultants; a competitive congressional or statewide race can pay its consultants hundreds of thousands of dollars over a cycle. The smartest thing a candidate can do is understand exactly where every consultant's money comes from before signing anything.

What kinds of political consulting firms are there?

"Political consultant" covers very different businesses. Knowing which kind you need saves you from paying a strategist to do a vendor's job, or a vendor to do strategy.

Firm typeWhat they doHow they usually get paid
General consultantOverall strategy, message, budget, managing the other vendorsMonthly retainer, sometimes a win bonus
Field firmPaid canvassing, voter registration, GOTV, petition drivesPer door, per contact, per signature, or cost plus management fee
Media firmWrites and produces TV, radio and video adsProduction fees plus commission on the media buy
Media buyerPlaces broadcast, cable, streaming and radio timeCommission or flat fee on the buy
Digital firmOnline ads, social, email, list building, websitesRetainer plus a percentage of ad spend
Mail firmDesigns, prints and drops direct mailMarkup on each piece, or design fee plus print and postage
PollsterBenchmark, tracking and message-testing surveysPer project
Fundraising consultantCall time, events, finance plans, donor programsMonthly retainer, sometimes bonuses tied to goals

Many firms combine several of these. That is fine, but it changes the incentives, which we cover below.

General consultant

The general consultant is the strategist who sits next to the candidate and campaign manager. They write or approve the plan, set the budget, shape the message and hold the other vendors accountable. On smaller races the general consultant may also do the mail or the digital, which is where conflicts start to creep in.

Field firm

A field firm puts people on doors and on phones. It recruits, trains and manages paid canvassers, cuts turf, runs the data, and reports results. Good field firms also run voter registration and signature drives. For a deeper look at what door knocking costs and moves, see our guide to canvassing cost and effectiveness.

Media firm and media buyer

The media firm makes the ads. The media buyer places them. Sometimes they are the same company, sometimes not. The buy is usually the single largest line in a serious campaign budget, so how the buyer is paid matters more than almost any other contract term. Our political TV ad cost guide explains what airtime actually runs.

Digital, mail, polling and fundraising

Digital firms run paid social, search, streaming video and programmatic ads, plus email and SMS. Mail firms design and produce printed pieces. Pollsters measure where the race stands and which messages move people. Fundraising consultants build the finance plan and keep the candidate on call time. Each is a specialty, and each can be done well or badly regardless of firm size.

How do political consultants get paid?

There is no published rate card for this industry, and fees vary widely by race size, region and how competitive the seat is. Here are the common structures, with sourced figures where they exist and our experience-based market ranges clearly labeled.

Retainers

A monthly retainer pays for strategy and time. These are market ranges we see across races, not a published survey and not Leo Operations rates:

Race levelTypical general consultant retainer (market range)
City council, school board, small-town mayor$1,500 to $5,000 per month
Big-city mayor, state legislature, countywide$5,000 to $15,000 per month
Congressional, statewide$10,000 to $30,000+ per month

Not every firm charges a big retainer. One Democratic consultant told the Center for Public Integrity that most firms working federal or major statewide races make their money on the buy rather than a monthly fee (Center for Public Integrity).

Commissions on media buys

The traditional media commission is 15 percent of the buy. The Center for Public Integrity documented that standard and noted that commissions on very large buys are often stepped down, for example 15 percent on the first $1 million and less after that (Center for Public Integrity). Today many campaigns negotiate single-digit commissions, a flat fee, or a capped commission on large buys. Commissions are usually buried inside the payment to the media firm, so they do not show up as a separate line in FEC reports. Ask for the commission in writing.

Markups on mail and printing

Mail consultants often earn their money through markups on each piece rather than an hourly fee. A 2023 investigation by The Stranger into Seattle council races found consultants charging markups ranging from 10 percent to 180 percent over printing cost, including one piece billed at $0.53 that cost $0.19 to produce (The Stranger, 2023). A markup is not wrong in itself, since targeting and design are real work. Hiding it is the problem.

Per-door, per-contact and per-signature pricing

Field firms usually price by the unit: per door attempted, per completed conversation, per registration, or per valid signature. Unit prices depend heavily on density, travel, how fast you need it and labor markets. Signature prices in particular swing with competition: in early 2026 California circulators were being paid $12 to $15 per signature on dueling statewide measures, up from about $4 when collection started (San Francisco Standard, 2026). Our ballot measure signature gathering guide goes deeper.

Polling

A quality benchmark poll is a project fee, and the price depends on sample size, mode and what the deliverable includes. Two University of Houston researchers gave High Plains Public Radio different reference points in 2024 (HPPR, 2024). Brandon Rottinghaus put a well-funded poll with a sample of about 1,500 people at $40,000 to $60,000. Richard Murray put live phone interviewing at about $100 per completed interview, with online samples roughly half that. Those are different assumptions, not one formula: 1,500 live-phone completes at $100 each would be $150,000 in fieldwork alone. These are separate estimates, not a single pricing formula, and the source does not reconcile them. When you get a quote, ask for the number of completes, the mode mix, the questionnaire length, and whether crosstabs, a written memo and a briefing are included. Smaller local polls with fewer interviews cost much less.

Win bonuses

A win bonus is a payment due only if the candidate wins. They are common in consulting contracts; a sample consultant agreement in an FEC enforcement file combined a monthly retainer with a bonus payable only on victory (FEC MUR 4742). In our experience they range from one or two months of retainer on local races to five figures on larger ones. Make sure the bonus is defined (primary, general, or both), capped, and budgeted, because the campaign has to have the cash on hand after Election Day.

Fundraising consultants

Political fundraisers are usually paid a monthly retainer, sometimes with bonuses for hitting goals. Paying a straight percentage of money raised is legal under federal campaign finance law in most cases but is widely frowned on by fundraising professionals, because it rewards the easiest money rather than the best plan. If you agree to a performance bonus, tie it to clear targets, not a cut of every check.

When should you hire a political consultant?

Earlier than most candidates think, but not all at once.

  1. Before you announce: a general consultant or experienced campaign manager to build the plan and budget. If the race is competitive, a pollster soon after.
  2. Six to nine months out: a fundraising consultant if your budget depends on major donors, and a digital firm to build your list and website.
  3. Four to six months out: the field firm, so it can recruit, train and test before voters are paying attention. Most campaigns start paid canvassing too late.
  4. Three to four months out: the media firm and buyer, so ads are produced and time is reserved before rates rise.
  5. Ten to twelve weeks out: the mail program, with the plan and drop dates locked.

For ballot measures the calendar is driven by the signature deadline, which often lands a year or more before the vote. For a full timeline and budget split, see our political campaign budget guide.

Questions to ask in an RFP or interview

Use these whether you send a formal RFP or just take a few calls.

  • Which races like this one have you worked in the last two cycles, and what did you personally do on each?
  • Who will actually do the work day to day? Will I meet them before signing?
  • How do you get paid on every line: retainer, commission, markup, unit price, bonus? Put it in one table.
  • What is your commission or markup on media, digital and mail? Is it on gross or net?
  • Do you own or have a financial stake in any vendor you will recommend (printer, list vendor, texting platform, ad tech)?
  • Who else are you working for this cycle, and in which districts or races?
  • What will you report, how often, and can we see raw data (door counts, GPS logs, ad invoices, print and postage receipts)?
  • What does the field firm do to verify canvassers actually knocked the doors they report?
  • Can we see sample work: ads, mail, a field report, a poll memo?
  • Can we call three past clients, including one who lost?
  • How do we end the contract if it is not working?

Red flags when hiring political consultants

  • They will not explain how they make money on your campaign. If the answer is "it's all bundled," walk.
  • The only number they talk about is the total budget, not results per dollar.
  • They want most of the budget to flow through their own shop and they own the vendor.
  • Field results arrive as totals with no door-level data. Inflated door counts are a real and documented problem in paid canvassing.
  • They promise a win. No honest consultant does.
  • The senior person who pitched you disappears after signing.
  • They are working for a candidate in a race that could conflict with yours.
  • They push to sign quickly and skip references.
  • They have no written contract template, or the contract has no termination clause.

Contracts and conflicts of interest

Every engagement should be in writing. A solid political consulting contract covers:

  • Scope of work, deliverables, and who the key staff are.
  • Every form of compensation: retainer, commission rates, markups, unit prices, bonuses and expense reimbursement.
  • Payment schedule and what happens to commissions if the buy is cancelled.
  • Ownership of data, voter contact records, video footage, creative and social accounts. The campaign should hold its own accounts (ad, social, email, texting, web) and own the data it creates, such as supporter and donor lists, volunteer records and contact results. Purchased voter files and party-provided data are usually licensed, not owned, so continued access and any transfer follow the license terms; get those terms in writing.
  • Termination for convenience with short notice, and what is owed on termination.
  • Conflict disclosure: other clients in overlapping races, and any ownership in subcontractors.
  • Compliance: who is responsible for disclaimers, FEC or state reporting support, and record keeping.

For federal races there is an extra layer. If your consultant also works for a super PAC or outside group spending in your race, FEC coordination rules can turn that group's spending into an illegal contribution. The "common vendor" rule looks at whether a vendor provided certain services to your campaign in the previous 120 days (FEC coordinated communications). Shared vendors are not illegal in themselves; the exposure comes from using or passing along material information about your campaign's plans. Firms that work both sides need a written, enforced firewall. Our guide to super PACs and independent expenditures explains how that works.

Members of the American Association of Political Consultants sign a code of ethics, which is worth asking about, though it is a trade association standard and not a license.

Frequently asked questions

How much do political consultants charge?

It depends on the race and the role. Based on market ranges we see, a general consultant on a local race might charge $1,500 to $5,000 a month, while congressional and statewide consultants can charge $10,000 to $30,000 or more a month. Media firms have traditionally taken a 15 percent commission on ad buys, and mail firms often mark up each piece. Ask for every fee in writing.

What does a political consultant do?

A general political consultant builds and runs campaign strategy: the plan, the budget, the message, and oversight of other vendors. Specialist consultants handle field, media, digital, mail, polling or fundraising. On small races one firm may wear several hats.

Do political consultants get paid if you lose?

Usually, yes. Retainers, production fees, commissions and unit-priced field work are earned as the work is done. Only a win bonus depends on the outcome, and only if your contract includes one.

What is a political field firm?

A field firm runs voter contact on the ground: paid canvassing, voter registration, get-out-the-vote and petition signature drives. It recruits and manages canvassers, handles turf and data, and reports results, typically priced per door, per contact or per signature.

What is the difference between a political media firm and a media buyer?

The media firm writes and produces ads. The media buyer negotiates and places the airtime and digital inventory. Some firms do both. Because the buy is often the biggest line in the budget, the buyer's commission or fee is worth negotiating carefully.

How do I find political consulting firms near me?

Ask your state party, past candidates and local elected officials who they used and whether they would hire them again. Look at your state's campaign finance reports or FEC filings for comparable races to see which firms were paid. Many good firms work nationally, so do not limit yourself to your zip code.

Is it legal to pay a political consultant a percentage of the money they raise?

Federal law generally allows it, but many fundraising professionals consider it unethical, and some states regulate it. Most political fundraisers work on a retainer, with bonuses tied to defined goals. Check your state's rules before agreeing to a commission.

When should a campaign hire a consultant?

Bring in strategy before you announce, fundraising and digital six to nine months out, field four to six months out, and media and mail on a schedule that lets you reserve time and lock drop dates. Starting field late is the most common and most expensive mistake.
