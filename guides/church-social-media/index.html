Church social media strategy: the right platforms, a weekly calendar, post ideas and a policy that protects your people

A working plan for pastors, communications staff and media volunteers, built on current platform data, with a content calendar, sermon clip workflow and social media policy checklist.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

A church social media strategy works when it is built around one weekly rhythm: the sermon and the life of the congregation, captured well and posted on a steady schedule to the platforms your people actually use. For most churches that means Facebook and YouTube first (Pew Research Center's 2026 survey found 70% and 84% of U.S. adults use them), Instagram for reaching younger adults and families (79% of adults 18 to 29 use it), and short vertical sermon clips on every channel. Add a written social media policy, especially for photos of children, and a clear owner who is accountable for posting every week.

Why social media is part of ministry now

Social media is where many first-time guests meet your church before they ever see the building. In Lifeway Research's 2023 study of churchgoers who switched congregations, 29% used social media sites to research churches before choosing one, alongside 37% who used church websites, according to Lifeway Research. Among people who had moved to a new area, social media research rose to 32%.

It is also how your own members stay connected between Sundays. A homebound mother sees the choir anniversary. A college freshman three states away catches Sunday's message in a one-minute clip. A neighbor sees the back-to-school giveaway and brings her kids. Every one of those moments depends on someone capturing it well and posting it on time.

What are the best social media platforms for churches?

The best social media platforms for churches are the ones your current members and your target neighbors already use daily. Pew's 2026 survey of 5,511 U.S. adults (conducted January to June 2026) shows how much that varies by age, per its social media fact sheet (published October 2026):

PlatformAll adultsAges 18 to 29Ages 30 to 49Ages 50 to 64Ages 65+
YouTube84%95%91%85%65%
Facebook70%69%78%71%59%
Instagram51%79%64%41%21%
TikTok31%65%45%31%11%

How to read that for a church:

  • Facebook is the church bulletin board for adults 30 and up. Events, live streams, prayer updates, birthdays, homegoing announcements and photo albums belong here. Facebook Groups work well for ministries such as men's, women's and youth parents.
  • YouTube is the sermon library, and the platform the most adults use. Every full sermon and every short clip should live here with a searchable title (sermon title, scripture, preacher's name, church name, city).
  • Instagram reaches young adults and young families. Reels, Stories with event countdowns, and polished graphics carry the most weight.
  • TikTok reaches the youngest adults. It rewards personality and short teaching moments. It is the right next step for a church with an active young adult or youth ministry and someone who understands the platform.

Do two platforms well before you add a third. An abandoned account with a last post from 2023 can leave guests wondering whether the church is still meeting.

A weekly church social media calendar

Consistency matters more than volume. A church social media calendar built around Sunday gives every day a job and makes the work predictable for a small team.

DayPostPlatforms
MondaySermon recap clip (30 to 90 seconds) plus full sermon linkInstagram, Facebook, YouTube Shorts, TikTok
TuesdayScripture or quote graphic from Sunday's messageInstagram, Facebook
WednesdayMidweek service or Bible study reminder, prayer request promptFacebook, Instagram Stories
ThursdayMinistry spotlight or volunteer story (photo carousel or short video)Instagram, Facebook
FridaySecond sermon clip or a worship momentInstagram, Facebook, YouTube Shorts
Saturday"See you tomorrow" post with service times and plan-your-visit linkFacebook, Instagram Stories
SundayLive stream, welcome post, photos from the service by eveningFacebook, YouTube, Instagram Stories

Layer events on top: announce four to six weeks ahead, remind two weeks out, post daily Stories the final week, then share photos and a recap within 48 hours. Plan a month at a time and schedule posts with Meta Business Suite or a scheduling tool, so one sick volunteer does not stop the calendar.

Church social media post ideas that people engage with

Here are church social media post ideas grouped by purpose. Posts that draw the most response tend to show real people, real moments and one clear idea.

Teaching

  • Sermon clips with burned-in captions, opening on the strongest line.
  • Scripture graphics tied to the week's message.
  • A 60-second "one thing to remember" from the pastor recorded after service.
  • Short series trailers before a new sermon series begins.

Community and care

  • Volunteer and ministry spotlights ("Meet the hospitality team").
  • Baptism celebrations, with written permission from the person baptized.
  • Milestones: church anniversaries, pastoral anniversaries, choir anniversaries, graduates, new members.
  • Prayer prompts that invite comments without exposing anyone's private needs.

Invitation

  • Service times and a plan-your-visit link pinned to every profile.
  • "What to expect" posts for first-time guests: parking, children's check-in, dress, how long the service runs.
  • Event countdowns and recap photo albums.
  • Community service days, food drives, voter registration and health fairs.

Worship

  • Short choir or praise team moments, cut cleanly at a musical phrase. Check your streaming license before posting music.
  • Behind-the-scenes rehearsal clips.

A common question is what makes social media posts engaging. For churches, the answer is usually faces and stories. A well-lit photo of an usher greeting a family often does better than a text-only flyer, and a clear 45-second clip of the pastor making one point often reaches more people than a 40-minute upload with no description.

Sermon clips and Reels

Short vertical video is now a central format on every major platform. Instagram extended Reels to three minutes in January 2025, according to Social Media Today, but the best sermon clips are still usually 30 to 90 seconds.

A clip workflow that holds up week after week:

  1. Record the sermon with a tight camera angle and audio from the soundboard. Our church live streaming guide covers the setup.
  2. On Monday, mark three to six moments: a strong opening line, a story, a practical takeaway, a memorable phrase.
  3. Reframe each clip to 9:16 vertical, centered on the speaker.
  4. Add captions in your church fonts and colors, and a title card with the series name.
  5. Post on a schedule across Instagram, Facebook, YouTube Shorts and TikTok, each with a caption that names the sermon, scripture and church.

The camera matters here. Clips cut from a distant wide shot with room-microphone audio are hard to see and hear on a phone. Clips cut from a well-framed, well-lit, well-mixed recording are easier to watch and share.

Church graphics that look like one church

Church social media graphics should look like they came from one place. Pick a primary logo, two or three brand colors and two fonts, then build templates for the post types you repeat: sermon series, scripture quotes, events, announcements, giving, and memorial or homegoing notices. Design at 1080 by 1350 pixels for Instagram and Facebook feed posts and 1080 by 1920 for Stories and Reels covers. Keep text short enough to read on a phone at a glance.

Church social media templates save time, but a template only works when it fits your brand system. Sermon series art deserves a designer's attention because it carries the series for six to eight weeks across slides, social, the website and print. Our guide to professional social media graphics covers sizes, brand kits and proofreading in detail.

Church social media policy: what to include

Every church should have a written social media policy, approved by leadership and shared with staff and volunteers. AG Financial, which serves churches, recommends written permission to post a person's photograph, written permission from parents or guardians before posting photos of minors, and never posting a minor's birth date, phone number, email address or school name, in its guide to creating a church social media policy. It also warns against posting prayer needs with people's names attached.

A practical policy checklist:

  • Ownership and access. The church, not an individual, owns every account. At least two staff members hold admin access, logins live in a shared password manager, and two-factor authentication is on.
  • Photos of children and minors. Collect a signed media release from a parent or guardian at registration for children's ministry, camps and youth events. Keep a "no photo" list and use a visual marker (a colored wristband or lanyard) so photographers know who to avoid. Post first names only or no names, never tag minors, and favor group shots.
  • Adults. Announce that services and events are photographed and streamed. Honor every request to remove a photo quickly.
  • Sensitive moments. No photos during altar calls, counseling, private prayer or grief without permission from the family.
  • Prayer requests. Share only with explicit consent and only the details the person approves.
  • Staff and volunteer conduct. No private direct messaging between adults and minors; youth leaders communicate in group threads that include parents or a second adult.
  • Comments. Respond within a set time, hide spam and abuse, and move pastoral conversations to private channels.
  • Music and copyright. Use only music your licenses cover and images you have rights to.
  • Crisis plan. Who speaks for the church, who approves statements, and how quickly.

Review the policy every year with your insurer and denomination's guidance. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

Who should run church social media: volunteer, staff or agency?

The right church social media manager is whoever can post with quality every single week, has authority to get answers from ministry leaders, and follows your policy. Churches use three models.

Volunteer. Gifted volunteers bring heart and energy. To work at a professional standard they need a brand kit, templates, the policy, a content calendar and a staff point person who approves posts. Volunteers should never be the only people with account access.

Staff. A part-time or full-time communications staffer gives the most control and can cover social, the website, slides and the bulletin. How much a church social media manager makes varies widely by region and church size. As a federal benchmark for the closest occupation, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median annual wage of $74,750 for public relations specialists (May 2025), per the Occupational Outlook Handbook.

Agency or media partner. A professional team brings cameras, editing, design and consistency that many churches find hard to staff internally, and frees church staff to focus on people. The strongest setup pairs an outside team that produces sermon clips, photography and graphics with an inside staff member who knows the congregation and approves everything.

Whichever model you choose, measure what matters each month: reach and followers on each platform, video views and completion on clips, clicks to the plan-your-visit and giving pages, and first-time guests who say they found you online.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best social media platforms for churches?

Facebook and YouTube reach the broadest range of ages, with 70% and 84% of U.S. adults using them in Pew's 2026 survey. Instagram is the strongest platform for young adults and families (79% of adults 18 to 29), and TikTok reaches the youngest adults. Most churches should run Facebook, YouTube and Instagram well before adding more.

What is a good church social media strategy?

Build everything around the weekly sermon and the life of the congregation. Capture the service well, cut it into short clips, post on a set weekly calendar, use consistent branded graphics, follow a written policy, and point every post to a next step such as service times or a plan-your-visit page.

What should a church post on social media?

Sermon clips, scripture graphics, service times, event countdowns and recaps, ministry and volunteer spotlights, milestones such as anniversaries and baptisms (with permission), and "what to expect" posts for first-time guests. Real people and real moments tend to perform best.

What are the most engaging social media posts?

Short vertical videos and posts with real faces and stories tend to outperform text-only graphics. For churches, that means clear sermon clips with captions, worship moments and photos of people serving and celebrating together.

How often should a church post on social media?

Aim for one meaningful post a day on your main platforms, plus a live stream and Stories on Sunday. Consistency matters more than volume, so start with a schedule your team can keep every week.

What should a church social media policy include?

Account ownership and admin access, written permission for photos, signed parent or guardian releases for minors, rules against naming or tagging children, guidelines for prayer requests and sensitive moments, conduct rules for staff and volunteers, comment moderation, copyright rules, and a crisis plan.

Can churches post pictures of children on social media?

Churches commonly do, with safeguards. Collect written permission from a parent or guardian first, never post identifying details such as a child's full name, birth date or school, avoid tagging minors, keep a "no photo" list, and favor group shots.

How much does a church social media manager make?

Pay varies by region, church size and whether the role also covers the website and slides. The closest federal benchmark is the Bureau of Labor Statistics median of $74,750 a year for public relations specialists (May 2025). Many churches instead pair a part-time staff member with an outside media team.
