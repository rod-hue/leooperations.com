How to get people to show up to your event, with the turnout math organizers use

RSVP-to-attendance ratios, a promotion timeline that works, the channels that actually move people, and the confirmation calls that turn "maybe" into a full room.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

To get people to show up to your event, work backward from turnout math: divide the crowd you want by the share of RSVPs you expect to show. For a free event, a quarter to a half of RSVPs typically stay home, which means 1.33 to 2 RSVPs for every person you need. We plan on two to one, the conservative end, because a full room is the goal. Then get those RSVPs mostly through personal invitations, not posts, and confirm every one of them by phone or text in the final 48 hours. Most people decide late, so concentrate your heaviest promotion in the last ten days.

Turnout math: how many RSVPs do you need?

The most important number in event planning is the gap between "yes" and "here." Recent data:

  • PheedLoop's 2026 analysis of check-in data from more than 1,070 live events found a median no-show rate of about 28% for free events and 17% for paid events. For small free events of 10 to 49 people, the median no-show rate was about 37%.
  • Industry reporting in Skift puts no-show rates for events with no registration fee as high as 40 to 60%, against roughly 10 to 20% for paid events.
  • In a 2021 randomized test of a political fundraiser by Sister District, about 56% of people who RSVP'd attended. RSVPs who confirmed attended at a much higher rate, though confirmation was not randomly assigned, so that gap shows who was already committed as much as what confirming does.

The formula is simple: RSVPs needed = desired attendance ÷ expected show rate. Our working rule for community and political events, based on those numbers and years in the field: an unconfirmed free RSVP is worth about half a person. A confirmed RSVP is worth closer to three-quarters. A paid ticket is worth 80% or more.

Put a little money in the way when you can. Skift described a Basecamp event where a refundable $100 deposit cut registrations from 84 to 55 but raised attendance from 38 to 50. Even a $5 or $10 ticket, or a suggested donation, changes how people treat their RSVP.

How to attract a crowd for an event: give people a reason

No promotion plan can save an event that is not worth the trip. Before you print anything, answer the question every invitee is silently asking: what do I get by being there in person that I cannot get from a recap video?

Things that genuinely pull a crowd:

  • A draw. A known speaker, a performer, a local celebrity, a candidate who will take questions.
  • Food. Free food is not a cliché. It is one of the most reliable drivers at community events.
  • A role. People come when they are needed. Give invitees a job: greet, bring a dish, read a name, hold a sign.
  • Their people. Partner organizations that bring their members as a bloc fill rooms faster than any ad.
  • Convenience. A time and place that fit real lives: near transit, parking that is free, childcare or a kids' corner, under two hours.
  • A clear ending and a reason to stay. Announce the program length. People skip open-ended evenings.

For a full production checklist (venue, permits, sound, security), see how to organize a concert or rally.

Promotion channels, ranked by what actually moves people

Every channel has a role, but they are not equal. Personal asks fill rooms. Broadcast channels make personal asks easier.

Personal asks and relational organizing

In our field experience, nothing fills a room like an invitation from someone the guest already knows. Recruit 10 to 20 "hosts" who each commit to bringing five people, and give them a simple text they can forward. A host committee of 20 people bringing five each is 100 attendees before you spend a dollar on ads. This is the same relational logic behind good volunteer recruitment, covered in how to recruit and manage volunteers.

Text messages

Texts are read quickly and work well for reminders and last-week pushes. Peer-to-peer texting platforms let volunteers send personal-feeling messages at scale. Follow the platform's consent and opt-out rules, and identify who is texting.

Email

Email is cheap and good for your existing list, but response rates are modest. M+R's nonprofit benchmarks found advocacy emails drew about a 2.3% click-through rate. Send a save-the-date, a full invitation, a "this week" reminder and a day-before logistics email.

Social media

Use organic posts to reach supporters and give hosts something to share, and use small paid boosts targeted by ZIP code in the final ten days. Facebook events, Instagram stories and short video of last year's event work better than flyers posted as images. For paid strategy, see political digital advertising.

Flyers and posters

Flyers work in tight geographies: laundromats, barbershops, salons, libraries, church bulletins, corner stores, apartment lobbies. They work best handed to someone with a sentence of explanation.

Community partners

Congregations, unions, neighborhood associations, Greek organizations and youth sports leagues can each move dozens of people. Ask leaders for a pulpit or meeting announcement, a newsletter mention and, best of all, a commitment to bring a group.

Local media

Send a media advisory to local TV assignment desks, community papers, Black, Latino and other community media, and local radio. Get listed on every community calendar, since those listings reach people outside your network. A news story the morning of the event raises your profile for the next one.

When should you start promoting? The promotion timeline

Promotion is a crescendo, not a flat line. Eventbrite's 2024 music data showed 57% of tickets sold a week or less before the show, and free community events skew even later. Start early enough to lock your hosts and partners, then save your heaviest push for the last ten days.

Confirmation calls and reminder cadence

Confirmation is where turnout is won. An RSVP is a soft yes. A confirmation is a commitment. Sister District's 2018 experiment with its own volunteers found that confirming by personal call and text raised attendance by 51% over an email confirmation. Its 2021 test also found that RSVPs who actively confirmed attended at a much higher rate, an association that makes confirmation a good way to sort your list into likely and unlikely attendees.

A good confirmation script is short:

  1. "Hi James, it's Keisha with the Eastside Neighbors. You signed up for Thursday's town hall."
  2. "Are you still planning to come at 6:30?" (Get a clear yes or no.)
  3. "Great. It's at the rec center on Elm, parking is free behind the building. Are you bringing anyone?"
  4. "I'll text you the details. See you Thursday."

The default cadence is two personal touches plus one automated reminder, exactly as the timeline above lays out. The 2021 Sister District test randomized an extra personal text the morning of, on top of Mobilize's automated reminders, and it did not change attendance. Anything beyond the default is conditional: one extra text only to people you never reached, never to people who already confirmed. Every text should identify who is sending it, and anyone who replies STOP or asks to be left alone comes off the list immediately.

How to boost attendance at events on the day

Day-of tactics decide the final count, especially for free events.

  • Start on time, but program the best moment 15 minutes in. Latecomers still catch it.
  • Hold the default cadence on the day. If you used the optional doors-open text for unreached RSVPs, that is the last message. Extra day-of texts to people who already got three contacts cost more goodwill than they add bodies.
  • Set up the room for the crowd you expect, not the one you hope for. Put out 70% of the chairs and stack the rest visibly. Adding chairs feels like momentum; empty rows feel like failure, and they look worse in photos.
  • Staff the door with greeters who know regulars by name and steer newcomers to a host.
  • Capture everyone. A sign-in sheet or QR code at the door, with name, phone, email and ZIP.
  • Make the ask from the stage. Every event should end with one clear next step: sign up for a shift, come to the next meeting, bring a friend.

Measuring turnout and building the list for next time

Every event is a list-building event. Within 48 hours:

  • Compare RSVPs, confirmations and actual sign-ins by source (host, partner, email, social, flyer). This tells you which channels actually delivered people, not just clicks.
  • Enter every sign-in into your database the same day, tagged with the event.
  • Send a thank-you with photos or a 30 to 60 second recap video, and the next ask. A recap showing a full, energized room is the strongest promotion you will have for the next event.
  • Call RSVPs who did not come (skipping anyone who opted out). "We missed you. Next one is the 14th." This is an invitation to the next event, not another reminder, and it turns no-shows into next month's attendees.
  • Write down your show rate. After three or four events you will have your own turnout math, which beats any national average.

Organizations that do this compound. A first town hall might draw 60 people; a fourth, built on three events of sign-ins and hosts, can draw several times that.

Frequently asked questions

How to boost attendance at events?

Get RSVPs through personal invitations from people the invitee knows, then confirm every RSVP by call or text three to five days out and send a logistics text the day before. Add a draw such as a speaker, performer or food, keep the event convenient and under two hours, and concentrate promotion in the final ten days.

How to attract a crowd for an event?

Give people a concrete reason to be there in person, then recruit hosts and partner organizations who each commit to bringing a group. Personal networks fill rooms far more reliably than ads or flyers. Use social media and local media to support those personal asks.

What percentage of RSVPs actually show up?

For free events, expect about 50% to 75% of RSVPs to attend. A 2026 analysis of over 1,000 events found a median no-show rate of about 28% for free events, and higher for small ones. Paid events typically see 80% or more attend.

How far in advance should you promote an event?

Start six to eight weeks out to recruit hosts and partners, announce four weeks out, and put your heaviest effort into the final ten days. A majority of attendance decisions are made in the last week.

How do I get people to come to a free event?

Divide the crowd you want by your expected show rate. For free events we plan conservatively on half, so we collect twice as many RSVPs as we need. Confirm personally, consider a small suggested donation or deposit, and give people a role so they feel needed.

How many reminders should I send before an event?

Use one default: a personal confirmation three to five days out, a personal logistics text the day before, and one automated morning-of reminder. Add a single extra text only for RSVPs you never reached. Sister District's randomized 2021 test found an extra personal text on top of automated reminders did not change attendance.
