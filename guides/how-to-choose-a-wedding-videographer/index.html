How to choose a wedding videographer: full films, audio, contracts, red flags and a scorecard you can use

A practical selection process for couples and the parents paying, with the contract clauses that protect your film and a Nashville section for Middle Tennessee weddings.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

To choose a wedding videographer, watch at least two complete wedding films (not just trailers), listen closely to the vows and toasts, and check how the footage holds up in a dark reception room. Then confirm crew size, coverage hours, exact deliverables and delivery dates in writing, and read the contract for backup gear, footage ownership, music licensing and cancellation terms. Book 10 to 12 months out, earlier for a Saturday between May and October. The scorecard below turns all of that into a side-by-side comparison you can fill in after each consultation.

For prices, see our wedding videographer cost guide; for cinematic versus documentary approaches, see wedding videography styles.

Step one: watch full films, not highlight trailers

A 60 second trailer shows you a videographer's best eight shots from a 10 hour day. A full film shows you whether they were in the right place for the ninth through the hundredth. Ask every videographer on your shortlist for two complete weddings delivered in the last 12 months, ideally one at a venue like yours.

When you watch, look for these things:

  • Coverage of the whole ceremony. A good crew keeps one camera locked on a wide shot of the full ceremony, so nothing is missed when the main camera moves.
  • Faces at the key moments. You should see the reaction of the person hearing the vows, not just the back of the person speaking them.
  • Parents and grandparents. Families paying for the wedding usually want to see themselves in the film. Count how often the older generation appears.
  • Consistency from start to finish. The first dance should look as good as the getting-ready footage shot in window light at noon.

In The Knot's 2026 Real Weddings Study, 81% of couples said examples of work were the most important factor when choosing a photographer, ahead of price at 73% (The Knot Real Weddings Study 2026). Apply that standard to video, with complete films, not reels.

Step two: listen before you judge the picture

Audio is where wedding videos most often fail, and it is the part you cannot fix with a filter. In The Knot's 2026 study, 61% of couples wrote their own vows (The Knot). Those words are the reason many couples hire a videographer in the first place.

Close your eyes for the vows and the toasts in a sample film. You should hear every word clearly, with no wind noise, no clipping when the best man shouts, and no echo from a stone sanctuary. Then ask how they capture it:

  • A wireless lavalier microphone on whoever is wearing the suit, and often on the officiant
  • A recorder on the podium or plugged into the DJ's soundboard for toasts and speeches
  • Backup recorders, so one dead battery does not erase the vows

If the answer is "the camera mic picks it up," keep looking. A camera microphone 30 feet back in a crowded room records the room, not the couple.

Step three: check low light, weather and your traditions

Ask to see footage from a dim reception hall, a candlelit church or an evening outdoor ceremony. Grainy, muddy or orange-tinted footage of the first dance means the videographer either lacks the lenses and lights for the job or does not know how to use them. Ask what lighting they bring, how they handle venues that ban added light during the ceremony, and what their plan is if rain moves everything indoors.

For many families, traditions matter most. A Nigerian traditional engagement, a nikah, a Somali celebration, a Catholic Mass, a tea ceremony or a hora each has its own order, its own key moments and its own rules about where cameras may stand. Ask directly:

  • Have you filmed this kind of ceremony before, and can we see it?
  • Who on your team will learn the order of events, and when?
  • Will you coordinate with our officiant, imam or pastor about camera positions?
  • Can you capture both a religious ceremony and a cultural ceremony on the same day, or on separate days?

A videographer who asks you questions back about your traditions is a good sign. One who says "we will figure it out on the day" is not.

Step four: confirm crew, coverage and deliverables in writing

Two couples can pay the same price and get very different films because of what is, and is not, included. Before you compare quotes, put every package on equal terms.

Number of shootersOne person cannot film both partners getting ready in two locations, or cover the vows from two angles
Consecutive coverage hoursA gap between ceremony and reception often means paying overtime
Highlight film lengthA 3 minute teaser and a 7 minute film are different products
Full ceremony and speeches editMany families want every word of the vows and toasts, uncut

On drones, ask whether the pilot holds an FAA Part 107 certificate. Paid drone work requires one, and a licensed pilot will also know your venue's rules before the day.

What should be in a wedding videographer contract

Every contract should name the date, venues, start and end times, crew, deliverables, delivery dates and payment schedule. These are the clauses couples most often skip.

Backup equipment and a backup shooter

The contract should say the videographer brings backup cameras, batteries, memory cards and audio recorders. It should also state what happens if the lead shooter is sick or injured: who replaces them, and with what experience.

Footage ownership and raw files

Under U.S. law, copyright belongs to the author of the work, not the person who owns the physical copy (U.S. Copyright Office), and a transfer of copyright is not valid unless it is in a signed writing (17 U.S.C. 204). In practice, your videographer usually keeps the copyright and grants you a license to use the film personally. That is standard. What you need in writing is the scope: public posting, re-editing by someone else, and whether the studio can use your film in its own marketing.

If raw footage matters to you, get it in the contract with a format, a delivery method and a date. Many studios keep raw files only for a set period after delivery, so download and back up everything the day it arrives.

Music licensing

Popular songs require permission to be paired with video, and that permission is separate from buying the song. Professional studios use licensed music from libraries such as Musicbed or Artlist so your film can be shared online without being muted or blocked. If you want your first-dance song in the film, the clean approach is to keep the audio recorded live in the room during the dance. Ask how the studio handles this, and get the answer in writing.

Cancellation, rescheduling and deposits

Read what happens to your deposit if you cancel, if you change the date, or if the studio cancels. Look for a clear refund schedule, a stated policy for moving the date to one the studio has open, and what the studio owes you if it cannot perform. Events outside anyone's control (severe weather, a venue closure) should be covered by a specific clause, not a vague one.

Overtime, meals and travel

The contract should list the overtime rate, travel fees for locations outside the studio's area, and whether the crew needs a vendor meal and a seat during dinner. Crews eat when guests eat so they are ready for the speeches.

A scorecard for comparing wedding videographers

Fill this in after each consultation. Score each line from 1 (weak) to 5 (excellent), multiply by the weight, and add it up. The weights reflect what is hardest to fix after the wedding. A missed vow cannot be reshot; a slightly different color grade can be lived with.

CriteriaWeightWhat a 5 looks like
Full films you watched3Two complete recent weddings, consistent quality throughout
Audio3Every vow and toast clear, with a backup recorder plan
Low light2Clean, natural reception and evening footage
Experience with your traditions2Has filmed your ceremony type and asked you smart questions
Crew and coverage2Enough shooters for your timeline, hours match your day

The highest possible score is 100. The videographer who wins on this sheet is often not the one with the flashiest trailer, and the sheet gives parents who are paying a way to tie the price to things they can check.

Red flags when hiring a wedding videographer

  • Trailers only. No complete films on request, or full films that are years old.
  • No audio plan. No lavalier microphones, no backup recorders, no answer for the toasts.
  • No delivery date. "It depends on our workload" instead of a written window.
  • No contract, or a one page contract. Anything this important needs the clauses above in writing.
  • Copyrighted pop songs in the portfolio. Your film may be muted or blocked if you post it.
  • A large deposit due immediately with no refund schedule.
  • They talk more than they ask. A good videographer wants to know your timeline, your family and your traditions before quoting.
  • Pressure tactics. "This price is good until Friday" is a sales script, not a reason to book.

When to book a wedding videographer

Zola advises booking your videographer 10 to 12 months before the wedding if possible (Zola). Saturdays in peak season go first. In The Knot's 2026 study, 76% of couples married between May and October, fall remained the most popular season, and The Knot projects October as the most popular month for 2026 weddings (The Knot Real Weddings Study 2026). With an average engagement of 14 months in the same study, most couples have time if they book video soon after the venue.

A simple order that works: venue first, then photographer and videographer together, then everyone else. Our guide on when to book a wedding photographer covers the full vendor sequence.

Choosing a wedding videographer in Nashville

A few local facts affect how you choose and schedule a videographer here. If you are comparing the best wedding videographers in Nashville, ask each one these Middle Tennessee specific questions, and see our Nashville wedding videographer page for how we cover weddings across the region.

  • Peak weekends fill early. The national May to October peak holds here, and big events add pressure. CMA Fest 2027 runs June 10 to 13 at the new Nissan Stadium and across downtown (Visit Music City, WSMV). A downtown wedding that weekend needs a videographer who plans for traffic, parking and hotel availability.
  • Drone rules vary by location. Metro Nashville Parks does not permit drones outside designated flying areas, requires a certificate of insurance for film permits, and may not grant applications with less than 48 hours notice (Metro Parks). Cheekwood requires explicit written permission for drones (Cheekwood guidelines). Radnor Lake prohibits both weddings and drones (Radnor Lake rules).
  • Venue types change the gear list. Historic estates and gardens bring bright afternoon sun and strict rules about where crews may stand. Barns and farm venues outside the city often mean dim interiors and long drives between locations. Downtown hotels and churches bring mixed lighting and indoor audio echo. Ask to see footage from a venue like yours.

Frequently asked questions

How can I choose a reliable wedding videographer?

Watch at least two full wedding films they delivered in the last year, check the audio on the vows and toasts, and read recent reviews. Then confirm in the contract that they bring backup equipment, have a replacement plan if the lead shooter cannot work, and commit to a specific delivery date.

What questions should I ask a wedding videographer?

Ask how many shooters will be there, how they record audio for vows and speeches, what they deliver and by when, whether raw footage is available, how they license music, what happens if they get sick, and whether they have filmed your ceremony type. Our full list of questions to ask a wedding photographer and videographer is organized by topic.

Is it worth it to have a videographer at your wedding?

Yes. Video is the only record of the voices, the vows, the toasts and the way your family moved on the dance floor. The Knot reports that 37% of couples book a videographer, at an average cost of $2,300 (The Knot). Our cost guide breaks down what drives that number.

How much should a wedding videographer be?

The Knot's reported national average is $2,300, and market quotes vary by region, crew size and deliverables. Compare quotes only after lining up the same hours, shooters and deliverables.

What should be in a wedding videographer contract?

Date, venues, hours, crew, deliverables and delivery dates, payment schedule, overtime and travel fees, backup equipment and a replacement shooter, footage ownership and usage rights, raw footage terms, music licensing, and clear cancellation and rescheduling terms. Ask for anything missing to be added before you sign.

How far in advance should I book a wedding videographer?

Zola recommends 10 to 12 months before the wedding when possible (Zola). Book earlier for a Saturday between May and October, a holiday weekend, or a date that overlaps a major event in your city.

Should my photographer and videographer be from the same company?

One coordinated team usually means fewer conflicts over angles, one timeline and one point of contact. Two separate vendors can work well if they communicate before the day. We compare both options in photographer and videographer: do you need both.

Who are the best wedding videographers in Nashville?

The best one for your wedding is the studio that scores highest on full films, audio, experience with your traditions and a clear contract, not the one with the most followers. Use the scorecard above with every Nashville videographer you interview, and ask how they handle permits and drone rules at your venues.
