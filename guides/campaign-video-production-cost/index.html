Campaign video production: what campaign videos and political ads cost to make

Market price ranges from DIY to high end, the videos every campaign needs, realistic turnaround times, and how to brief a production team so you get usable footage.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

A professionally shot launch or bio video for a local race typically runs about $3,000 to $15,000 (a market range), and a TV spot for a House-level campaign runs $10,000 to $40,000 to produce. Statewide hero spots commonly run $25,000 to $100,000 or more, and presidential or major PAC work can pass $200,000. The biggest cost drivers are shoot days, crew size, locations, licensing and how many edited versions you need. The smartest money is spent on one well-planned shoot day that feeds every video the campaign makes for months.

How much does a campaign video cost?

Here are market ranges by tier. The high end comes from published vendor ranges; the lower tiers are our experience-based ranges for professional local work. None of these are Leo Operations rates.

Generic commercial video pricing sits in a similar band. One vendor's 2025 survey of clients and agencies put corporate video at about $1,500 to $7,000 per finished minute, and another guide lists $500 to $2,000 per finished minute for social content. "Per finished minute" is a misleading way to price political work, though. A 30-second spot can take as long to make as a three-minute bio.

People also search "how much does a campaign video cost per day." Day rates are how most crews actually bill. Expect a full shoot day for a small professional team to cost several thousand dollars before editing, with editing billed by the day or by deliverable. Ask for both numbers separately.

What drives the cost of a campaign video

Shoot days

The single largest lever. Every additional day adds crew, gear, travel and the candidate's time, which is the scarcest resource a campaign has. Plan aggressively so a single day covers the bio interview, two or three TV spot setups and a few hours of b-roll.

Crew size

A one-person crew can film an interview well. It cannot also light a living room, run a second camera, record clean sound in a diner and manage a drone. Typical political shoot roles: director or producer, director of photography, second camera, sound, gaffer or grip, and sometimes a makeup artist and a field producer who wrangles locations and supporters.

Locations and permits

Filming at a kitchen table costs nothing. Filming in a hospital, a factory, a school or on a city street can require permission, permits, insurance certificates and a location fee. Public buildings often have rules about political filming. Sort this out a week before, not the morning of.

Drone footage

Aerial shots of the district are popular and effective. In the US, commercial drone pilots must hold an FAA Part 107 remote pilot certificate, and some airspace near airports and government sites needs authorization. Make sure your crew is certified.

Licensing: music, stock and archival

Licensed music for broadcast and paid digital use costs more than a personal-use track. Stock footage and news clips each carry their own license terms, and using a network's news footage in an attack ad without rights is a fast way to get a cease-and-desist letter. Budget a few hundred to a few thousand dollars per spot for licensing, more for well-known songs.

Edits, versions and revisions

This is the hidden cost. A single concept often becomes a 60-second web version, a 30-second TV spot, a 15-second cutdown, a 6-second bumper, a vertical 9:16 version for Reels and Shorts, a Spanish-language version and a captioned silent-autoplay version. Each needs editing, color, sound mix and export. Agree on how many deliverables and how many revision rounds are included before the shoot.

Legal and compliance review

TV and digital ads need correct disclaimers. For federal candidates, the FEC's TV disclaimer rules require the candidate's "I approved this message" statement plus a written disclaimer visible for at least four seconds, and FEC internet rules require video disclaimers visible for at least four seconds. Every factual claim in a contrast ad should be backed by a source document your lawyer has seen.

Who can refuse an ad depends on who is buying. Digital platforms enforce their own political ad policies and can reject or pull ads that break them. Broadcast stations work under different rules for candidates and for everyone else: under Section 315 of the Communications Act, a station that airs a legally qualified candidate's own "use" cannot censor its content, and federal candidates have a reasonable-access right to buy time, while ads from parties, PACs and issue groups are generally accepted or declined under the station's own discretion and standards (FCC political programming). Stations still enforce sponsorship identification and political file paperwork, so a spot missing them comes back for a fix. Have counsel review the specific spot and buy.

How to make a campaign video: turnaround timelines

ProjectRealistic timeline
Vertical social clip from existing footageSame day to 2 days
Rapid response spot using existing b-roll24 to 72 hours
Testimonial video (one subject)1 week
Bio or launch video3 to 5 weeks from first call to final
Set of 2 to 3 TV spots4 to 6 weeks including scripting, shoot, edits and legal review
Statewide ad campaign with testing6 to 10 weeks

Stations need finished spots several days before they air, so work backward from your first air date. A practical rule: if your TV flight starts in mid-September, your shoot should be finished by mid-August.

The usual causes of delay are the candidate's schedule, slow approvals by committee, and late script changes after a shoot. Pick one person with final sign-off.

How to brief a production team

A good brief saves more money than any discount. Send this before the first call:

  1. The race. Office, district, election dates, primary or general, and the electorate you need to win.
  2. The message. Your one-sentence reason for running and the two or three issues you will repeat until Election Day.
  3. Who must be persuaded. The specific voters this video is for. "Everyone" is not an audience.
  4. Deliverables. Every format you need, including TV lengths, vertical versions, Spanish or other languages, and captions.
  5. Distribution. Broadcast, CTV, YouTube, Meta, organic social, email. This decides aspect ratios and length.
  6. Locations and people. Home, workplace, district landmarks, family members, supporters willing to appear on camera, and release forms for each.
  7. Existing assets. Old photos, previous footage, logos, brand colors and fonts.
  8. Budget and dates. A real number and a real air date. A crew that knows your budget can design to it; a crew that does not will either overbuild or underbuild.
  9. Approvals. Who signs off on the script and the final cut, and how fast they respond.

Ask the team for a shot list and a schedule for the shoot day in return. If they cannot produce one, find another team.

B-roll libraries and outside groups

Campaigns increasingly publish raw b-roll of the candidate on a public page of their website (walking the district, talking with voters, at work, with family), and super PACs and allied groups have used that footage in their own ads. Two separate legal questions sit underneath that practice, and neither is answered by the footage being public.

  • Republication. Under 11 CFR 109.23, an outside group that pays to disseminate or republish campaign-prepared material, in whole or in part, is treated as making a contribution to the candidate, unless a listed exception applies (for example, use in an ad opposing that candidate, or a brief quote showing the candidate's position within the group's own message).
  • Coordination. The coordinated-communication rules (11 CFR 109.21) look at conduct such as requests, suggestions and substantial discussion. Using publicly available information can matter under some of those tests, but that is a separate analysis from republication.

The FEC has not settled this. In the 2022 open-B-roll matters (MURs 7666, 7675, 7681 and 7715), the agency's Office of General Counsel recommended finding reason to believe that outside groups made in-kind contributions by republishing candidates' public B-roll. The commissioners deadlocked 3 to 3 and closed the files, with some commissioners writing that limited uses should be allowed absent actual coordination and others writing that the regulation plainly applies (Commissioners Dickerson and Trainor statement, MUR 7715). A deadlock is not approval, and posted "red box" message guidance raises its own coordination questions. Before posting B-roll or guidance publicly, have campaign counsel review the specific material and the proposed use, and never send footage privately to an outside group.

Tips for a b-roll library, which earns its keep in your own ads and social content first:

  • Shoot it on purpose. Schedule two to three hours of the launch shoot just for b-roll in varied settings.
  • Shoot in horizontal 4K so editors can crop to vertical.
  • No dialogue required; ambient sound is fine.
  • Avoid copyrighted logos, music or identifiable people who have not signed releases.
  • Keep clean masters (no music, no titles) so your editors can recut fast.

Political ad agency or freelancer: who should make your videos?

A full-service political ad agency or media firm writes strategy and scripts, produces spots, and often places the media. A freelance videographer shoots and edits what you tell them to. The tradeoff:

  • Freelancers cost less per day and can cover events and social content. Most do not work in FEC disclaimer rules, station delivery specs or persuasion structure every week, and those gaps show up when an ad is rejected or has to be recut.
  • A political video production team brings message discipline, legal awareness and the ability to turn a rapid response spot overnight, and it plans every shoot so one day of footage feeds months of content.
  • Many campaigns combine them: a firm for the bio and TV spots, staff or a freelancer for weekly vertical content.

Whoever you hire, ask to see political work specifically, ask who will actually be on set, and ask who owns the raw footage at the end. You want to own it. Frequently asked questions

How much does a campaign video cost?

A professionally shot bio or launch video for a local race usually runs about $3,000 to $15,000 as a market range, while TV spots for a congressional race commonly cost $10,000 to $40,000 each to produce. Statewide spots often run $25,000 to $100,000 or more.

How much does a campaign video cost per day?

Most crews bill a day rate for shooting plus separate charges for editing. A small professional team for a full day usually costs several thousand dollars before post-production, with larger crews and drone or lighting packages adding more.

How do I make a campaign video on a small budget?

Put the money into one well-planned professional shoot day rather than several thin ones. Choose one strong location, write a tight script under 90 seconds, and have the candidate talk to one person off camera rather than reading to the lens. A good editor will cut that single interview and its b-roll into a bio, several short vertical clips and the raw material for later ads.

How long does it take to produce a campaign ad?

Plan four to six weeks for a set of TV spots, including scripting, the shoot, edits and legal review. Rapid response ads using existing footage can be turned around in 24 to 72 hours.

What should a campaign launch video include?

Why you are running, who you are, and what you will do, in that order. Show the candidate with real people in real places in the district, and end with a clear ask: donate, volunteer or sign up.

Do I need a political ad agency to make campaign videos?

For any race that will run paid video, a political production team pays for itself. It knows disclaimer rules, station and platform specs, and how persuasion ads are structured, and it can turn rapid response spots around in a day or two. Staff can still shoot quick vertical clips week to week inside that system.

Who owns the footage from a campaign video shoot?

Whatever your contract says. Make sure the campaign owns or has unlimited use of the raw footage and final files, so you can recut ads later or post b-roll without going back to the vendor.

Can a super PAC use my campaign's video footage?

Not safely on the assumption that public footage is free to use. Under 11 CFR 109.23, an outside group paying to republish campaign-prepared material is generally treated as making a contribution unless a specific exception applies, and the FEC deadlocked on enforcement in the 2022 open-B-roll cases rather than approving the practice. Coordination is a separate question. Have campaign counsel review any plan to post footage publicly, and the outside group's counsel review any specific use.
