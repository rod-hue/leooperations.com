How to run a voter registration drive, from one table at a festival to a $10 million multistate program

What registration really costs per form, the state rules that trip up organizers, and how the same playbook scales from a church lobby to twelve states.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 13 min read

A voter registration drive is any organized effort to help eligible people fill out and submit voter registration applications. A one-day volunteer drive can cost almost nothing beyond printed materials, but a paid field program is a labor business: one 2024 analysis put the all-in cost at about $45 per completed registration card, up from about $30 in 2020. Before anyone touches a form, check your state's rules, because several states require drive organizers to register, train, or turn in applications within days (Florida allows 10, Texas generally 5) with fines or criminal penalties for missing the deadline.

What a voter registration drive is, and who you are trying to reach

The Census Bureau's survey of the 2024 election found that 73.6% of voting-age citizens were registered. That leaves roughly one in four eligible citizens unregistered, and they are not randomly distributed. They skew young, recently moved, renters, new citizens and lower-income households. A good drive goes where those people already are.

Drives come in four basic shapes, and most serious programs mix them:

  • Site-based tables at campuses, festivals, transit hubs, naturalization ceremonies, grocery stores and congregations.
  • Door-to-door registration in neighborhoods with a high share of unregistered residents, using a list built from the voter file and commercial data. This is the same discipline as a paid canvassing program, with stricter paperwork.
  • Mail programs that send unregistered people a form, sometimes pre-filled with their name and address, plus a return envelope.
  • Digital and text programs that push people to their state's online registration portal.

The single most important distinction is between handing someone a blank form and collecting the completed one. Collecting is what triggers most state regulation. In Florida, for example, the Division of Elections says distributing blank forms or helping someone use the online system does not require 3PVRO registration. Collecting and returning completed forms does.

How much does a voter registration drive cost?

There are two honest ways to measure cost: per completed form, and per net new registrant (people who would not have registered anyway). Funders increasingly ask for the second.

TacticReported costSource and year
Paid field program, per completed cardAbout $30 (2020) and about $45 (2024)NCRP brief citing Florida Immigrant Coalition, 2024
Mailed pre-filled registration form, per new registration$13.20 (2006) and $31.90 (2008)Mann study, summarized by Caltech Election Updates, 2011
Cold text messages to unregistered people, per net registrant$82Vote.org test, 2016
Same text test, per form submitted onlineAbout $6Vote.org test, 2016

Notice the gap in the last two rows. A form submitted is not a person registered. In that Vote.org test, only some submitters registered as a result of the program, which is why the net cost was more than ten times the cost per form.

What drives the price of a field program up or down:

  • Wages. Voter registration canvasser postings commonly list $19 to $24 an hour on ZipRecruiter in 2026, before payroll taxes, supervision and travel.
  • Density of unregistered people. A table outside a community college in August produces several times what a table outside a suburban supermarket produces.
  • Compliance overhead. The same NCRP brief estimates added costs in restrictive states of several thousand dollars per cycle for training requirements and tens of thousands for shorter turn-in deadlines.
  • Scale. Statewide groups spread fixed costs (data, legal review, management) across more forms. Smaller groups carry more overhead per card.

On field programs we run, the ratio we hold to is one field lead for every eight to ten canvassers. Go wider than that and quality control slips, which is where drives get into legal trouble.

State rules: registration, training and turn-in deadlines

State law is the part of this work that sinks good intentions. The Movement Advancement Project counts 24 states that restrict third-party registration drives and 2 that do not allow them. The Brennan Center's 50-state guide groups those rules into official volunteer systems, training programs, registration and reporting, and return deadlines with penalties. Two states show the range.

Florida third-party voter registration organizations (3PVROs)

Under section 97.0575 of Florida law, a group must register with the Division of Elections before collecting applications, and that registration expires at the end of each general election cycle, so you re-register every cycle. Completed applications must reach the county Supervisor of Elections or the Division within 10 days of the applicant completing them, or by book closing if that comes first. According to the Division of Elections' 3PVRO page, fines run $50 per day late (up to $2,500 per application), $100 per day for forms received after book closing (up to $5,000), $500 for each collected application never submitted, higher fines for willful violations, and up to $250,000 per organization per calendar year. The state's citizenship requirement for collectors has been permanently enjoined by a federal court, as the Division notes on that same page. Every applicant must get a receipt.

Texas volunteer deputy registrars (VDRs)

Texas does not register organizations; it appoints individuals. Every person who collects and delivers forms must be appointed a volunteer deputy registrar by the county voter registrar, complete Secretary of State training first, and only accept applications from residents of the county where they are appointed. According to the Fair Elections Center's 2026 Texas guide, VDRs must hand-deliver applications by 5 p.m. on the fifth day after receiving them (with a special rule near the deadline), may not mail them in, and lose their appointment on December 31 of each even-numbered year. Paying canvassers per registration or setting a fixed quota as the sole basis for pay is illegal. A statewide program in Texas means getting appointed in every county you work.

Rules worth checking in every state

  • Does the group or each collector need to register or be appointed?
  • Is training required, and how far in advance is it offered?
  • How many days do you have to turn in forms, and to whom?
  • Can you pay per form? (Many states, including Pennsylvania and Texas, say no.)
  • Are you allowed to copy forms or keep the applicant's data?
  • What is the registration deadline, and does the state offer same-day registration?

Read the statute, then call the county or state election office. Get the answer in writing when you can.

Quality control and fraud prevention

The fastest way to destroy a registration program is a canvasser inventing forms. It happens when pay or job security depends on volume. In the Pennsylvania case reported by Votebeat in 2025, seven people tied to a 2024 drive were charged after canvassers allegedly faked applications. Workers described a quota of about 1.5 to 2 forms per hour and bonuses for extra forms, in a state that bans per-form pay. County officials caught the problems before the election.

A defensible QC system has these pieces:

  1. Pay hourly. Measure productivity, but never tie pay or firing to a form count alone.
  2. Log every form at end of shift: canvasser, site, time, county, and a count that matches the receipt book.
  3. Review every form the same day for completeness, legibility and duplicates.
  4. Call back a meaningful sample of applicants, and every applicant on a form that looks odd, to confirm they filled it out.
  5. Flag patterns: identical handwriting, sequential addresses, the same phone number across forms, impossible volume.
  6. Turn in forms early, well inside the legal window. Holding forms to the deadline is a red flag for regulators and delays voters.
  7. Never alter a form. If something is incomplete, the applicant fixes it, in their presence, or it goes in as is with a note per state rules.
  8. Report suspected fraud to the election office yourself. Self-reporting protects the organization and the voters.

Data, voter file matching and follow-up

A completed form is the start of the relationship, not the end. Capture contact information on a separate, voluntary commit card (never copy the registration form itself, which Texas and Florida both restrict). After the election office processes applications, usually a few weeks later, match your commit cards against the updated voter file to confirm who actually landed on the rolls. That match rate is your true performance number, and it is the number serious funders want.

Matched registrants become your first GOTV universe. Many are first-time voters with no voting habit yet, and even movers and re-registrants who voted reliably at an old address need new polling place and ballot information. Send them their polling place, early voting dates and vote-by-mail options. Our GOTV planning guide covers that hand-off.

Online voter registration

Forty-three states plus Washington, D.C. offer online registration, according to the Movement Advancement Project (2026). It is cheaper for election offices too: a Pew study found online registration in Phoenix cost about 3 cents per transaction versus at least 83 cents for paper.

For drives, online registration changes the job. A canvasser with a tablet can walk an applicant through the state portal with no paper to transport, no turn-in deadline to miss and fewer handwriting errors. The limits: online systems usually require a state driver's license or ID number, which rules out some applicants, and a handful of large states (Texas among them) still lack full online registration. Keep paper forms for everyone the portal cannot serve.

Timing in the cycle

Registration drives work best early. Texas closes registration 30 days before Election Day, and many states fall in a similar window. Plan backwards:

  • 12 to 6 months out: legal review by state, organization registration, data purchase, site agreements, hiring plans.
  • 6 to 3 months out: peak field months. Summer events, campuses in August and September, naturalization ceremonies year-round.
  • National Voter Registration Day (the third Tuesday of September; September 15 in 2026) is a natural peak for site-based work.
  • Final 3 to 4 weeks before the deadline: highest volume, highest compliance risk. Shorten your internal turn-in cycle to daily.
  • After the deadline: shift everyone to confirmation and GOTV.

Off-year registration is one of the smartest investments a program can make. Costs are lower, people are less saturated, and registrants have time to receive their cards before the next big election.

Nonpartisan 501(c)(3) rules

Charities can run registration drives. The IRS says voter registration and get-out-the-vote drives are not prohibited political campaign activity when they are conducted without bias toward any candidate or party. Under Revenue Ruling 2007-41 (summarized in the IRS political campaign FAQ), the IRS weighs all the facts and circumstances, so there are no bright-line safe harbors.

In practice, a 501(c)(3) drive should not target people based on likely party or candidate preference, should not mention candidates or parties in its materials, and should register anyone eligible who asks. Low registration rates, age or geography can support a nonpartisan program when those communities are chosen and served without any candidate or party bias, and the IRS will look at the whole program, not just the selection criteria. Private foundations face stricter rules under section 4945(f) of the tax code. Partisan groups (PACs, parties, 501(c)(4)s within their limits) can target their outreach, but they face the same state collection rules. Targeted outreach does not permit refusing, discarding or delaying a completed application because of the applicant's party preference. Once your team collects a form, it goes to the election office on time, whatever box the applicant checked, and an application handed to a party's drive registers the voter in whatever party the applicant chose, not the party that collected it.

Frequently asked questions

How do you run a voter registration drive?

Check your state's drive rules first, then pick a site or neighborhood with many unregistered people, get current state forms from the election office, train every volunteer on what they may and may not do, give each applicant a receipt if required, and deliver completed forms well before the state's turn-in deadline. Afterward, confirm who got on the rolls and follow up before the election.

How much does it cost to run a voter registration drive?

A volunteer table costs little beyond printed materials and a few hours. Paid programs cost far more: one 2024 analysis put the cost at about $45 per completed registration card, up from about $30 in 2020. Mail and digital tactics can be cheaper per form, but their cost per actual new registrant is often higher than it looks.

Do I need permission or training to hold a voter registration drive?

It depends on the state. Florida requires organizations that collect forms to register with the state each election cycle, and Texas requires every collector to be appointed and trained as a volunteer deputy registrar in each county. Many other states have no requirement. Simply handing out blank forms is usually unregulated.

How long do I have to turn in voter registration forms?

Deadlines vary widely. Florida allows 10 days from the date the applicant completes the form, and Texas VDRs generally must deliver within five days. Late or missing forms can bring fines or criminal penalties. Our advice in every state is to turn forms in within a few days, regardless of the legal maximum.

Can you pay people to register voters?

You can pay canvassers for their time. Many states, including Texas and Pennsylvania, prohibit paying them per completed form or using a quota as the sole basis for pay, because per-form pay invites fabricated applications. You may never pay or give anything of value to a person for registering.

Can a 501(c)(3) nonprofit hold a voter registration drive?

Yes. The IRS treats nonpartisan registration drives as permissible. The drive cannot favor or oppose any candidate or party in who it targets or what it says. Private foundations have additional requirements under section 4945(f).

What is a good voter registration drive for a high school?

A number of states let 16- and 17-year-olds preregister, so a high school drive can reach students before they turn 18. Work with school administrators, check your state's preregistration age and drive rules, and use the online portal where students have a state ID. Keep it strictly nonpartisan.

Are voter registration drives effective?

They add real registrants, especially among young people, movers and new citizens, but the measure that matters is net new registrants who then vote. Programs that confirm registrations against the voter file and follow up with GOTV get far more value from each form than programs that stop at collection.
