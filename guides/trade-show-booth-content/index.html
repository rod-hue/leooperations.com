Trade show booth content: how to leave every show with photos and video that keep selling

A working shot list for exhibitors and organizers, from the empty booth at 7 a.m. to the sponsor recap reel, plus the contractor rules that decide whether your photographer gets on the floor.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

The best trade show booth content comes from a plan made before move-in: clean booth photos before the doors open, busy photos once buyers arrive, product shots, short demo and interview videos, and vertical clips posted while the show is still running. Exhibitors spend more on shows than on any other channel (face-to-face exhibitions take 41 percent of B2B exhibitors' marketing budgets, per CEIR's 2025 Marketing Spend Decision Report), so the photos and video are how that spend keeps working after teardown. Book a photographer or video team who knows exhibit hall rules, register them as your contractor, and hand them a shot list matched to the show schedule.

Why booth content deserves its own budget line

A booth is three days of live marketing. Content is what turns it into a year of marketing. The same CEIR survey of 362 B2B exhibitors found that 75 percent invest in one or more digital channels, and those channels need fresh, real material. A photo of your sales lead walking a buyer through a product at a crowded booth does more for a LinkedIn post, a sales deck or a website case page than any stock image.

Most exhibitors get this wrong in the same way. Someone on the booth team grabs phone shots between conversations, the light is flat, half the frames have a coffee cup in them, and nobody captures the moment the booth was actually packed. Then the marketing team spends the next quarter asking for photos that do not exist.

Clean booth photos before the doors open

The empty booth matters more than people think it does. Get there during the last hour of setup or first thing on day one, before attendees walk in. Shoot straight-on wides, three-quarter angles from each aisle, and tight details of graphics, product displays and lighting. Pull trash cans, boxes and personal bags out of frame first.

Busy booth photos with real buyers

The second set is the one sales teams fight over: the booth full of people. Time it to traffic, not to your schedule. The first hour of the first day and the 30 minutes after a keynote lets out are usually the peaks. A good trade show photographer shoots from slightly above eye level when possible so the crowd reads as depth, and catches staff mid-conversation rather than posing. Get a release process in place for any attendee who will be featured prominently in advertising.

Product shots on the floor

For product shots that will run in ads or a catalog, the photographer should bring portable lighting and shoot before the doors open, when they can move product without interrupting buyers.

Trade show booth video ideas that work

Video is where most exhibitors leave the most value on the table. Here are the formats that earn their keep.

  1. The 60-second demo. Your best product specialist walks through the one thing that makes buyers stop. Shoot it twice: once horizontal for the website and once vertical for social.
  2. Customer interviews. Buyers and partners who already use your product are at the show. Book three or four for 10-minute slots in a quieter corner, and ask simple questions: what problem it solved, what changed, what they would tell a peer.
  3. Executive message. A short welcome or "what we launched this year" from your CEO or product lead, filmed in the booth with the brand behind them.
  4. Booth walk-through. One continuous move through the booth, showing the flow and every station. This is the clip that sells the experience to people who did not attend.
  5. Reaction and atmosphere clips. Hands on product, a buyer laughing, a demo screen lighting up. These are the B-roll that makes every other video work.
  6. Recap reel. Sixty to ninety seconds that compress the whole show into one shareable piece, cut to music and built around your brand and the people who visited.

Audio on a loud show floor

Exhibit halls are loud. Phone audio will pick up the PA system, the booth next door and every forklift beeper during move-in. Interviews need wireless lavalier microphones on the speaker, and the crew should frame them with the booth graphics behind.

Vertical clips posted during the show

The show's hashtag is busiest while the show is open. That is when attendees are scrolling to decide which booths to visit next, and it is the window your content should hit.

Plan a short daily rhythm:

  • Morning: a vertical clip of the booth opening or the product reveal, posted before the floor fills.
  • Midday: a demo snippet or a quick buyer reaction.
  • End of day: a 20 to 30 second wrap of the day, posted that evening.

This requires a crew that edits on site. A same-day social edit is a different job from a polished recap, and it should be scoped that way: one shooter capturing, someone cutting on a laptop, and a marketing contact who can approve and post fast. Tag the show's official accounts and use its hashtag. Organizers often reshare exhibitor content during the event, which puts your booth in front of their full audience.

Sponsor recap content for organizers

Organizers are selling the next show from the floor of this one. The content a sponsorship team needs is specific:

  • Every sponsor logo photographed in context: on banners, stage screens, lanyards, the step-and-repeat and branded lounges.
  • Busy aisles and full rooms that prove traffic.
  • Award ceremonies, keynotes and receptions, with the sponsor's name visible where it was promised.
  • Exhibitor booths that show the variety and quality of the floor.
  • A recap reel built to close sales, with a clear call to sponsor, exhibit or attend next year.

Lead-gen tie-ins: making content pay off after the show

Content and lead capture belong in the same plan.

  • Put a QR code on a booth graphic that goes to a show-specific landing page, and put that page's link in every social post from the show.
  • Photograph the people you scan. If a buyer agrees, a quick photo with your team becomes a personal follow-up email the next week ("Great meeting you at the show") that reads like a real conversation instead of a blast.
  • Send the demo video in follow-up. Buyers who stopped for 90 seconds get the full demo in their inbox two days later.
  • Give buyers a reason to stay. A headshot station in or near your booth holds people for several minutes, and you collect an email to deliver the photo.
  • Turn interviews into sales assets. One customer interview yields a testimonial video, three quote graphics and a case-study paragraph.

Exhibitor-appointed contractor rules for photographers and videographers

This is the part that catches exhibitors. Your outside photographer usually cannot just show up with a camera.

What an EAC is

An exhibitor-appointed contractor (EAC) is any outside company you hire to work in your booth instead of the show's official contractor. Shows define the term broadly. The International Association of Exhibitions and Events' 2024 EAC rules for its show at the Los Angeles Convention Center list installation and dismantling companies, production firms, "photographer, florist or any person or company providing direct services" as contractors who must be registered by a set deadline. That show also required the contractor to carry at least $1,000,000 per occurrence and $2,000,000 aggregate in general liability, with the organizer, the general contractor and the venue named as additional insured.

Requirements vary by show. Shoptalk Fall, held at Music City Center, published contractor insurance terms of $1,000,000 per occurrence and $3,000,000 aggregate, with Music City Center listed as certificate holder and additional insured. Your exhibitor manual is the authority, and the EAC deadline is often weeks before move-in.

Photography policies on the show floor

Many shows restrict photography of other exhibitors' booths. One Informa show's rules, for example, state that exhibitors and attendees are prohibited from taking any photograph or video of the exhibition or other booths without Informa's consent, and unauthorized equipment can be confiscated. Your crew should shoot your own space freely and get permission before any aisle wide that features a neighbor's booth prominently. Organizers hiring their own media team grant that permission show-wide.

The EAC checklist

  1. Find the EAC form and deadline in the exhibitor manual the day you sign for space.
  2. Register your photographer or video company by name, with on-site contacts.
  3. Collect their certificate of insurance with the exact certificate holder and additional insured wording the show requires.
  4. Get them exhibitor or contractor badges, and confirm which entrance contractors use.
  5. Confirm power for chargers and lights is ordered through the official channel.

Nashville is one of the busiest meeting cities in the country. Deana Ivey, CEO of the Nashville Convention & Visitors Corp, said in August 2026 that Nashville is "now the No. 3 meeting destination in North America". The biggest shows land at Music City Center, and its exhibit hall shapes how booth content gets made.

What that means for your crew:

  • Power is exclusive. Music City Center "provides exclusive electrical and utility services", and extension cords must be UL listed. If your video team needs to charge batteries or run lights in the booth, order power through the building's portal.
  • Wi-Fi is free but unmanaged. The building's technology page says an exhibitor that needs a higher level of connectivity should buy a wired service. If you plan to upload same-day edits from the booth, order it.
  • Badges and entrances. Everyone working in the building must be on a daily call list and wear an approved badge, and contractors enter through the Contractor Entrance at 700 Korean Veterans Boulevard, per the Event Planning Guide.
  • No drones inside. The same guide states drones are not allowed inside the building. Overhead wides come from stage platforms, mezzanines or a tall tripod.

Frequently asked questions

How do you get content from a trade show?

Plan it before the show. Write a shot list that covers clean and busy booth photos, product shots, demos, interviews and vertical clips, register your photographer or videographer as an exhibitor-appointed contractor, and book customer interviews in advance. Assign one person on the booth team to coordinate with the crew so content gets captured during peak traffic, not only during slow hours.

What are good trade show booth video ideas?

The most useful formats are a 60-second product demo, customer and partner interviews, a short executive message, a booth walk-through, and a recap reel. Shoot demos and interviews both horizontal and vertical. Add vertical clips posted during the show to catch attendees while they are deciding which booths to visit.

Do I need to register my photographer with the trade show?

Usually, yes. Most shows require outside service providers, including photographers, to register as exhibitor-appointed contractors by a deadline and provide a certificate of insurance. IAEE's 2024 rules, for example, named photographers explicitly. Check your exhibitor manual for the form, the deadline and the insurance wording.

Can I take photos of other booths at a trade show?

Often not without permission. Many shows prohibit photographing or recording other exhibitors' booths without show management's consent, and some will confiscate equipment. Photograph your own booth freely and ask before featuring a neighbor's space.

What should a trade show photographer capture?

Clean booth wides before doors open, busy booth shots with real buyers, product close-ups, staff portraits, demos in action, sponsor signage and aisle wides that show the scale of the show. Organizers also need keynotes, awards and receptions with sponsor branding visible.

When should trade show content be posted?

Post during the show, not only after it. The show hashtag is most active while attendees are on the floor. A morning clip, a midday demo and an evening wrap each day keep your booth visible, and the polished recap reel follows within days.

How do I use trade show photos and video after the event?

Put them in follow-up emails to the buyers you met, on show-specific landing pages, in sales decks and case studies, and across social for months. Customer interviews become testimonial videos and quote graphics. Organizers use the recap reel and sponsor photos to sell next year's booths and sponsorships.
