Political digital advertising: how to plan, target and spend a campaign's digital budget

Sample budgets for $25,000, $100,000 and $1 million, the verification rules on every major platform, and how to know whether your ads are moving voters.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

Political digital advertising means paid ads on social platforms, search, YouTube, streaming TV (CTV/OTT), streaming audio and programmatic display, targeted to voters. Digital is now a major share of political spending: AdImpact projects about $1.6 billion in digital and $2.7 billion in CTV and streaming for the 2026 cycle, out of $11.6 billion total. A practical rule for most down-ballot campaigns is to put 20% to 40% of the paid voter-contact budget into digital and streaming, more if you cannot afford real TV weight. Before spending a dollar, you must get verified on Meta and Google, which can take days to weeks.

How much should a campaign spend on digital ads?

There is no legal or industry standard percentage, so the right number comes from your district, your TV market and your calendar. The spending data sets the benchmarks:

  • In 2024, AdImpact counted $11.1 billion in total political ad spending, with CTV at $2.3 billion (21%) and digital at about $1.58 billion.
  • The Brennan Center counted at least $1.9 billion in 2024 online political ads across Meta, Google, Snap and X, and called it an undercount because platforms are not required to publish complete data.
  • Put CTV and digital together and roughly a third of political ad money in recent cycles has gone to screens other than traditional TV.

Our working guidance from campaigns we have planned: a local race in an expensive TV market (where broadcast reaches far beyond the district) should push digital and CTV toward 50% or more of paid media. A statewide race in affordable markets might sit at 25% to 35%. A ballot measure needing early name recognition often spends more digital money early and shifts to TV late.

The bigger mistake is timing. Most campaigns start digital persuasion too late and then try to cram six weeks of frequency into ten days.

How to run political ads on Facebook and Instagram (Meta rules)

Meta treats anything about candidates, elections, ballot measures or social issues as a special category. Per Meta's policy on ads about social issues, elections or politics:

  • The person running the ads must complete Meta's ad authorization (identity confirmation) for the United States. Third-party guides describe US requirements including a US government ID, a US residential address and two-factor authentication; some campaigns report the full process taking two to three weeks. Start early.
  • Every ad needs a verified "Paid for by" disclaimer naming the responsible entity. Ads missing one get disapproved and still land in the public Ad Library.
  • Ads that use photorealistic AI-generated or digitally altered people, voices or events must be disclosed. Meta says it began adding automated "AI info" labels in June 2026.
  • For the 2026 US midterms, Meta says it will block new political, electoral and social issue ads during the final week of the campaign, as it has in previous US general election cycles. Ads that served at least one impression beforehand can keep running with only budget, bid and schedule edits. Load your closing GOTV ads before the cutoff, and check Meta's announcement for each cycle rather than assuming the same window applies to every local or off-cycle election.
  • Political ads are not allowed in the European Union, which matters for national organizations with overseas audiences.

Meta removed detailed targeting options tied to political beliefs and other sensitive topics in a 2021 policy change, so the strongest targeting today is your own list. Meta lets authorized advertisers upload customer lists as Custom Audiences, which is how most campaigns put voter-file segments to work.

How to run political ads on YouTube and Google

Google calls these "election ads." In the US, that covers ads featuring a current officeholder or candidate for federal, state or territory-level office, a federal, state or territory political party, or a state or territory ballot measure that has qualified for the ballot. Local offices are not in that definition, though Google applies extra rules to state and local election ads in some states. Per Google's political content policy:

  • You must complete US election ads verification; Google says each step can take up to five business days.
  • Google auto-generates a "Paid for by" disclosure for most formats from your verification details; for some formats, including third-party served ads on YouTube and the Display Network, you add it yourself.
  • Election ads can only target geography (not a radius), age, gender and contextual options such as placements, topics and keywords. Google ended voter-record and Customer Match targeting for election ads in 2019.
  • Synthetic or digitally altered content must be disclosed.
  • Some states add rules: Google notes certain ad restrictions in Idaho, Maryland, Nevada and Washington, and registration or notification steps in New Jersey, New York, Virginia and California.

Because you cannot upload a voter file to Google for election ads, YouTube works best for broad reach in tight zip codes, while voter-file precision comes from Meta and CTV vendors.

Disclosure does not make a prohibited paid partnership permissible. Campaign reach on TikTok comes from your own organic video and from supporters who post without being paid.

FEC disclaimers on digital ads

Federal committees and anyone paying for public communications that expressly advocate for federal candidates must follow FEC disclaimer rules. The FEC's internet disclaimer rule, effective March 1, 2023, covers ads placed for a fee on other people's sites, apps and platforms. Video disclaimers must be visible for at least four seconds without the viewer doing anything, and audio ads need the disclaimer in the audio. State and local races follow state law, which varies; NCSL tracks state digital political ad laws.

CTV political ads and targeting with voter files

CTV (connected TV) is streaming video watched on a television: Hulu, Peacock, Roku, Tubi, YouTube on TV and others. It has become the fastest-growing political channel, at $2.3 billion in 2024 per AdImpact.

Why campaigns like it: you can match the voter file to households and aim delivery toward the persuadable or low-propensity households you choose, subject to match quality and available inventory. A matched household is not a guarantee that only the intended voter sees the spot. Vendors have built political data directly into streaming platforms, for example FreeWheel's 2025 political data partnerships.

What to watch:

  • Match rates. Ask what share of your list the vendor actually found. A cheap CPM on a 30% match is not cheap.
  • Frequency caps. CTV is easy to overserve. Ask for household-level frequency reporting.
  • Inventory quality. Know which apps your ads ran in. Premium services cost more per thousand; free ad-supported apps cost less but vary.
  • No candidate discount. The FCC lowest unit charge applies to broadcast and cable, not streaming. See our political TV ad cost guide.

Your voter file also drives the rest of the plan: persuasion audiences for Meta and CTV, low-propensity supporters for GOTV, and exclusion lists so you stop paying to persuade people who already voted early.

How to measure whether digital ads persuade

Clicks are the wrong metric for persuasion. Someone watching a 30-second spot on a TV screen cannot click it, and someone who clicks a donation ad is usually already with you.

Better measures, roughly in order of rigor:

  1. Randomized holdout tests. Split a voter-file audience, show ads to one half only, then survey both halves. The difference is your persuasion effect.
  2. Platform lift studies. Meta and Google offer brand lift style surveys on larger budgets. Useful, though designed for brands, so frame the questions carefully.
  3. Pre-testing creative. Online panel tests before launch tell you which of three spots moves people most. This is the cheapest money you will spend.
  4. Delivery quality. Completed views, on-target reach and frequency against the actual voter list.
  5. Turnout and vote history. After the election, vote history shows who turned out among the voters you targeted. That describes turnout patterns; it does not measure your GOTV impact, because targeted and untargeted voters usually differed in turnout likelihood to begin with. Estimating incremental turnout needs a comparison designed in advance, ideally a randomized holdout of voters who were eligible for the ads but did not receive them. Persuasion needs its own survey-based measurement, as in item 1.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a campaign spend on digital ads?

The working benchmark: most down-ballot campaigns should plan 20% to 40% of paid voter contact for digital and streaming, and more when broadcast TV wastes money outside the district. Across the industry, AdImpact projects about $4.3 billion in digital plus CTV in 2026 out of $11.6 billion.

How do I run political ads on YouTube?

Complete Google's US election ads verification, then build video campaigns in Google Ads targeted by geography, age, gender and context. Google adds the "Paid for by" disclosure automatically on most formats. You cannot upload voter files or use Customer Match for election ads.

How do I run political ads on Facebook?

Complete Meta's ad authorization with ID and two-factor authentication, connect your Page, create a "Paid for by" disclaimer, and mark your ads as about social issues, elections or politics. Upload your voter-file segments as Custom Audiences. Start verification weeks before you need to run ads.

Does Facebook allow political ads?

Yes, in the United States, for authorized advertisers with a verified disclaimer. For the 2026 midterms Meta is blocking new political and issue ads in the final week of the campaign, as in past US cycles, and it does not allow political ads in the European Union.

What is CTV advertising for political campaigns?

CTV political ads are TV-style spots delivered through streaming apps on a television. Campaigns use them to reach cord cutters and to aim delivery toward matched voter-file households (subject to match quality and inventory), which broadcast TV cannot do.

Does TikTok allow political ads?

No. TikTok has banned paid political ads since 2019, and the ban covers paying creators to make branded political content, even with disclosure. Political accounts also lose ad tools. Campaigns can still post compliant organic content and benefit when unpaid supporters share it.

Can I target voters by party on Google?

No. For election ads (federal, state and territory candidates and officeholders, parties, and qualified ballot measures), Google limits targeting to geography, age, gender and contextual signals. Party-based and voter-file targeting are available on Meta through your own uploaded lists and through CTV and programmatic vendors that use political data.

Do digital political ads need a disclaimer?

Yes for federal races under FEC rules, and on every major platform under their own policies. Many states also require disclaimers on digital ads for state and local races.
