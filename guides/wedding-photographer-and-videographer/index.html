Wedding Photographer and Videographer: Do You Need Both, and Should They Be One Team?

What a combined package costs, how to split the budget, and how photo and video crews actually work together on the day.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Most couples who can afford it should hire both, because photos and video preserve different things: photos give you the frames you print, and video keeps the vows, voices and toasts. At The Knot's 2026 averages, a photographer ($3,000) plus a videographer ($2,300) comes to about $5,300, while Zola's current vendor pricing puts the pair closer to $8,400. One coordinated team often costs less than two separate top-tier vendors, delivers a consistent look across photos and film, and is far easier to manage on the day.

Do you need both a photographer and a videographer?

Nearly every couple hires a photographer. Far fewer hire video. The Knot reports that only about 37% of couples book a videographer, and in its 2023 study 19% of couples said skipping one was among their top wedding regrets.

Here is how we would think about it:

  • Hire both if you have a ceremony with spoken vows or readings you will want to hear again, toasts from people who matter, elderly relatives, or a cultural or religious ceremony family abroad will want to watch.
  • Prioritize photography if the budget truly only covers one excellent vendor. Photos are what end up on walls, in albums and on holiday cards, and they are what your parents will ask for.
  • Consider a lighter video option (ceremony and toasts only, or a shorter highlight) rather than skipping video entirely, if the full package does not fit.

The question to ask yourselves is simple: in 20 years, will you want to hear it, or only see it?

One team or two separate vendors?

The choice comes down to whether you want one point of contact and a unified look, or you have already committed to a specific artist in one medium. For most couples who want both, one coordinated team is the cleaner plan.

When one team makes sense

  • You want photo and video and do not have a planner coordinating vendors.
  • You want consistent color and style across the gallery and the film.
  • Your day has a tight timeline, a lot of family formals or multiple ceremonies, where crews that already move together save real minutes.
  • The budget is mid-range and bundling helps it go further.

When two vendors make sense

  • You have already booked a photographer and simply need to add video.
  • You want a very specific film style (for example, documentary or editorial) that a combined team does not offer.
  • You have a planner who will handle coordination between them.

If you go with two vendors, introduce them before the wedding. A 15-minute call between the photographer and videographer prevents most day-of friction.

A note on true "hybrid" shooters

Some vendors sell one person who shoots both photos and video. That can work for an elopement or a very small wedding. For a full wedding, one person cannot be in two places or hold two cameras at the vows. Ask exactly how many people will be working, and who is doing what during the ceremony.

How to split the budget between photo and video

The Knot's 2026 budget breakdown puts photographer and videographer together at about 10% of the average wedding. Zola puts photography at about 9% and video at about 8%, which suggests that couples who hire both often spend 15% or more on the two.

Practical splits we see work well:

  • 55/45 photo to video: the default for most couples. Roughly mirrors The Knot's averages.
  • 65/35: photos are the priority, video is ceremony, toasts and a short highlight.
  • 50/50: you are film people, or you want drone, same-day edit or a longer documentary edit.

On a $34,200 wedding (The Knot's 2026 national average), a 15% allocation is about $5,100, right around the combined national average. On a $20,000 wedding, the same 15% is $3,000, which points toward a combined team, shorter coverage or a lighter video package.

How photographers and videographers work together on the day

This is the part most guides skip, and it is where the difference between a smooth day and a frustrating one shows up.

Audio

Video depends on sound. The videographer typically puts a small lapel microphone on the person officiating and often on the person being married, places a recorder near the lectern or speakers, and takes a feed from the DJ or sound system for toasts. Tell your DJ and venue in advance. Photographers should know where the mics are so they do not stand on cables or block the recorder.

Positioning at the ceremony

A good crew agrees on positions before the processional:

  1. One video camera locked off at the back center for the wide shot.
  2. A second video angle at the front, slightly to the side, on the couple's faces.
  3. The lead photographer moving between the aisle and the front, staying low and out of the video frame.
  4. A second photographer on the opposite side or at the back for wide and guest-reaction shots.

The worst outcome is a photographer standing in the middle of the aisle during the vows. Coordinated teams rehearse this. Separate vendors should talk it through on the pre-wedding call.

Timeline

Photo and video need some of the same minutes: getting ready, first look, portraits, details. Video also needs time for the couple to read letters or say a few words on camera. Build these into one shared timeline. Our wedding photography timeline guide has sample schedules.

A useful rule: add 10 to 15 minutes to couple portraits when video is in the mix. The videographer needs a few moments of movement and the photographer needs still frames, and they cannot always happen at once.

Lighting

Photographers often use flash at receptions. Videographers rely on continuous light. A team that works together agrees on a lighting plan so flash bursts do not ruin the video. With separate vendors, ask the photographer to avoid off-camera flash pointed into the video frame during key moments.

Questions to ask a photo and video team

  1. How many people will be shooting, and who covers what during the ceremony?
  2. Have your photographers and videographers worked together before? How many weddings?
  3. How do you capture audio for vows, readings and toasts?
  4. Can I see one complete gallery and one complete film from the same wedding?
  5. What are the delivery timelines for photos and for each film?
  6. Is the style consistent between photo and video? Who color grades?
  7. What is your plan if someone on the team is sick?
  8. Is drone footage included, and is your pilot FAA Part 107 certified?
  9. If we hire separate vendors, will you coordinate a call with the other team before the wedding?
  10. What do you need from our venue, DJ and planner?

Frequently asked questions

Do you need a photographer and videographer for a wedding?

You need a photographer, and you should plan for video too, because it is the only record of the voices and vows. The Knot reports about 37% of couples hire video, and 19% of couples in its 2023 study named skipping it as a top regret.

How much to spend on a photographer and videographer for a wedding?

At The Knot's 2026 averages, both together cost about $5,300. Zola's current pricing suggests closer to $8,400. As a share of budget, 12% to 18% of your total is a reasonable target for both.

How much to charge for wedding photography and videography?

For couples comparing quotes, combined photo and video packages commonly run $4,500 to $8,000 for six to eight hours of coverage in 2026, with premium teams above $8,000. Prices vary heavily by city, crew size and deliverables.

Is a videographer worth it for a wedding?

For most couples, yes. Photos preserve how the day looked. Video preserves how it sounded, including vows, speeches and the voices of family. If you only have budget for one, prioritize the photographer.

Is it cheaper to hire one company for photo and video?

Often, yes. A single team shares planning, travel and editing systems, which can lower the combined price compared with two separate premium vendors. It also simplifies coordination on the day.

Can one person do both photography and videography at a wedding?

For an elopement or very small wedding, sometimes. For a full wedding, no. One person cannot capture still frames and stable video of the vows at the same moment. Ask exactly how many people will be on site.

What is the 50/30/20 rule for weddings?

It is a loose budgeting idea, not an industry standard. One version puts 50% toward essentials (venue, food, drink), 30% toward the experience (attire, photo, video, music, flowers) and 20% toward everything else plus a buffer. Photo and video live in the 30% bucket. Our who pays for what guide covers it in detail.

How Leo Operations approaches this

Leo Operations is a national field, media and creative firm headquartered in Nashville. Our team has photographed more than 1,000 events and has 56 films on our YouTube channel, so photo and video working side by side is how we operate every day, not a bundle we assembled for weddings.

We cover weddings with photography, 4K videography, or both from one coordinated team. Two camera professionals are on site, and we add a third when the timeline needs it. Coverage runs up to eight consecutive hours, with drone exterior footage when the weather allows. Because the same team plans and shoots both, the photos and film share one timeline, one shot list and one look, and nobody is standing in the other camera's frame during the vows.

Before the wedding, a planning conversation produces a written shot list and timeline that covers audio, camera positions and family formals. Afterward, edited photos arrive within three business days in a curated, color-corrected gallery, and the 5 to 7 minute highlight film, with color grading, audio enhancement and licensed music, arrives within 14 business days.

We are based in Middle Tennessee and travel to weddings in other states. You can see how we work on our wedding page. If you are weighing one team versus two, email us your date, venue and budget and we will tell you honestly what makes sense.
