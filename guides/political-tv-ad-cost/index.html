How much does a political TV ad cost? Production, airtime and how campaigns actually buy TV

The real math behind a political TV buy, from cost per point and the lowest unit charge to what $500,000 and $50 million actually purchase.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

A political TV ad has two separate price tags: making it and airing it. Production for a 30-second spot commonly runs $10,000 to $40,000 for a House-level race and $25,000 to $100,000 or more for a statewide hero spot. Airtime is the bigger bill. One 2026 rate guide puts a single 30-second broadcast spot at roughly $25 to $2,000 in small markets and $15,000 to $75,000 in top-10 prime time, and a serious campaign buys hundreds of those spots per week. In practice, a competitive local TV flight costs $100,000 to $500,000 and a battleground Senate race spends tens of millions.

Production cost vs. airtime: two different budgets

Most first-time candidates ask "how much is a TV ad" and mean one number. There are two.

Production is a one-time cost per spot (or per shoot, if you capture several spots in one day). A production vendor's published ranges put congressional spots at about $10,000 to $40,000 each and Senate or governor hero spots at $25,000 to $100,000 or more, with presidential and major PAC work past $200,000. Treat those as vendor ranges, not a survey. Our full breakdown is in the campaign video production cost guide.

Airtime is what you pay stations, cable systems and streaming platforms to show the spot. It scales with market size, daypart, time of year and how many times you want each voter to see it. A good rule from the field: production should be a small slice of a TV budget, often 5% to 15%. If production is eating a third of your money, you either have too little for airtime or you are overproducing.

How much does a 30-second political ad cost by market size?

TV markets are organized by Nielsen into 210 Designated Market Areas (DMAs), ranked by TV households, with New York at number one. Prices fall steeply as you move down the list. These per-spot ranges come from Adwave's 2026 political ad rate guide; they are one vendor's market ranges, not rate cards, and real prices move week to week.

Two things this table hides. First, local news is the political buyer's home turf because it reaches older, high-turnout voters, so news inventory sells out first. Second, rates climb hard in the final weeks. A companion Adwave guide notes rates can run 200% to 300% above baseline near Election Day for advertisers not protected by the candidate rate, which is why outside groups booking late pay a premium.

Gross rating points (GRPs) explained, with a points-to-dollars example

Professional buyers do not think in spots. They think in points. One rating point equals 1% of the target audience (Ballotpedia uses TV households). GRPs sum the ratings of every placement in a flight, so they count exposures, duplication included, not unique people. They can be expressed as reach percentage times average frequency: if your spots reach 80% of the target audience an average of 5 times, that is 400 GRPs. Always state the audience the points are measured against (TV households, adults 35+, and so on), because the same buy delivers different points against different audiences.

Stations price inventory, and buyers evaluate it, as cost per point (CPP). The formula is simple:

  • Weekly cost = weekly GRPs x cost per point

Here is a worked example. In September 2026, AdImpact data reported by CNN showed Democrats paying an average of $201 per GRP and Republicans about $274 across nine competitive Senate races. Using $201:

Weekly weightApproximate effectCost per week at $201 CPP
500 GRPsLight presence, mostly reach$100,500
1,000 GRPsCommon "real campaign" baseline$201,000
1,500 GRPsHeavy$301,500
2,000 GRPsSaturation$402,000

TV professionals quoted by the Star Tribune described 1,000 weekly points as the floor for saturation and 2,000 as doing it well. Our planning judgment, which matches that: in a contested race where opponents or outside groups are on air heavily, or where the station footprint spills far beyond your district, a campaign that can only afford 300 points a week is usually better off in streaming and digital video, where it can concentrate on persuadable voters. The exception is a low-cost market whose coverage area roughly matches the electorate, in a race where nobody else is saturating the air. There, a short build starting near 300 points can still carry real weight, as in the local example below.

CPP varies widely by market, demo (adults 35+ vs. adults 18+), daypart mix and calendar. Always ask your buyer for CPPs by station and daypart, not just a total.

Lowest unit charge: why candidates pay less than PACs

Federal law gives candidates a discount that nobody else gets. Under Section 315(b) of the Communications Act and FCC rule 47 CFR 73.1942, broadcast stations may charge a legally qualified candidate no more than the station's lowest unit charge for the same class and amount of time:

  • during the 45 days before a primary or primary runoff, and
  • during the 60 days before a general or special election.

Cable systems are covered by a parallel rule, 47 CFR 76.206. A few practical details matter:

  • The discount covers candidate "uses," meaning ads where the candidate appears and is identifiable. Broadcaster guidance notes the discount has traditionally gone only to candidates and their authorized committees, so issue ads from outside groups pay much higher rates.
  • Federal candidates must certify they will include the required "stand by your ad" statement to keep the rate. The FEC's TV disclaimer rules require the candidate's approval statement plus a written disclaimer visible for at least four seconds.
  • Super PACs, party independent expenditures and issue groups pay whatever the market will bear. Reporting by Wisconsin Watch cites estimates that outside groups can pay two to three times more, and older Kantar analysis found gaps of 5x to 20x for the same slot late in a cycle.
  • Streaming and CTV inventory is generally sold at market rates, not the lowest unit charge.

This is why a dollar raised by the candidate committee buys more TV than a dollar raised by an allied PAC. The CNN analysis above found outside groups made up about 90% of Republican ad dollars in those Senate races versus about half for Democrats, which goes a long way toward explaining the $201 vs. $274 gap. For how outside groups work around this, see our super PAC and independent expenditure guide.

Adwave's guide lists streaming CPMs from about $12 to $22 on free ad-supported services like Tubi and Pluto up to $30 to $50 on premium services, with local cable zone buys as low as $25 to $250 per spot. Those are vendor market ranges.

The money is following viewers. AdImpact's 2024 cycle review found broadcast took $5.3 billion and CTV $2.3 billion (21% of the total), making streaming the second-largest category, while cable fell. For 2026, MediaPost reported AdImpact projects broadcast at $5.6 billion (48%) and CTV at $2.7 billion (23%). Our political digital advertising guide covers CTV buying in detail.

How political campaigns buy TV ads, step by step

  1. Set the electorate. Which DMAs, which zones, which voters. A House district often sits inside one expensive market, which is why cable zones and CTV matter.
  2. Pull the political files. Every station posts political ad buys in the FCC's online public inspection file. Your buyer should check what opponents and PACs have booked.
  3. Reserve early. Candidates have reasonable access rights (federal candidates) and the lowest unit charge, but the best news inventory goes to whoever reserves first. The CNN analysis quoted a Democratic super PAC spokesperson saying early booking was much cheaper, and the numbers back that up.
  4. Build the flight. Most campaigns start lighter and build, saving the heaviest weight for the last two to three weeks and early-vote windows.
  5. Traffic the spots. Stations need finished files and traffic instructions several days before air. Missing that deadline is the most common avoidable failure we see.
  6. Verify and adjust. Buyers confirm what actually aired, chase make-goods for missed spots, and shift weight as polling moves.

A media buyer typically earns a commission or fee on placed media. Ask how they are paid before you sign.

At $201 a point, $25 million in broadcast buys roughly 124,000 GRPs across all markets. Spread over 10 weeks and 8 to 10 markets, that is about 1,200 to 1,500 points per market per week, a sustained heavy presence. A plan this size should test creative before spending big, keep several spots in rotation, and hold reserve for the attack ad that lands in week three.

If the market's CPP is $150 (an assumption for illustration), $250,000 buys about 1,650 points, enough for a four-week build of 300, 400, 500 and 450 points. In a cheap market where the station footprint fits the district and the opposition is not saturating the air, that is a credible local flight; against a heavy opposing buy, you would compress it into fewer, heavier weeks. In a top-10 market the same money buys a fraction of that, and a district candidate there should lean on cable zones, CTV and digital.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a 30-second political ad cost?

It depends on the market and time slot. Market ranges for a single broadcast spot run from under $100 in daytime in a small market to $15,000 to $75,000 in top-10 prime time, with live sports higher. A campaign buys many spots, so plan in weekly totals (cost per point times points) rather than per spot.

How much does it cost to run a political ad on TV for a local race?

A credible local broadcast flight in a mid-size market usually runs $100,000 to $300,000 over the last three to four weeks, plus production. Smaller markets cost less. In very expensive markets, local candidates often get more value from cable zones and targeted streaming.

Do candidates really pay less than super PACs for TV ads?

Yes, at broadcast stations and cable systems during the 45 days before a primary and 60 days before a general election. Candidates get the lowest unit charge for their own ads; PACs and issue groups pay market rates, which can be several times higher late in a race.

What is a GRP in political advertising?

A rating point is an exposure equal to 1% of a defined target audience, such as TV households. GRPs sum ratings across all placements and can be expressed as reach percentage times average frequency, so they include duplication: 1,000 GRPs does not mean every voter saw the ad ten times. Around 1,000 GRPs a week is a common planning benchmark for a serious presence in a contested race.

How do political campaigns buy TV ads?

Usually through a media buyer who reserves time with each station and cable system, negotiates rates, traffics finished spots and verifies they aired. Buys are disclosed in the FCC's online public files, so opponents can see what you booked.

Is CTV cheaper than broadcast TV for political ads?

Per impression, broadcast is often cheaper for candidates because of the lowest unit charge. CTV costs more per thousand viewers but lets you target specific households and voter-file segments, so less money is wasted on people who cannot vote for you.

How much is spent on political ads in 2026?

AdImpact projects $11.6 billion for the 2025 to 2026 cycle, with $6.1 billion already spent by late August 2026. That would be the highest ever for a midterm, with $2.7 billion going to CTV and streaming.

How far in advance should a campaign reserve TV time?

As early as you can commit the money. Reservations made in summer for fall flights lock in inventory and generally better rates, especially for outside groups who do not get the candidate rate.
