How to recruit and manage volunteers, the way organizers actually build teams

Where to find volunteers, what to say when you ask, how to cut flake rates, and how to turn one-time helpers into leaders who recruit their own teams.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

The best way to recruit volunteers is to ask specific people, in person or by phone, for a specific shift, and then confirm them twice before the day. Mass emails and social posts find your first few names, but personal asks do the real work: Sister District's 2018 testing found that personally contacting volunteers to confirm raised attendance by at least 51% compared with an email confirmation. Keeping volunteers is a management job, not a recruiting one. Give people real work, a team, a next step up and a thank-you within 24 hours.

Why volunteers matter, and how many people actually volunteer

According to AmeriCorps and the Census Bureau, 75.7 million Americans, or 28.3% of people 16 and older, volunteered formally through an organization between September 2022 and September 2023. Independent Sector values an hour of volunteer time at $36.14 for 2025. A team of 20 volunteers each giving four hours a week for ten weeks is 800 hours, worth roughly $29,000 in replacement labor.

The deeper value is that volunteers are messengers. A neighbor knocking on a door or inviting a friend carries trust that a paid ad cannot buy. That is why serious campaigns invest in volunteer structure even when they also pay canvassers. (For when paid canvassing makes more sense, see canvassing cost and effectiveness.)

Community organizing fundamentals that make recruiting work

Most volunteer programs fail because they treat people as labor to be scheduled. Organizing treats them as leaders to be developed. Three ideas from Harvard's Marshall Ganz, who trained organizers for the 2008 Obama campaign, are worth learning before you make your first ask.

Public narrative: story of self, us and now

Ganz teaches that leaders move people with a story of self, a story of us and a story of now. The story of self is why you personally are doing this, told through a specific moment. The story of us is the values and experience you share with the person in front of you. The story of now is the urgent challenge and the concrete action you are asking for today. A two-minute version of this is the backbone of every good recruitment ask.

One-on-ones

A one-on-one (also called a relational meeting) is a 20 to 30 minute conversation, usually over coffee, where you learn what someone cares about, what they are good at and what they would be willing to do. It is not a pitch. Organizers who do five one-on-ones a week build leadership teams. Organizers who only send emails build lists.

The snowflake model

Ganz and Ruth Wageman developed what became known as the snowflake structure: instead of one captain doing everything, you build teams of five to seven people with clear roles, and each team member builds a team of their own. One organizer coaches three team leads, each of whom coaches a phone bank lead, a canvass lead and a data lead. Leadership multiplies instead of bottlenecking at one tired volunteer coordinator.

What are the 5 steps of community organizing?

In practice, most organizing campaigns move through five steps: build relationships through one-on-ones, identify a shared problem and a winnable goal, recruit and develop leaders into teams, take action with a clear target and deadline, then evaluate and celebrate so people come back for the next fight.

The ladder of engagement

A ladder of engagement is a planned path from low-effort actions to leadership, with each rung only slightly harder than the last. The rule is simple: ask people for the next rung, not the top one. Someone who just signed a petition will not run a phone bank next week, but they will come to one.

RungExample actionYour next ask
1. SupporterSigns up, follows, signs a petitionAttend an event or one shift
2. ParticipantComes to an event or a single phone bankCommit to a recurring weekly shift
3. RegularWeekly canvass, phone or text shiftTake a role: data, greeter, trainer
4. Team memberOwns a role on a 5 to 7 person teamRecruit two friends, run a shift
5. Team leadRuns a team and its weekly meetingCoach a new lead, build a second team
6. LeaderCoaches leads, helps set strategyHelp plan the next campaign

Track every volunteer's rung in your database. When you look at the list on Monday, you should know exactly what you are asking each person to do next.

Where is the best place to recruit volunteers?

The best place to recruit volunteers is your own existing network and the networks of people already helping you. A Pathways to Service report citing Current Population Survey data found that 43.7% of volunteers got involved with their main organization because someone asked them. People rarely raise their hands unprompted.

Places that reliably produce volunteers:

  • Your event sign-in sheets. Every rally, town hall and house party should have a sign-up with a volunteer checkbox. Call those names within 48 hours.
  • Current volunteers' contacts. Ask every regular to bring one person to their next shift.
  • Congregations, unions, fraternities and sororities, and civic clubs. Ask the leader for five minutes at a meeting and a sign-up sheet at the door.
  • Campuses. Student government, political clubs, service-learning offices.
  • Donors. People who gave $25 already believe in you. Ask them for time as well as money.
  • Online platforms. Mobilize, Idealist and VolunteerMatch work for issue groups and nonprofits. They fill shifts, but rarely produce leaders on their own.

What to say when asking for volunteers

A good ask is short, personal and specific. It names a date, a time and a task, and it asks a yes-or-no question. Here is a script that works on the phone or at a door:

  1. Connect. "Hi Maria, this is Darnell. You signed up at the town hall last Tuesday. Do you have two minutes?"
  2. Story of now. "Early voting starts in three weeks, and our precinct had the lowest turnout in the county last time. We are trying to change that."
  3. Specific ask. "Can you join us Saturday from 10 to 1 to knock doors on your own street? We train you on site and you go out with a partner."
  4. Stop talking. Let them answer.
  5. Handle the hesitation. If they say they are not comfortable with doors: "No problem. Could you make calls from home Thursday night instead?" Offer one lower rung.
  6. Lock it in. "Great, I have you down for Saturday at 10. I will text you the address now and call you Friday to confirm."

Never ask "Would you be interested in volunteering sometime?" It invites "maybe," and maybe means no.

Onboarding and training

First shifts make or break retention. A new volunteer who stands around for 30 minutes waiting for a clipboard will not return.

  • Greet every new volunteer by name at the door, and pair them with an experienced partner.
  • For simple roles (greeting, sign-in, phone banking from a script, a canvass with an experienced partner), keep the orientation to 15 minutes, then practice once with a role play.
  • For roles with legal, safety or privacy duties, train to the requirement, not the clock. Voter registration is the clearest case: in Texas, volunteer deputy registrars must be appointed and trained through the county voter registrar before they can accept applications, and other states have their own rules for registration drives, petition circulators and anyone handling voters' personal data. Volunteers who handle money, drive others or work with minors need their own briefings too.
  • Give them a one-page cheat sheet: script, safety numbers, who to call.
  • End with a debrief: what happened, what surprised you, and the next ask (the next rung).

Train leaders more deeply. Team leads should learn how to run a shift, do a one-on-one, make confirmation calls and enter data.

Scheduling, confirmation calls and flake rates

Flaking is the normal state of volunteer scheduling. Sister District's 2021 randomized test measured attendance at a fundraiser rather than a volunteer shift, and about 56% of people who RSVP'd actually attended; people who actively confirmed were significantly more likely to show. That is a useful signal for shifts, not a measured shift rate. Our planning assumption is that roughly half of unconfirmed sign-ups will show up. Start there, track your own show rate shift by shift, and replace the assumption with your own numbers after a few weeks.

How to reduce flake rates:

  • Ask for a specific shift, not general availability. Commitment to a time and place sticks.
  • Confirm twice. Call or text three to five days out, then again the day before. Ask them to reply "YES."
  • Make it personal. Sister District's 2018 research found that personal confirmation contact raised attendance by at least 51% over an email confirmation. Once a good automated reminder is in place, though, its 2021 test found an extra personal text added almost nothing. One real human contact matters; the fifth reminder does not.
  • Give them a buddy. People show up for people, not for clipboards.
  • Over-recruit. If you need ten volunteers, confirm fifteen.
  • Call the no-shows. Not to scold. "We missed you Saturday. Can we get you in for next week?" Many will say yes.

Weekly management rhythm

Volunteer programs run on rhythm. The same meetings, at the same times, every week.

DayActivityWho
MondayReview last week's numbers by team, set this week's goalsOrganizer and team leads
TuesdayRecruitment calls to new sign-ups and lapsed volunteersOrganizer, recruitment lead
WednesdayTeam lead check-ins (one-on-one, 20 minutes each)Organizer
ThursdayFirst round of confirmation calls and texts for weekend shiftsTeam leads
FridaySecond confirmation, prep materials and turfTeam leads, data lead
SaturdayMain shifts, with debrief at the end of eachEveryone
SundayThank-you messages, data entry, call no-showsTeam leads

Volunteer appreciation and retention

People quit volunteering for three reasons: their time was wasted, nobody noticed them, or they never saw what difference it made. Fix all three.

  • Thank people within 24 hours, by name, and tell them what their shift produced: "Your team knocked 312 doors and found 41 new supporters."
  • Celebrate milestones publicly: tenth shift, first time leading a team.
  • Hand out real responsibility. Volunteers who own a role stay longer than those who just fill a slot.
  • Hold a social event that is not a work shift. A potluck builds the "us" in story of us.
  • Ask for feedback and act on it visibly.

Tools: VAN, Mobilize and spreadsheets

Use the simplest tool your team will actually keep updated.

  • NGP VAN (VoteBuilder) or PDI for campaigns that need voter files, turf cutting and canvass data.
  • Mobilize for event and shift sign-ups, automated reminders and attendance tracking. It integrates with VAN.
  • Spreadsheets for small community groups. A shared sheet with name, phone, email, rung, last shift, next ask and notes is enough for 50 volunteers.
  • Peer-to-peer texting tools for confirmations at scale. Follow the platform's consent rules.

Whatever you use, enter data the same day. A volunteer who was called twice by two different people with two different asks is a volunteer you are about to lose.

Safety and code of conduct

Write a one-page code of conduct before your first shift and have every volunteer agree to it. Cover respect for voters and each other, zero tolerance for harassment, how to report a problem and to whom, and what volunteers may not do (for example, speak to the press for the campaign or handle cash without a partner). For field work, send people in pairs, share locations with a shift lead, set check-in times, and tell volunteers they can always walk away from a door that feels unsafe. Run background checks for anyone working with minors. Have one named person responsible for incident reports, and follow up the same day.

Frequently asked questions

Where is the best place to recruit volunteers?

Start with people already connected to you: event attendees, donors, current volunteers' friends and family, congregations, unions and campus groups. A substantial share of volunteers became involved because someone asked them: 43.7% in Current Population Survey data. Online platforms help fill shifts but rarely produce long-term leaders.

What to say when asking for volunteers?

Explain briefly why it matters now, then ask a yes-or-no question for a specific date, time and task. "Can you knock doors with us Saturday from 10 to 1?" works far better than "Would you like to volunteer sometime?" Confirm the commitment before you hang up.

What are the four C's of volunteering?

Several versions circulate. One set, used by the Dallas Sports Commission's volunteer program, is consideration, communication, clarity and comfort: respect people's time, keep them informed, make expectations clear and make them feel welcome. Any version boils down to treating volunteers like valued teammates.

What is the golden rule of volunteering?

Never waste a volunteer's time. If someone shows up and there is nothing ready for them to do, you may never see them again. Have materials, a trainer and a task ready before the first person arrives.

What are the 5 steps of community organizing?

A practical version: build relationships through one-on-ones, identify a shared problem and a winnable goal, develop leaders into teams, take action with a clear target and deadline, and then evaluate and celebrate. The cycle repeats with each campaign.

How do I reduce volunteer no-shows?

Confirm every shift personally three to five days ahead and again the day before, give people a buddy, and confirm about 50% more volunteers than you need until your own shift data tells you the right number. Personal calls outperform automated emails. Call no-shows afterward to reschedule rather than writing them off.

What is the best app for volunteer management?

Campaigns typically use NGP VAN with Mobilize for shift sign-ups and reminders. Small community groups can do well with a shared spreadsheet and a texting tool. The best tool is the one your team updates the same day.
