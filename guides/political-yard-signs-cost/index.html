How much do political yard signs cost, and how many should your campaign order?

2026 market prices by size and quantity, the stake and shipping costs people forget, a sign-count formula that holds up, and the disclaimer rules that draw FEC complaints.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

A standard 18x24 inch corrugated plastic political yard sign costs about $2 to $4 at bulk quantities in 2026 (PoliticalLawnSigns.com). If you order only 5 or 10, expect $10 to $16 each. The wire H-stake that holds it adds roughly $1 to $2.25 per sign. In practice, a small local race ordering 250 to 500 signs spends about $1,000 to $2,850 with stakes, and a countywide or legislative race ordering 1,000 to 1,500 one-color signs spends about $3,600 to $5,350. A common starting count is one sign for every 30 registered voters, adjusted for how many real supporter yards and high-traffic corners you can fill.

The sign itself is the cheap part. A program that moves votes depends on the address list and the delivery crew.

Other published prices line up. My Union Printer prints every sign in full color and lists 18x24 campaign signs from $14.50 each at 10 signs down to $4.00 each at 500. Super Cheap Signs starts political yard signs at $2.35. The Campaign Workshop puts the average at about $2 to $2.50 apiece.

The market range for the sign alone is roughly $2 to $4 at 250 or more, with one-color signs at the low end and full color near the top. Below 100 signs, the per-sign price climbs fast: $4.63 at 50 and $9.68 at 10 in the grid above.

What drives the price

  • Quantity. The biggest lever by far. In the grid above, a one-color sign drops from $9.68 at 10 to $3.09 at 100.
  • Colors. PoliticalLawnSigns.com prices its grid by colors, sides and quantity. One-color signs sit at the bottom of the market range. Full color handles photos and gradients and costs more.
  • Sides. Two-sided printing raises the per-sign price. It is worth it for any sign that faces traffic from both directions, which is most of them.
  • Union printing. Union print shops publish their rates. My Union Printer offers a union bug on every product, and VictoryStore lists one-color 18x24 union label signs from $3.12.
  • Rush and shipping. Next-day production exists (VictoryStore lists next-day 18x24 signs from $4.50), but freight on a pallet of signs and stakes is real money. Get a delivered price.

What does a yard sign cost once you add stakes?

The stake is the line item first-time candidates forget. Corrugated plastic signs have flutes (the little channels inside), and a wire H-stake slides up into them. Published prices:

  • Uline: $85 for a carton of 50 galvanized 10x30 inch H-stakes, which works out to $1.70 each, or $80 a carton when you buy three or more.
  • Super Cheap Signs: standard 10x30 stakes from $1.19 each in bulk, economy 10x24 stakes from $1.09, heavy-duty stakes from $3.40.

Buy the 30 inch stake. The shorter one leaves the sign low, behind shrubs and in the path of mowers. On windy corridors and in rocky ground, heavy-duty stakes earn their price; Super Cheap Signs recommends them for windy or harsh weather and for 24x36 signs.

How many yard signs do I need for my campaign?

There is a top-down rule and a bottom-up method. Use the first to set a budget line early. Use the second to place the order.

The top-down rule of thumb

The most repeated starting point among sign printers and consultants is one sign for every 30 registered voters in the district (Victory Enterprises, YardSignPlus). A council district with 12,000 registered voters would start at 400 signs. Printers also publish planning ranges by race type:

RacePlanning rangeSource
City council, 5,000 to 15,000 voters150 to 300 signsMy Union Printer
Small local race (school board, council district, township)250 to 500 signsUZ Marketing
Larger city or county race750 to 1,500 signsUZ Marketing

Victory Enterprises itself says the one-in-30 figure may run high or low in a given district. In dense neighborhoods, window signs fill in where apartments have no yards.

The bottom-up method we trust more

A sign count should come from places a sign will actually stand. Build it from four numbers:

  1. Confirmed supporter yards. Every yes from your doors, phones, house parties and endorsers who asked for a sign. This is the core of the program.
  2. Earned placements. Supportive business owners, union halls, friendly landlords.
  3. Visibility locations. Corners and road frontage where you have written permission from the property owner.
  4. Reserve. UZ Marketing advises holding 10 to 15 percent back for new corners, late endorsements and damaged signs, and its quantity calculator adds a reserve percentage on top of planned placements.

Order a first batch below your top-down estimate, then reorder once requests are real. UZ Marketing's advice is to stage signs in waves rather than spend the whole inventory in the first week. The price gap between 500 and 1,000 is small in the grid above ($1.91 against $1.89), so splitting an order costs little.

Do political yard signs actually win votes?

Yes, measurably. The best evidence is a set of four randomized field experiments by Donald Green, Jonathan Krasno, Alexander Coppock and co-authors, published in Electoral Studies in 2016. The four experiments covered real campaigns, including a congressional race and a mayoral race, with signs placed in randomly chosen precincts. On average, lawn signs increased the advertised candidate's vote share by 1.7 percentage points, and the effects spilled over into adjacent precincts.

In a close race, 1.7 points can decide the winner.

Signs do their best work inside a field program. A sign request is a recorded supporter, which means a GOTV contact later. Read our get-out-the-vote plan for how that list turns into turnout.

The real cost is labor: plan sign delivery like a field operation

The Campaign Workshop estimates each sign takes about 5 minutes to confirm, 5 minutes to assemble and 10 minutes to place. At 20 minutes a sign, 500 signs is about 167 hours of work and 1,500 signs is about 500 hours. That labor is where sign programs succeed or fail.

Most campaigns start sign delivery too late and run it from the candidate's trunk. A better system:

  • Capture requests in your voter file or CRM, not a spreadsheet on someone's phone. Tag them with address, phone and placement notes.
  • Batch deliveries by turf. Route drops by neighborhood, the same way you cut canvass turf.
  • Pair delivery with a door knock. When the sign goes in, ask the supporter to commit to a vote plan and to name two neighbors. Our guide to canvassing cost and effectiveness covers doors-per-hour math.
  • Run a weekly replacement shift for stolen and blown-over signs.
  • Schedule pickup. Check whether your city or county sign code sets a removal deadline after the election. Assign a crew before election night, not after.

Volunteers can carry it if someone owns scheduling (see our volunteer management guide). Larger races fold sign drops into a paid canvassing operation.

Campaign yard sign rules: disclaimers, placement and HOAs

The "paid for by" disclaimer

The FEC reminded committees in a July 6, 2026 notice that public communications by political committees, including yard signs, must carry a proper "paid for by" disclaimer, while small items like pens and bumper stickers are exempt. Under 11 CFR 110.11, a printed disclaimer must sit inside a printed box set apart from the rest of the sign, in clearly readable type with a reasonable degree of color contrast. Black text on a white background satisfies the contrast rule (FEC).

The box is not a technicality. In 2026, the Republican Party of Arkansas filed an FEC complaint against Democratic congressional candidate Chris Jones because the disclaimer on his yard signs was not enclosed in a box. Proof the art before you print 1,000 copies.

State and local candidates follow state law, which varies. Idaho, for example, requires the name of the person or committee responsible on campaign communications under Idaho Code 67-6614A. Check your state election office's candidate packet.

Where signs can go

Private property is the base of every sign program, and every placement needs the owner's permission. Public property is another matter. Idaho's 2026 county candidate packet, for example, says signs cannot be placed on public road rights-of-way and lets the agency that owns the right-of-way remove them. Polling places have their own electioneering boundaries. Before signs go out, pull your state transportation department's rules, your city or county sign ordinance and your election office's polling-place guidance.

Homeowners associations

Some states limit how far HOAs can restrict political signs. Texas is a clear example: Texas Election Code Section 259.002 (moved there from Property Code Section 202.009 by HB 2554 in 2019) bars a property owners' association from prohibiting signs for a candidate or measure on an owner's property from the 90th day before an election through the 10th day after. Elsewhere, read the HOA covenants before promising a supporter in a gated subdivision that their sign is safe.

Who pays

The campaign pays the printer. Party committees have their own rules: 11 CFR 100.87 lets state and local party committees pay for campaign materials such as yard signs used in volunteer activity on behalf of the party's nominees without the cost counting as a contribution, provided its conditions are met.

How to design campaign signs and when to put them up

A driver passing at road speed gets a glance at your sign. Design for that:

  • The name is the sign. Make the last name the largest element by far. Office sought in smaller type below.
  • Few colors, high contrast. Dark on light or light on dark. Avoid thin fonts and photos on 18x24 signs.
  • A few words beyond the disclaimer. Slogans rarely survive the drive-by.
  • No dates or election year if you might reuse signs in a runoff or the next cycle. "Elect" can also become "Re-elect" with a sticker, but it is cleaner to print just the name and office.

Stake one proof sign at the curb and drive past it before approving the run.

When to order and put up campaign signs

Finalize art once your ballot name is set and the filing deadline has passed. The Campaign Workshop notes that production often takes 5 to 10 days, plus shipping time if your printer is not local. Have the first run in hand before early voting starts, because signs that go up after early voting begins miss the earliest voters. If a local ordinance or an HOA window like Texas's limits how early signs may stand, build your install date from that.

Frequently asked questions

How much do political yard signs cost?

A standard 18x24 corrugated plastic yard sign costs about $2 to $4 each at quantities of 250 or more, based on 2026 published printer prices. Small orders of 10 to 25 signs run roughly $8 to $15 each. Add about $1 to $2.25 per H-stake, plus freight.

What is the average cost of political yard signs?

The Campaign Workshop puts the average at about $2 to $2.50 per sign. Published one-color prices run $1.81 to $2.29 from 250 to 2,500 units, and My Union Printer lists full-color signs at $4.00 each at 500. With a $1.70 Uline stake, plan on roughly $3.50 to $5.70 per sign before labor and freight.

Are political yard signs free?

For supporters, usually yes. Campaigns typically give signs free to anyone who will display one.

Do you have to pay for political yard signs?

The campaign pays the printer, and supporters usually get signs free. Party committees can fund yard signs used in volunteer activity under 11 CFR 100.87, within its conditions.

How many yard signs do I need?

Start with one sign for every 30 registered voters, then check it against confirmed supporter yards, permitted locations and a 10 to 15 percent reserve. Printers publish ranges of 150 to 300 signs for a city council race and 750 to 1,500 for a larger city or county race.

What is the standard campaign yard sign size?

18x24 inches, on 4mm corrugated plastic with a wire H-stake. My Union Printer recommends 24x36 signs for intersections, and campaigns add 4x4 or 4x8 signs for major roads.

What are the campaign yard sign rules?

Federal campaigns need a "paid for by" disclaimer inside a printed box. State and local races follow state disclosure law. Signs on private property need the owner's permission, some states bar signs from public road rights-of-way (Idaho is one), and Texas limits HOA bans from 90 days before an election through 10 days after.
