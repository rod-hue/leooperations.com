Church website design: what every church website needs, builders vs. custom, giving, accessibility and cost

A practical guide for pastors, administrators and communications teams on building a church website that first-time guests can use in seconds and members use every week.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

Good church website design answers a first-time guest's questions in seconds: when are services, where do I park, what should I expect, what is there for my kids, and what does this church believe. Every church website needs service times and address on the home page, a plan-your-visit page, recent sermons, events, online giving, ministries and staff. Church website builders cost about $19 to $199 a month in published 2026 pricing, while professionally designed sites commonly run from about $1,000 for freelance work to $25,000 to $40,000 for fully custom builds at large or multisite churches. The right choice depends on your size, your team and how much original photography and video you can put on the page.

Why your church website matters to guests

Your website is the first visit most guests make. In Lifeway Research's 2023 study of churchgoers who switched congregations, 37% used church websites to research churches before choosing one, more than social media (29%) or online search tools (27%), according to Lifeway Research. For people who had moved to a new area, it was 40%.

The same study asked people introduced through a website what they found helpful:

Website contentFound it helpful
Beliefs and mission67%
Church location66%
Worship times64%
Ministries or events50%
Sermons42%
Contact information39%
Staff profiles33%
Social media contacts26%

That table is a church website design brief in eight lines. Lead with who you are, where you are and when you meet. Then show what happens during the week and what the preaching sounds like.

First impressions happen fast. Our guide on why a professional website matters covers the research showing people judge a web page's visual appeal in about 50 milliseconds, and that design is the factor they cite most when deciding whether to trust a site.

What every church website needs

Service times and location, above the fold

Put service times, the street address and a "Plan your visit" button at the top of the home page on a phone screen. Include every service (Sunday school, worship, midweek Bible study), the time zone if you stream, and a map link.

A plan-your-visit page

This is the most important page for growth. Answer what guests worry about: where to park and which door to use, how long the service runs, what people wear, what the music and preaching are like, how children's check-in works, accessibility (ramps, elevators, hearing assistance, large-print materials), and whether someone will greet them. Add photos of the entrance and the greeters. Some churches add a short form so a guest can let the church know they are coming.

Sermons

Post every sermon with title, scripture, preacher, date and series. Embed video from YouTube or your streaming platform, add audio for people who listen in the car, and consider transcripts. Transcripts help members who are deaf or hard of hearing, and they give search engines the full text of the message. Link to the live stream page with the next service time. Our guide to church live streaming covers the stream itself.

Events

A calendar that is current, with registration for events that need it. Old events left on the home page tell guests nobody is minding the site.

Online giving

A "Give" button in the main navigation on every page, opening a short form that works on a phone. Details below.

Ministries

Children, youth, young adults, men, women, seniors, music, outreach and care ministries, each with a photo, meeting time, leader and a way to connect.

Beliefs, history and staff

A plain-language statement of beliefs and mission, denominational affiliation if you have one, a short history (anniversaries and founding pastors matter to many congregations), and staff and leadership with real photos and a sentence about each person.

Contact and connection

Phone, email, office hours, a contact form, prayer request form, and links to your social accounts.

Church website ideas and examples of good patterns

The strongest church website examples share patterns you can borrow without copying anyone:

  • A real photo, not stock. A wide shot of your congregation in worship or a warm greeting at the door tells guests more than any headline. Stock photos of strangers with raised hands signal a template.
  • A short welcome video. Sixty to ninety seconds of the pastor inviting people in, filmed well, on the home page or plan-your-visit page.
  • A "Next Sunday" strip. Current sermon series art, next service time and a link to watch live.
  • A new here path. One clear route from home page to plan your visit to a form, instead of ten equal buttons.
  • Sermon series pages. Each series gets its artwork, description and all its messages together.
  • Ministry pages with faces. Real leaders and real kids' ministry rooms (with parental permission) build trust with parents.
  • Simple navigation. Five to seven top items, such as New Here, Watch, Events, Ministries, About and Give.

Church website builders vs. custom design

A church website builder gives you templates and hosting for a monthly fee. A custom design is built around your church's identity, content and goals. Published pricing as of October 2026:

Nucleus$65, $99 or $199 a monthChurch website platform; top tier adds giving and media
Ekklesia 360$70, $95 or $190 a month, plus $500 theme setupChurch CMS with sermon and event tools

For professional design, market ranges vary with scope:

  • Freelance web designers: Thumbtack's national cost data shows a typical website developer project at $614 to $1,817, averaging $1,057, per Thumbtack. That level of budget usually means a builder template customized for you.
  • Church-specialist platforms with design help: Ekklesia 360's own cost scenarios put template sites at $500 setup for churches under 500 in attendance, $1,000 to $4,000 setup for churches around 1,000, and most fully custom projects for churches of 2,500 or more at $25,000 to $35,000, with $25,000 to $40,000 for custom multisite builds. Monthly fees run $60 to $295 depending on plan.

Choose a builder when you have a volunteer or staff member who will own the site every week and your needs are standard. Choose professional design when the site has to carry a strong identity, when you have rich content such as sermons with transcripts, anniversary history and ministry video, or when nobody on staff has time to build it right. Either way, own your domain and keep admin logins in the church's name, not a volunteer's.

How do I set up a website for my church?

  1. Gather content first. Service times, address, beliefs statement, history, staff names and photos, ministry descriptions, logo and brand colors. Content delays sink most church website projects.
  2. Secure the domain. Register it in the church's name with auto-renew on, using a church email account.
  3. Choose your platform and giving provider together, so the "Give" button opens your giving form directly.
  4. Get professional photography. A Sunday photo session gives you a home page image, plan-your-visit photos, ministry images and staff headshots.
  5. Build mobile first. Design and test on phones before desktops.
  6. Write for guests. Plain words, no insider acronyms, short paragraphs.
  7. Set up Google Business Profile and analytics.
  8. Test with a real guest. Ask someone who has never attended to find the service time, parking and children's check-in. Watch where they get stuck.
  9. Launch the essentials, then add. Home, plan your visit, sermons, give and contact first.
  10. Assign an owner. One person updates events and sermons every week.

Online giving integration

Online giving is now a core part of church finances. In Ministry Brands' 2026 Protestant Annual Church Giving Report, churches that offer digital giving said an average of 41% of their giving came from digital methods, and 62% of surveyed churches offered giving through their website. Among churches that reported giving increases in 2025, 57% saw digital giving rise compared with 31% for cash and check.

Make giving easy on the site:

  • A "Give" button in the header of every page.
  • A form that opens in one tap, with Apple Pay, Google Pay and bank options.
  • Recurring giving offered by default as an option.
  • A short explanation of funds (tithes, offerings, building fund, missions) and where to mail a check.
  • A giving link beside the live stream player.

Accessibility and mobile

Guests often check service times on a phone from the car on Sunday morning. Google found that as mobile page load time goes from one second to three, the probability of a visitor bouncing increases 32% (Think with Google, 2017 data). Compress photos, limit plugins and test on real phones.

On accessibility, churches are in a different legal position from businesses: the Justice Department's Title III Technical Assistance Manual states that religious entities are exempt from Title III of the ADA, though employment rules and federal funding can bring other obligations. Hospitality is the better reason anyway. Build to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 at level AA: strong color contrast, text that can be enlarged, alt text on images, captions on video, labeled forms and full keyboard navigation. Older members, visitors with low vision and people using screen readers should all be able to find service times and give.

SEO and Google Business Profile for churches

When someone searches "churches near me" or "Baptist church in [your city]," Google's map results usually appear first. Claim and verify your Google Business Profile, choose the most accurate category (Church, Baptist church, AME church and many others exist), and add photos, your website and service details. Google's Business Profile guidelines say businesses with varied schedules, specifically including worship services, should not list regular hours, so put your service schedule in the description and posts, and keep it current on your website.

For search on your own site:

  • Give each page a clear title with your church name and city ("Sunday Worship Times | [Church Name], [City]").
  • Write a real plan-your-visit page with your neighborhood and nearby landmarks.
  • Post sermons with titles, scripture and transcripts.
  • Keep name, address and phone identical everywhere online.
  • Ask members to leave honest Google reviews, and respond to them.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a church website cost?

Church website builders run about $19 to $199 a month in published 2026 pricing. Professional design ranges from roughly $600 to $1,800 for typical freelance projects on Thumbtack to $25,000 to $40,000 for fully custom builds at large or multisite churches, plus monthly hosting.

What is the best church website builder?

The best church website builder is the one your team will update every week and that connects directly to your giving and sermon tools. Church-specific platforms such as Nucleus, Ekklesia 360 and Tithely include sermon and giving features, while general builders like Squarespace offer design flexibility with church content added manually.

How do I set up a website for my church?

Gather your content and photos, register the domain in the church's name, choose a platform and giving provider together, build mobile first, launch with home, plan your visit, sermons, give and contact pages, connect Google Business Profile, then assign one person to update the site weekly.

What should a church website include?

Service times and address on the home page, a plan-your-visit page, sermons, events, online giving, ministries, beliefs, history, staff and contact information. Lifeway Research found beliefs, location and worship times were the most helpful content for people who found their church through its website.

What are good church website ideas?

Use real photos of your congregation, a short welcome video from the pastor, a clear "new here" path, sermon series pages with artwork, ministry pages with real leaders, and simple navigation with five to seven top items.

Do church websites have to be ADA compliant?

The Justice Department states that religious entities are exempt from Title III of the ADA, but federal funding and employment rules can create other obligations. Building to WCAG 2.2 AA is the right standard because it makes your site usable for older members, visitors with disabilities and everyone on a phone.

Should a church use a template or a custom website?

A template works when your needs are standard and someone on staff will maintain it. A custom design makes sense when your church has a strong identity, rich sermon and history content, or needs a site that looks and works like no one else's.
