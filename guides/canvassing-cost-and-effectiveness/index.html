What paid canvassing costs, how many doors per hour to expect, and whether door knocking still works

Market ranges for canvasser pay and per-door costs, the productivity math, the field research on effectiveness, and how to vet a canvassing company before you sign.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

Paid canvassers commonly earn about $18 to $30 an hour in 2026; Indeed's national average for canvassers was $21.88 an hour as of October 2026. Once you add supervision, training, travel, data and vendor overhead, a professional door-knocking program commonly lands around $40 to $75 per canvasser hour, which works out to roughly $3 to $6 per door knocked and $5 to $15 per actual conversation (a market-range planning estimate built from the math below, not a published rate card). Door knocking is the best-tested voter contact tactic there is: in a six-city set of 2001 field experiments, a face-to-face conversation raised turnout among the voters reached by about 7.1 percentage points, or roughly one additional vote for every 14 conversations in that study.

How much do canvassing companies charge?

Canvassing firms rarely publish prices, so the smart move is to understand the cost structure underneath any quote. Firms price in one of four ways:

Pricing modelHow it worksWhat to watch
Per canvasser hourYou pay a fixed rate for each hour a canvasser is in the fieldAsk how "field hours" are defined; training and travel time should be clear
Per door attemptYou pay for each door knocked, answered or notInvites speed over quality; needs strong GPS and call-back checks
Per contact or conversationYou pay only when a canvasser talks to a targeted voterHighest unit price, most aligned with results, most vulnerable to fake contacts
Flat program feeOne price for a defined universe, number of passes and timelineGet the door, contact and shift assumptions in writing

Here is how a per-door estimate is built, so you can sanity-check any quote:

  1. Wage. Political canvasser postings run about $18 to $30 an hour on ZipRecruiter in 2026, with many postings clustered in the low to mid $20s.
  2. Loaded cost. Add payroll taxes, workers' compensation, field leads (often one per 8 to 10 canvassers), training hours, recruiting, travel, food, printed materials, canvassing software, data and the firm's margin. One civic engagement budget guide recommends $8 per person per shift just for supplies, gas and food. In our experience, the loaded cost is usually 1.8 to 2.5 times the wage.
  3. Productivity. That same guide expects paid canvassers to knock 15 doors an hour. Real rates range from about 8 to 30 depending on terrain (see below).

At $22 an hour in wages and a 2x loading, you are paying about $44 per field hour. At 15 doors an hour, that is about $3 per door. In rural turf at 8 doors an hour, the same hour costs $5.50 a door. These are market-range estimates, not anyone's price list, and they move with local wages and timing. Late October rates in a battleground are higher than June rates anywhere.

How much do political canvassers get paid?

The national data points in 2026:

  • Indeed: $21.88 an hour average for canvassers, based on about 6,100 salaries over 36 months, updated October 12, 2026.
  • ZipRecruiter: political canvassing listings in the $18 to $30 an hour range, and voter registration canvasser listings at $19 to $24.
  • Payscale: a broader canvasser average of $18.12 an hour (all canvassing, not only political).
  • Real postings: $25 an hour for political canvassers in Colorado and upstate New York, and $25 base in San Francisco with a bilingual premium, per Landslide Political listings.

Political work pays more than general canvassing because it is seasonal, short-notice and physically demanding. Pay that is too low shows up as turnover and bad data. Hourly pay is also the safer structure. Paying per door or per form creates an incentive to fake work, and several states ban per-form pay for voter registration outright.

How many doors per hour can a canvasser knock?

Turf typeDoors per hour (planning range)
Apartment corridors (with access)25 to 35
Townhomes and dense older grids20 to 28
Standard suburban15 to 20
Large-lot suburban or semi-rural8 to 12
Rural routes5 to 10

Those ranges are planning estimates from CampaignLogix, which also puts contact rates at about a quarter to half of doors, or roughly 5 to 8 conversations an hour in suburban turf. Candidates knock slower because conversations run longer. Canvassing in pairs roughly halves doors per hour.

Three other numbers belong in every plan:

  • Shift length. One budget guide plans 5-hour paid shifts with about 4 hours actually knocking, and 3-hour volunteer shifts with about 2 hours at doors (Crossroads Campaign Solutions guide).
  • People per door. The same guide estimates about 1.2 targeted voters per household, so a 10,000-person universe is closer to 8,200 doors.
  • Repeat passes. Expect to knock the universe two or three times to reach a solid share of it. The guide assumes about 10% attrition per pass as you remove people already contacted.

Timing matters as much as turf. Weekday evenings from about 5:30 until dusk and Saturday late mornings into mid-afternoon produce the most conversations.

Is door-to-door canvassing effective?

For turnout, yes. It has the deepest evidence base of any voter contact tactic.

  • In the original 1998 New Haven experiment, Gerber and Green found turnout was increased substantially by personal canvassing, slightly by direct mail, and not at all by phone calls.
  • Six follow-up experiments in 2001 local elections in six cities found that, among voters canvassers actually reached, turnout rose by about 7.1 percentage points, which works out to roughly one extra vote per 14 contacts in that study. Across everyone assigned to be canvassed, including people never reached, the average effect was 2.1 points, and results varied by city (Raleigh showed no significant effect). The authors' cost estimate assumed $10 an hour wages. Treat these as historical benchmarks from specific low-turnout local elections, not a conversion rate a paid program today is guaranteed to hit; at current wages and full costs, cost per vote is several times higher, and door knocking still compares well with most alternatives.
  • The Chronicle of Philanthropy's 2004 summary of Green and Gerber's research put canvassing at about $19 per vote, against $59 to $200 for direct mail.

Persuasion at the door has to be built differently from turnout work. Kalla and Broockman's review of 49 field experiments found the average persuasive effect of campaign contact in general elections was essentially zero, with exceptions in primaries, ballot measures and early contact that tends to decay. One notable related finding: in a 2016 study of Miami-area voters, a roughly 10-minute "deep canvassing" conversation based on listening and perspective-taking reduced transphobic attitudes and increased support for a nondiscrimination law, with effects lasting at least three months. That study measured attitudes toward transgender people and a policy, not votes for a candidate. Persuasion at the door works when the program is designed for it: longer, listening-based conversations, canvassers trained specifically for them, and contact timed close enough to the vote to hold.

Our view: use canvassing for turnout, ID and ballot chase first. Use it for persuasion where the race is low-information (local races, ballot measures, primaries) and you can afford real conversations.

Volunteer vs. paid canvassing

Volunteer canvassPaid canvass
CostLow cash cost; real cost is organizer timeAbout $40 to $75 per canvasser hour, all-in (estimate)
ReliabilityShift no-show rates are high; volume swings with enthusiasmPredictable shifts and volume you can plan to
Message credibilityVery high; neighbors talking to neighborsHigh with strong training and local hiring
ScaleHard to exceed a few hundred shifts a week without big staffScales with budget and hiring lead time
Data qualityVaries widelyCan be high with QC; can be terrible without it
Best useGOTV, relational work, local racesLarge universes, tight timelines, multiple passes

Most serious programs blend the two. Volunteers carry the most credible conversations and the final-weekend surge. Paid canvassers guarantee the base volume. Most campaigns start paid canvassing too late; by October, the best canvassers are already hired.

How to evaluate a canvassing company

Ask every firm the same questions and get the answers in writing. These are the questions we expect to answer ourselves when a campaign interviews a canvassing company:

  1. What are your doors-per-hour and contact-rate assumptions for my turf, and what did you hit on comparable programs?
  2. How do you pay canvassers? (Hourly is the answer you want.)
  3. What is your canvasser-to-lead ratio in the field?
  4. How do you verify that doors were knocked? GPS, timestamps, call-backs, photo or audio checks?
  5. What happens to a canvasser whose data fails verification, and do I get a credit?
  6. Will I get daily reporting from the canvassing app, not just a weekly summary?
  7. How do you recruit, and how many of your canvassers are local?
  8. How do you handle safety incidents and complaints?
  9. Who owns the data, and when do I get it back in my voter file?
  10. What are the payment terms, and how is the invoice tied to verified work?

Walk away from any firm that cannot show you raw, timestamped door data. Ask for references from campaigns in your state, and call them.

Quality control: GPS, call-backs and ghost knocks

Fake door knocks are the canvassing industry's open secret. In October 2024, reporting first published by The Guardian found that about a quarter of door knocks in Arizona and Nevada for one large PAC's program were flagged as suspicious in a single week, and that some canvassers used GPS-spoofing apps. Even the original Yale experiments threw out walk lists where a canvasser implausibly claimed to have contacted every other house in sequence.

A real QC system includes:

  • GPS and timestamps on every door, checked against the address and against how fast the canvasser is moving.
  • Pattern flags: too many contacts in a row, identical survey answers, impossible doors per hour, knocks logged from one spot.
  • Call-backs to a sample of contacted voters every day, asking whether someone came to the door.
  • Field leads walking turf alongside canvassers and spot-checking doors.
  • Same-day review of each canvasser's numbers, with coaching or removal for anyone who fails checks.
  • Pay tied to hours worked, not to contacts claimed.

A program that knocks 10,000 real doors beats one that reports 15,000 with 4,000 invented.

Safety on the doors

Canvassing is generally safe, but it puts people alone on unfamiliar streets. Basic practices: canvass in daylight or early evening, use buddy systems in unfamiliar turf, require check-ins every hour through the app or a group text, skip any door that feels wrong, never enter a home, carry water and plan for heat, and have a lead on call during every shift. Know the local rules too. The Supreme Court struck down a village ordinance requiring permits for door-to-door advocacy in Watchtower Bible v. Village of Stratton (2002), but canvassers should still respect "no soliciting" signs and trespass laws.

Canvassing tech: VAN, MiniVAN and alternatives

Most Democratic and progressive campaigns use NGP VAN's VAN for lists and MiniVAN as the phone app canvassers carry. Republican campaigns commonly use their party's data platforms and apps, and nonpartisan groups use tools such as Ecanvasser, Qomon and others reviewed in the Commons Library roundup. Whatever the platform, you need walk lists cut by turf, scripts and surveys in the app, offline mode for weak signal areas, GPS logging, and data that syncs back to your voter file nightly.

Hiring and training canvassers

Start recruiting six to eight weeks before you need people in the field. Post on job boards, campuses and community networks; local hires know the streets and the culture. Run a paid training of two to four hours covering the script, the app, safety, legal limits and what to do when a voter is hostile. Put every new canvasser in the field next to an experienced lead for their first shift. Review their numbers that night. Good programs coach quickly and remove people who fake work just as quickly.

Frequently asked questions

How much do canvassing companies charge?

Firms rarely publish rates. Based on 2026 market wages of about $18 to $30 an hour and typical overhead, an all-in paid program commonly runs about $40 to $75 per canvasser hour, or roughly $3 to $6 per door knocked. Get any quote broken down into hours, doors and contacts so you can compare firms.

How much do political canvassers get paid?

Indeed reported a national canvasser average of $21.88 an hour in October 2026. Political canvassing postings on ZipRecruiter commonly list $18 to $30 an hour, and many firms advertise $25. Pay is usually weekly and the work is seasonal.

How many doors can you knock in an hour?

About 15 to 20 in typical suburban neighborhoods, 20 to 35 in dense townhomes or apartments with access, and 5 to 12 in rural areas. Contact rates usually run a quarter to half of doors. Candidates and canvassing pairs knock fewer.

Is door-to-door canvassing effective?

For turnout, yes. In a six-city set of randomized experiments in 2001 local elections, a face-to-face conversation raised turnout among voters reached by about 7.1 percentage points, roughly one extra vote per 14 conversations in that study. Results vary by place and program, so treat that as a historical benchmark rather than a guarantee. On attitudes, a 2016 deep-canvassing study in Miami found longer, listening-based conversations reduced transphobic attitudes for at least three months; that measured views on an issue, not candidate vote choice.

Is door-to-door canvassing safe?

Generally, yes, with sensible precautions: daylight hours, check-ins, buddy systems in unfamiliar areas, never entering homes and a lead on call. Firms should have a written safety and incident policy.

Is door-to-door canvassing illegal?

Political door-to-door canvassing is protected speech, and the Supreme Court struck down a permit requirement for door-to-door advocacy in 2002. Local rules on hours and trespass still apply, and canvassers should respect posted "no soliciting" signs.

Should we use volunteers or paid canvassers?

Most programs need both. Volunteers bring neighbor-to-neighbor credibility, but their volume is hard to predict. Paid canvassers deliver reliable volume for large universes and tight timelines. The common mistake is hiring paid canvassers too late in the cycle.

How do you know a canvassing company is not faking doors?

Require GPS and timestamped data for every door, daily reporting, call-back verification and the right to inspect raw data. Make sure the firm pays canvassers hourly and ties your invoice to verified work.
