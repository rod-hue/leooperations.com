How to host a political fundraiser, from a living-room house party to a host-committee reception

The planning timeline, the ask, the ticket math and the federal rules that hosts and first-time candidates most often get wrong.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 14 min read

A political fundraiser works when three things are true: a host has personally committed to raising a specific dollar amount, the guest list is people who have already been asked to give, and someone stands up during the event and makes a clear, numbered ask. The party itself is the closing mechanism, not the fundraising plan. For federal races in the 2025-2026 cycle, an individual can give a candidate up to $3,500 per election (primary and general count separately), and a volunteer can spend up to $1,000 per candidate, per election on food, drinks and invitations for an event in their home without it counting as a contribution. State and local races have their own limits, often very different, so check your state ethics or elections office before you print anything.

What kind of political fundraiser should you host?

Pick the format based on who you are trying to raise from, not on what sounds fun. Every format below can work at the city council, state legislative or congressional level.

FormatTypical sizeTypical askBest for
House party15 to 40 guests$25 to $250First-time candidates, neighborhood networks, small-dollar donors
Host-committee reception40 to 150 guests$100 to $1,000+ tiersMid-level donors, professional networks
Seated dinner10 to 30 guestsNear or at the legal maxMajor donors, people who expect time with the candidate
Breakfast or coffee10 to 25 guests$50 to $500Busy professionals, weekday mornings
Virtual or call-time "party"Unlimited$10 to $100Out-of-district supporters, low-cost list building

Ask ranges above are common market ranges. Set yours from what your list has actually given.

Small-dollar events vs. major-donor events

Small-dollar events build your base. People who give $25 at a neighbor's house are the people who later knock doors, put a sign in their yard and give again in October. Major-donor events build your budget. Most campaigns need both, but they need different hosts, different invitations and a different ask.

The 80/20 rule and the 3 to 1 rule, explained

Two rules of thumb come up every time someone searches for fundraising advice.

The 80/20 rule says roughly 80% of your money will come from about 20% of your donors. Research backs the general shape. The Chronicle of Philanthropy reported on a Lilly Family School of Philanthropy analysis that found the top fifth of donors gave 84% of individual donations at small charities and up to 95% at large ones, and political giving shows a similar concentration (Campaigns and Elections). The practical lesson: spend most of your planning time on the 10 to 20 people who can give or raise the most, then make sure everyone else has an easy way to give.

The 3 to 1 rule has a few meanings. In nonprofit stewardship it means that after every gift you thank the donor, report results and offer a way to get involved before asking again. In parent-teacher groups it means three non-fundraising programs for every fundraiser. For a campaign, the stewardship version is the useful one. A donor who gets a personal thank-you, an update on what their money did and an invitation to a volunteer shift will give again. A donor who only ever hears "chip in $5" will unsubscribe.

How to build a host committee

A host committee is a group of people who each agree to raise (or give and raise) a set amount and lend their names to the invitation. Their names are the reason guests say yes. A host's commitment is a raise goal, not a personal giving limit: a $5,000 co-chair in a federal race gives no more than the $3,500 per-election maximum personally and collects the rest as lawful contributions from other people, each within their own limit.

  1. Set the event goal first. Example: $25,000.
  2. Divide it into host commitments. Ten hosts at $2,500 each, or a ladder (two co-chairs at $5,000, six hosts at $2,000, four friends at $750).
  3. Give every host a written commitment: their dollar goal, a list of people they plan to ask, and a deadline one week before the event.
  4. Have the finance director or candidate check in with each host weekly. Hosts who hear nothing do nothing.
  5. Print names on the invitation by tier. "Co-Chairs," "Hosts" and "Friends" is a common structure.

The most common failure is recruiting hosts who agree to have their name on the invitation but never commit to a number. A host without a number is a guest.

Ticket levels and RSVP-to-attendance math

Build a ticket ladder with three to five levels. People tend to pick the middle option, so put the amount you most want there.

Example ladder for a state legislative reception: Friend $100, Supporter $250, Host $500, Co-Chair $1,000. For a federal race, the top level might be the $3,500 primary maximum or the $7,000 primary-plus-general amount, with a clear note that general election money is only usable if the candidate reaches the general.

On attendance, plan for attrition. Free political events routinely lose a third to half of their RSVPs, while prepaid events lose far fewer. These are the planning ranges we use:

  • Free or "suggested donation" event: invite 4 to 5 people for every 1 you need in the room, and expect 50% to 65% of "yes" RSVPs to attend.
  • Prepaid ticket event: expect 80% to 90% of ticket holders to attend.
  • Money raised is mostly decided before the doors open. A good host committee has 60% to 75% of the goal committed before the event. The room is for closing the rest.

For more on turnout, see how to get people to show up to your event.

The run-of-show and how to make the ask

A 90-minute evening reception might look like this:

  1. 6:00 Doors open, sign-in table collects name, email, phone and contribution forms.
  2. 6:00 to 6:40 Mingling. The candidate works the room with a staffer who knows who has given and who has not.
  3. 6:40 Host welcome (2 minutes). The host says why they personally support the candidate.
  4. 6:43 Candidate remarks (7 to 10 minutes). One personal story, one reason this race matters, one thing the money will do.
  5. 6:55 The ask (2 minutes). Delivered by the host, a respected supporter, or the candidate.
  6. 7:00 to 7:30 Mingling, contribution collection, photos.
  7. 7:30 Close. Host thanks everyone and names the next step (volunteer shift, next event).

The ask should be specific and tied to something concrete: "We need $8,000 more tonight to fund our first mail piece. If you can give $250, please fill out a card now. If you have already given, thank you, and please ask one friend to match you." Name a number. Say it twice. Have cards and pens in people's hands, plus a QR code to the online contribution page. A 60 to 90 second candidate video played right before the ask does a lot of the emotional work, and it can be reused in the follow-up email. Silence after the ask is fine. Do not rush it.

Contribution limits, in-kind rules and the federal home exemption

These are the federal rules. State and local races follow state law, which can be lower, higher or unlimited depending on where you are. NCSL's 2025-2026 state limits chart is a good starting point, but confirm with your state agency.

  • Individual limit (2025-2026): $3,500 per election to a federal candidate, and the primary and general are separate elections (FEC). PACs that qualify as multicandidate committees can give $5,000 per election.
  • Corporate contributions are banned in federal races. Corporations cannot give money or anything of value to a federal campaign under 52 U.S.C. 30118, and campaigns cannot knowingly accept it. This includes free use of a company's event space, catering or mailing list. Many states also ban or limit corporate giving.
  • Partnerships and LLCs are different. A partnership can contribute within the limits, and the gift is attributed to the contributing partners and counts against each partner's own limit. An LLC that has not elected corporate tax treatment and is not publicly traded is treated as a partnership, and a single-member LLC's gift is attributed to its owner. An LLC taxed as a corporation is treated as a corporation and cannot give. Partnerships and LLCs that hold or are negotiating federal contracts are prohibited, and no share can be attributed to corporate or foreign-national members (FEC partnership and LLC contributions, 11 CFR 110.1(e) and (g)). The entity must tell the campaign in writing how to attribute the gift.
  • Cash is limited. A federal campaign cannot accept more than $100 in currency from one person, and anonymous cash over $50 must be disposed of (11 CFR 110.4).
  • Home use is not a contribution. A volunteer can host in their home, or a church or community room regularly used for noncommercial community purposes, without making a contribution (FEC volunteer activity).
  • Food, drinks and invitations: up to $1,000 per candidate per election per host, spent on an event in their home, church or community room, is exempt. Each person living in the home has their own allowance. Anything above that is an in-kind contribution the campaign must report and that counts against the host's limit (FEC). A cohost who does not live there and pays for food is making a contribution.
  • Restaurants and hotels are not covered by the home exemption. If a host pays for a private room or catering at a commercial venue, it is an in-kind contribution. A venue that gives the campaign a below-market rate on the room or other services can be making a prohibited corporate contribution.
  • The food and beverage vendor discount is the one narrow exception. A vendor may sell food or beverages to a campaign below its normal price, as long as the price is at least the vendor's own cost, and the total discount stays at or under $1,000 per candidate, per election (FEC food and beverage discounts). It covers food and drink only. It does not make a donated venue, free catering or below-cost pricing lawful, and discounts the vendor offers every customer on the same terms are not political discounts at all.

Disclaimers on invitations and solicitations

Federal campaign solicitations, including invitations that ask for money, need a "Paid for by" disclaimer, and committees must include it on mass mailings of more than 500 substantially similar pieces, emails sent to more than 500 people, and public websites (FEC advertising and disclaimers). Printed disclaimers must sit in a box set apart from the rest of the piece. Black 12-point type on white is a safe harbor.

Two more lines belong on most invitations and reply cards:

  • A request for the contributor's name, address, occupation and employer, because federal campaigns must record those details for anyone giving more than $200 in a calendar year (FEC recordkeeping).
  • A statement that contributions are not tax deductible. Federal tax law requires political organizations with normal gross receipts over $100,000 to say so conspicuously in solicitations (26 U.S.C. 6113), and it is good practice for every campaign.

Add a line stating that corporate contributions are not accepted, plus the contribution limit. State rules on disclaimers differ, so check your state's wording requirements.

Compliance and recordkeeping on the night

Put one trusted person in charge of money and nothing else. They collect checks and cards, check that each card has name, address, occupation and employer, and keep a log. For each contribution over $50 the campaign must record the amount, date and contributor's name and address, and keep a copy of the check. Return corporate checks in federal races. Hand everything to the treasurer within 24 hours. For the full spending side, see our political campaign budget guide.

Sample fundraiser budget

A federal-race house reception for 60 guests with a $20,000 goal, held in the host's home. These are market ranges, and costs vary widely by city. This version assumes the host pays for food and drinks and the campaign pays for invitations.

Food and beverages$600 to $1,000Host (within the $1,000 home exemption)
Printed invitations and postage (150)$250 to $450Campaign
Rentals (chairs, high-tops, mic)$150 to $400Campaign
Signage and name tags$75 to $150Campaign

Online processing fees (about 3% to 4% of online gifts)$300 to $600Campaign
Host total$600 to $1,000
Campaign total$1,075 to $2,500

If the host also pays for the invitations, the host's total becomes $850 to $1,450 and the campaign's drops to $825 to $2,050. Anything the host spends above $1,000 is an in-kind contribution: the campaign reports it, and it counts against the host's own $3,500 limit. A second resident of the home has a separate $1,000 allowance, so two spouses can cover up to $2,000 together. The cleanest approach is to agree in writing before the event who pays for what, and have the treasurer log any excess.

A reasonable target is spending no more than 10% to 20% of the goal on the event itself. If a $10,000 event costs $4,000, it was a party, not a fundraiser.

Follow-up and thank-yous

Thank every donor within 48 hours. The candidate should personally call anyone who gave at the top two levels, and hosts should get a handwritten note plus a phone call with their final total. Send all attendees a short email the next day with a photo from the event, the amount raised and one specific thing it will pay for. Then chase pledges. People who said "I will give online tomorrow" need a text with the link the next morning. This is where the 3 to 1 approach pays off: thank, report, invite to volunteer, and only then ask again.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 80/20 rule in fundraising?

It is the observation that about 80% of the money comes from about 20% of donors. Studies of charitable giving find the top fifth of donors often give 84% to 95% of individual dollars, and campaign giving is similarly concentrated. For a fundraiser, it means your top ten prospects deserve personal calls and most of your prep time.

What is the 3 to 1 rule for fundraising?

The most common version says that for every gift, you thank the donor, report on results and offer an engagement opportunity before you ask again. Some PTA and school groups use it differently, meaning three non-fundraising programs for every fundraiser. For campaigns, the stewardship version is the one that raises repeat money.

How do you host a political fundraiser at home?

Pick a date with the campaign, set a dollar goal, invite 4 to 5 times as many people as you want in the room, and personally ask your closest friends to commit before the event. In federal races you can spend up to $1,000 per candidate, per election on food, drinks and invitations at your home without it counting as a contribution. Make sure the campaign provides contribution forms and that someone makes a clear ask.

What type of fundraisers make the most money?

Small seated dinners and host-committee receptions usually raise the most per hour of candidate time because the host committee has already committed most of the money. House parties raise less but build a donor and volunteer base. Galas and golf outings look impressive but often have high costs relative to net money raised.

Is 40 people a lot for a house party?

Forty is a strong turnout for a house party fundraiser and fits most homes for a standing reception. If you need 40 people, plan to invite 150 to 200 and have hosts personally call their invitees. Fewer people who were asked in advance will raise more than a packed room of strangers.

Can a business pay for a political fundraiser?

It depends on how the business is organized. In federal races a corporation, including an LLC taxed as a corporation, cannot contribute money, venue space, catering or other goods and services. A partnership or an LLC taxed as a partnership can contribute within the limits, with the gift attributed to its partners or members, unless it is a federal contractor. A food and beverage vendor may also discount food and drink to no lower than its cost, up to $1,000 per candidate, per election. State rules vary widely, so have the treasurer confirm the specific arrangement before anyone pays.

What is the easiest way to start a fundraiser?

Ask one close supporter to host a small house party with a modest goal, such as $2,500, and have them personally invite 50 to 75 people. Use the campaign's online contribution page so nobody has to handle cash. Make one clear ask, thank everyone within two days, and use what you learn for the next one.
