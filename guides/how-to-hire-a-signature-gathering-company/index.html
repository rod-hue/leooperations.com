How to hire a signature gathering company (and manage it once you do)

A practitioner's guide to choosing a petition firm: what they do, what to ask in the RFP, how to structure the contract, state pay and circulator rules, validation, fraud red flags and the reports you should demand every day.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

A signature gathering company recruits, trains, deploys and manages paid petition circulators, then checks, counts and delivers the signed sheets your measure or candidate needs to qualify. Hire one through a written RFP, pay for valid signatures (or hours, in the nine states that ban per-signature pay), and write daily validation reporting, circulator records and fraud remedies into the contract. Firms are not interchangeable: among the most-used petition companies of 2024, Ballotpedia found average cost per required signature ranged from $4.51 to $32.16, and a single dishonest subcontractor can cost you the ballot.

This guide is about choosing and managing the vendor. For drive budgets, circulator pay trends, validity padding and timelines, read signature gathering for a ballot initiative. For how many signatures each state requires and when they are due, read ballot initiative signature requirements by state.

What does a signature gathering company actually do?

A good petition firm runs a temporary field operation built around one deliverable: valid signatures, on compliant sheets, by the deadline. The work usually includes:

  • Drive planning. Raw signature goal, validity assumptions, county or district distribution targets and a weekly production curve.
  • Recruiting and staffing. Local circulators, crew leaders and, in big drives, traveling crews brought in from other states where the law allows it.
  • Compliance setup. Circulator registration, required training, badges or paid-status disclosures, affidavits and notarization where the state requires them.
  • Field management. Site selection (transit hubs, grocery stores, campuses, events), shift scheduling, turf rotation and supervision.
  • Validation. Checking signatures against the voter file, auditing sheets for technical defects and tracking validity by circulator.
  • Turn-in and custody. Logging every sheet, meeting state turn-in deadlines and delivering organized, numbered petitions to the campaign or to election officials.
  • Reporting and records. Daily counts and validity data, plus the circulator files you will need if opponents sue.

Your political field consulting firm or general consultant should help write the RFP and sit in on interviews, since they inherit the voter contacts the drive produces.

Types of signature gathering firms compared

Ballotpedia counted 44 petition drive management companies working 2024 initiative campaigns. Three of them (Advanced Micro Targeting, Fieldworks and PCI Consultants) each ran six drives, together accounting for 35 percent of 2024 signature drives. Beyond the national names, the market breaks into a few models.

Vendor typeBest fitStrengthsWhat to watch
National petition management firmStatewide measures with high thresholds, tight windowsDeep bench of traveling circulators, scale, experience with challengesHeavy reliance on subcontractors; your drive competes with their other clients for crews
Regional field or canvassing companyStatewide or countywide drives in states with residency rules or per-signature bansLocal hires, hourly management culture, can roll the team into GOTVConfirm petition-specific validation and compliance experience, not just door knocking
Subcontracted crews or brokersSurge capacity late in a driveFast volumeLeast visibility into who is circulating; highest fraud exposure
Hybrid paid and volunteer programMeasures with strong member organizationsLower cost per signature, high validity, builds the campaignNeeds paid staff to manage volunteers and enforce sheet discipline

The most expensive mistake is hiring on headline price alone. A firm quoting a lower rate per raw signature can cost more per valid signature than a firm that validates aggressively and cuts weak circulators early.

How to write a signature gathering RFP

Send the same written request to at least three firms so the bids are comparable. Include:

  1. The measure or candidate, the jurisdiction and the filing deadline.
  2. The legal signature requirement, any distribution requirement (counties or districts) and your target raw total with the validity rate you are assuming.
  3. The circulation window, including any date the petition form must be approved before collection starts.
  4. State circulator rules you already know apply: residency, registration, training, badges, affidavits, pay structure.
  5. Your required reporting format and frequency (daily, by circulator, by county, by validity).
  6. Your validation standard: voter-file match, sample size and who performs it.
  7. Required references from drives in the same state within the last two cycles, with qualification results.
  8. Pricing in a fixed format: per valid signature, hourly plus management fee, or both, with every pass-through cost (travel, lodging, printing, notary, data) listed separately.
  9. The firm's subcontracting policy and the names of any subcontractors.
  10. Insurance, indemnification and the remedy the firm offers for fraudulent or invalid signatures.

Questions to ask a signature gathering company

Use the interview to separate operators from brokers. Ask:

  • How many drives have you run in this state, and did they qualify? Ask for the measure names and check the results yourself.
  • What validity rate did you deliver, measured how? A firm that cannot answer by state and by year is not tracking it.
  • Who will actually be on the street? Your employees, subcontractors or independent crews? Get it in writing.
  • How do you validate, and when? Daily voter-file checks catch problems in days. Weekly spot checks catch them after the damage is done.
  • What happens to a circulator with a low validity rate or a suspicious sheet? You want a documented process: pull, quarantine the sheets, investigate, cut.
  • Are you licensed or registered where this state requires it? In Colorado it is unlawful for a petition entity to pay circulators without a license from the Secretary of State, and a license can be denied to firms found to have violated petition laws elsewhere.
  • How do you handle competing drives? In busy years, crews move to whoever pays the most. Ask how the firm will protect your production when rates spike.

Per-signature vs hourly contracts

There are two separate pay questions: how the campaign pays the firm, and how the firm pays circulators.

Contract modelHow it worksWhere it fits
Per valid signatureCampaign pays only for signatures that pass validationStrongest protection for the campaign where per-signature pay to the firm is lawful
Per raw signatureCampaign pays for every signature turned inAvoid unless paired with validity guarantees and clawbacks
Hourly plus management feeCampaign pays circulator hours, supervision and overheadStandard in states that ban per-signature pay
Fixed fee with milestonesPayments released at signature and validity checkpointsSmaller or local drives with predictable volume

Ballotpedia reports that nine of the 26 initiative and referendum states prohibit paying signature gatherers based on the number of signatures collected: Arizona, Arkansas, Florida, Montana, North Dakota, Oregon, South Dakota, Utah and Wyoming. The other 17 allow it. Oklahoma enacted a ban in 2025 through SB 1027, but on November 10, 2026, the Oklahoma Supreme Court struck down the entire act in McVay v. Lepak, a 5-4 decision that restored the prior law (News 9).

In a ban state, a firm that hints at per-signature bonuses, quotas tied to pay or "production pay" is offering you a legal problem. Florida's 2025 law requires circulators to sign a statement affirming they were not paid based on the number of signatures collected. Hourly programs manage productivity through scheduling, site quality and coaching instead.

Circulator residency, registration and disclosure rules

Before any firm quotes you, both of you need the current circulator rulebook for your state. As of November 2026:

  • Residency. Ballotpedia lists five states with circulator residency requirements: Arkansas, Florida, Idaho, Mississippi and North Dakota. A national firm built on traveling crews has to staff those states locally.
  • Registration. Arizona requires every paid circulator and every nonresident circulator on statewide initiatives and referendums to register with the Secretary of State before circulating, and the state disqualifies all signatures collected by a circulator who fails to register. Florida's HB 1205 requires anyone who physically possesses more than 25 signed petition forms to register, complete training and turn signed petitions in to the voter's county supervisor of elections within 10 days (Ballotpedia News).
  • Badges and paid-status disclosure. Eleven states require badges or petition-form disclosure of whether a circulator is paid. Colorado, for example, requires paid circulators to wear a badge stating "paid circulator" with the employer's name and phone number.
  • Affidavits and training. At least 22 states have witness or affidavit requirements, and Colorado and Oregon require training sessions (Ballotpedia).

Florida's law has been in federal litigation since 2025, and several states rewrite these rules every session. Put a clause in the contract making the firm responsible for compliance with the law in effect on each day of circulation.

Validation and quality control

Validation is where good firms earn their fee. Raw counts are vanity; the only number that matters is valid signatures on sheets that survive a challenge.

What a serious quality control program looks like:

  • Daily voter-file checks of every sheet or a statistically meaningful sample, with results by circulator, crew, site and county.
  • Sheet audits for technical defects: wrong county on a county-specific sheet, missing dates, incomplete addresses, circulator affidavit errors. One defect can void a whole sheet.
  • Duplicate detection across the full database, because duplicates are struck at verification.
  • Circulator scorecards reviewed every day, with a clear cutoff for retraining or removal.
  • Independent checks. On large drives, many campaigns hire a second firm or their own data team to validate a sample of the vendor's work.

Regulators watch these numbers too. Under Florida's 2025 law, county supervisors must refer a petition drive to the Office of Election Crimes and Security when its invalid signature rate in any reporting period exceeds 25 percent.

Fraud red flags and real enforcement cases

Fraud is rarely the campaign's idea. It almost always comes from individual circulators or a subcontractor chasing a bounty, and the campaign pays for it. Two recent cases show how.

Michigan, 2022. Candidates for governor and judge contracted, directly or through consultants, with businesses run by Shawn Wilmoth for nominating signatures. According to the Michigan Attorney General, Wilmoth charged the campaigns nearly $400,000 for valid signatures and delivered tens of thousands of forged ones. Five Republican candidates for governor were kept off the ballot. In March 2026, Wilmoth was sentenced to 4 to 20 years and ordered to pay $376,601 in restitution, and co-defendant Willie Reed received 24 to 240 months and $333,817 in restitution.

Florida, 2024. The sponsor of the state's abortion rights amendment paid a $164,000 settlement to the Department of State after allegations that paid circulators forged signatures and signed petitions for deceased voters. The sponsor neither admitted nor denied violations. The state's report said several paid circulators were charged with felonies in 2024 and three were convicted.

Red flags to act on:

  • A circulator or crew whose production is far above everyone else's.
  • Validity rates that suddenly drop for one person or one site.
  • Many entries on a sheet in similar handwriting or the same ink.
  • Signers who were dead, moved away years ago or do not recall signing.
  • Sheets turned in as a large batch long after the signature dates.
  • A vendor that will not name its subcontractors or share circulator records.
  • Anyone offering signers money or gifts. That is illegal everywhere.

When you see one, quarantine the sheets, have the firm pull the circulator, document everything and talk to counsel before you turn anything in. Submitting sheets you suspect are fraudulent is far worse than falling short of a daily goal.

Reporting and contract terms that protect the campaign

Demand a daily report that shows:

  • Raw signatures collected, by circulator, crew, site and county.
  • Validated signatures and validity rate on the same breakdown.
  • Cumulative progress against the weekly production curve and the distribution targets.
  • Sheets turned in, sheets in custody and any sheets quarantined.
  • Compliance status: registrations filed, trainings completed, badge or disclosure checks.

Contract terms worth fighting for:

  • Payment on valid signatures (where lawful) or validity floors with clawbacks on hourly contracts.
  • Fraud indemnification covering fines, legal fees and replacement signatures.
  • Subcontractor approval in writing before any new crew starts.
  • Records ownership. Circulator rosters, IDs, registrations, training records, affidavits and sheet chain-of-custody logs belong to the campaign and are delivered at turn-in.
  • Right to audit the firm's validation data and to bring in an independent validator.
  • Exclusivity or priority during your window, so your crews are not pulled to a competing drive.
  • Termination for cause if validity falls below an agreed floor for a set number of days.
  • Litigation cooperation, including witnesses and records, through certification and any challenge.

For the money side, Ballotpedia reported in October 2026 that this year's initiative drives averaged $13.51 per required signature, down from a record $14.87 in 2024, while total signature drive spending hit a record $193.4 million. Treat those as market benchmarks, not quotes. Our ballot measure signature gathering guide breaks down budgets and padding.

Frequently asked questions

How much do signature gatherers get paid?

It depends on the state and the competition. In early 2026, California circulators were being paid $12 per signature on one measure and $15 on the opposing measure, up from $4 when collection started, according to data cited by the San Francisco Standard. In the nine states that ban per-signature pay, circulators are paid by the hour.

How much do petitioners make per signature?

Where per-signature pay is legal, rates rise and fall with demand, and competing measures bid against each other for the same crews. The 2026 California rates of $12 to $15 per name were described by a veteran consultant as unprecedented for that early in a cycle. What the campaign pays the firm per signature is higher than what the circulator receives, because the firm also covers management, validation, travel and overhead.

What are petition drive management companies?

They are firms that contract with campaigns to collect signatures for ballot measures and candidate petitions. Ballotpedia tracked 44 such companies working 2024 initiative drives. Most also handle circulator compliance, validation and turn-in.

Is it legal to pay petition circulators per signature?

In 17 of the 26 initiative and referendum states, yes. Arizona, Arkansas, Florida, Montana, North Dakota, Oregon, South Dakota, Utah and Wyoming prohibit it, per Ballotpedia. Oklahoma's 2025 ban was struck down by the state Supreme Court on November 10, 2026.

Where is it legal to gather petition signatures?

Public sidewalks and other traditional public forums are generally open to petitioning, subject to local rules on blocking traffic or entrances. Private property is different: stores and malls can usually restrict petitioners, although some state constitutions give petitioners more access, as California's does under Pruneyard Shopping Center v. Robins. A good firm keeps a vetted site list for your state.

Can a campaign hire more than one signature gathering company?

Yes, and large drives often do, either splitting territory or bringing in a second firm late for surge capacity. Ballotpedia's 2024 data shows several initiatives used multiple petition companies. Assign clear territories, use one validation standard and one reporting format, and make sure every firm meets the same compliance terms.
