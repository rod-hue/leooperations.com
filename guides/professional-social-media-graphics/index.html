Professional social media graphics: why polish matters and how to get it right

A practical guide for campaigns, causes and businesses on brand systems, current image sizes, readable text, alt text, approvals and choosing between Canva and a designer.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

Professional social media graphics matter because most people meet your campaign or business in a feed, on a phone, for about a second, and they judge you by what they see. Polished graphics use a consistent brand system (one logo, two or three colors, two fonts and a set of templates), are sized correctly for each platform (for example 1080 by 1350 pixels for an Instagram portrait post and 1080 by 1920 for Stories, Reels and TikTok), carry short readable text, and pass a proofread before anyone hits post. That consistency is what makes a volunteer's post, an event flyer and a paid ad all look like they came from the same serious organization.

This is one guide in our series on looking clean and professional in public. The pillar guide is why professional photography and video matter.

Why polished social graphics matter

Social media is where most of your audience is. Pew Research Center's 2025 survey found 84% of U.S. adults use YouTube, 71% use Facebook, 50% use Instagram and 37% use TikTok. Those are voters, donors, customers and reporters scrolling past your posts between everything else in their lives.

Research on websites shows how fast people form an impression. In a 2006 study, people rated the visual appeal of web pages after seeing them for just 50 milliseconds, and those ratings matched what they said after looking longer. A feed works the same way, only faster. A blurry photo, a stretched logo or a typo reads as "small and disorganized" before anyone reads a word.

Polish does three specific jobs:

  • Recognition. When every post uses the same colors, fonts and layout, people start recognizing you before they see the name. That matters for a candidate whose name is new to most voters and for a business competing with national brands.
  • Credibility. Clean graphics signal that a real team with real standards is behind the account. Sloppy graphics make people wonder what else is sloppy.
  • Shareability. Supporters and customers share things that make them look good. Nobody reposts a graphic with clip art and three fonts.

Every post does not need a designer. Every post needs the same system.

Build a simple brand system first

A brand system is a short set of rules plus ready-made files. It is the most useful thing a campaign or small business can build, because it lets many people produce consistent work.

What goes in it

  • Logo files. Full color, one-color white, one-color dark, and a square version for profile pictures. PNG with transparent background and SVG.
  • Two or three colors. One primary, one accent, and a neutral. Write down the exact hex codes. Campaigns often default to red, white and blue; you can still pick specific shades that are yours.
  • Two fonts. One bold font for headlines, one clean font for body text. Both should be legible at small sizes on a phone.
  • Photo style. Real, current, well-lit photos of the candidate or team with real people. Our pillar guide explains why professional photography carries so much weight.
  • Templates. Quote card, event announcement, endorsement card, issue graphic, thank-you post, story frame, and a video caption style. Each one built in the right sizes.
  • A one-page rules sheet. Minimum text size, where the logo goes, what never to do (stretching the logo, adding drop shadows, using off-brand colors).

Accessible color and contrast

Pick colors that pass contrast checks. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines call for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 for normal-size text and 3 to 1 for large text. Low-contrast text was among the most common accessibility failures on the web in the WebAIM Million 2025 report. Light gray on white and red on blue look fine on a designer's monitor and disappear on a phone in sunlight.

Readability on phones

Most graphics fail because they try to say too much. A graphic is a headline, not a press release.

  • Use one message per graphic. A quote, a date, a number or a call to action. Put the rest in the caption.
  • Keep the text short. As a working rule, if it takes more than a few seconds to read, cut it.
  • Make the text big. Preview it on an actual phone at actual size before posting. If you squint, it is too small.
  • Use high contrast. Dark text on light, or light text on dark, with a solid band or overlay behind text placed over photos.
  • Respect safe zones. Platform buttons, captions and profile names cover the edges of vertical formats. Keep critical text in the middle.
  • Do not rely on the image alone. Put the key information (date, time, address, link) in the caption too, so it is searchable and readable by screen readers.

Alt text, captions and accessibility

Alt text is a short written description of an image that screen readers read aloud. Instagram, Facebook, X and LinkedIn all let you add it. The WebAIM Million 2025 report found missing alt text among the most common accessibility errors on web pages, and the same habit shows up in social feeds.

Good alt text says what the image shows and any text it contains: "Graphic: Maria Lopez for City Council. Early voting starts October 14. Find your polling place at the link in bio." Skip "image of" and keep it to a sentence or two.

For video, burn in captions. A 2019 survey of 5,616 U.S. adults by Verizon Media and Publicis Media found 69% watch video with the sound off in public places, and 80% of respondents said they were more likely to finish a video when captions are available.

The graphics every campaign and business needs

Quote cards

A strong photo, a short quote of 20 words or fewer, the speaker's name and title, and your logo. Use them for candidate lines from debates, customer testimonials and community voices. Get permission for any quote that is not your own, and quote people exactly. Our testimonials and reviews guide covers consent.

Event flyers

Build two versions: a full flyer (4:5) with every detail, and a story version (9:16) with the name, date, time and one line. Check that the date and day of week match. That mistake is common, and the correction post always looks worse.

Endorsement graphics

Endorsements are only persuasive when they look official. Use the endorser's correct name, title and logo (with permission), a good photo, and the same template for every endorsement so the series builds. Have the endorsing person or organization approve the final graphic before it goes out.

Issue and service graphics

One issue or service, one plain-language line, one supporting fact with a source in the caption. For campaigns, these are the graphics opponents screenshot, so be accurate.

Thank-you and recap posts

After every event, post a short photo set within 24 hours. Professional photos of a full room do more for momentum than any announcement.

Disclaimers on paid graphics

If you boost or run a graphic as a paid political ad, it needs a disclaimer. The FEC's advertising and disclaimer rules say text and graphic internet ads need a written disclaimer viewable without the user taking any action, with text at least as large as most other text in the ad, with an adapted version allowed when space is tight. Meta and Google add their own "paid for by" requirements on top. State and local races follow state law. See our political digital advertising guide for the full picture.

Keeping volunteers and staff consistent

The fastest way to look disorganized is for ten people to make ten different-looking graphics. Fix that with access, not rules alone.

  • Shared template library. Put locked templates in a shared Canva team, Adobe Express account or design folder. People edit the words and swap the photo; colors, fonts and logo stay locked.
  • Photo library. A shared, organized folder of approved, professional photos with names in the file names. Bad photos usually appear because good ones were not easy to find.
  • A short how-to. One page with screenshots.
  • Ready-to-share kits. For big moments (launch, endorsement, event, election day), post a folder with finished graphics in every size and suggested captions. Supporters share more when it takes ten seconds.

An approval workflow so nothing goes out with a typo

Every organization eventually posts a misspelled name or wrong date. A simple workflow makes it rare.

  1. One person owns the calendar. They know what is going out and when.
  2. Creator builds from a template and writes the caption and alt text.
  3. A second person proofreads the graphic and caption on a phone, not a laptop. They check names, dates, times, addresses, links and the disclaimer.
  4. Sensitive items get a third look. Anything about an opponent, a policy claim, an endorsement or a crisis goes to the campaign manager, owner or communications lead.
  5. Schedule, then check it live. Look at the post on the actual platform after it publishes. Crops and link previews sometimes change.

Canva or a designer: when DIY is fine

Canva and Adobe Express are good tools, and plenty of professional teams use them daily. The problem is starting from a blank page or a random template every time.

DIY works well for:

  • Daily posts built from professionally designed templates.
  • Event reminders, thank-yous and quick reactions.
  • Very small races and new businesses with no budget yet, as long as someone keeps the brand rules.

Bring in a designer for:

  • The brand system itself: logo, colors, fonts and master templates.
  • Launch materials, major announcements and anything that becomes a paid ad.
  • Endorsement series, ballot measure explainers and data graphics where accuracy and clarity matter.
  • Anything going on a billboard, mailer or yard sign alongside the digital version, so print and social match.

A sensible split: pay a professional to build the system and hero assets, then let trained staff or volunteers produce daily posts from templates, with a proofreader on every one.

Frequently asked questions

What size should social media graphics be?

For most uses, design at 1080 by 1350 pixels (4:5) for Instagram and Facebook feeds, 1080 by 1920 (9:16) for Stories, Reels and TikTok, 1080 by 1080 for square posts and LinkedIn, and 1600 by 900 (16:9) for X and website use. YouTube thumbnails are 16:9, commonly 1280 by 720. Platforms change specs, so check a current guide before a big push.

What makes a good social media graphic?

One clear message, big readable text, strong contrast, a real photo and consistent brand colors, fonts and logo. Details go in the caption.

What should be in a brand kit for social media?

Logo files in several versions, two or three exact colors, two fonts, a photo style, templates in each size and a one-page rules sheet.

Is Canva good enough for professional graphics?

Yes, for day-to-day posts, when you start from a well-designed brand template and someone proofreads every post. It is weaker as a substitute for a real brand system or for launch, ad and print materials, where a designer usually pays for itself.

Do I need alt text on social media posts?

You should add it. Alt text lets people using screen readers understand the image, and it is especially important when the graphic contains text such as a date or address. Most major platforms have an alt text field in the posting options.

Do political graphics need a paid-for-by disclaimer?

Paid political ads do. Under FEC rules, text and graphic internet ads need a written disclaimer viewable without any action by the user, with an adapted format allowed when space is limited. Platforms like Meta and Google have their own requirements, and state and local races follow state law.

How do I keep volunteers' graphics on brand?

Give them locked templates, approved photos, a short how-to and share kits for big moments, and require a second-person proofread on official accounts.
