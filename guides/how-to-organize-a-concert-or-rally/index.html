How to organize a concert or rally, from the permit application to the last song

A practical guide for benefit concerts, campaign rallies and kickoff events: venue, permits, insurance, sound, security, music licensing, campaign finance rules and a realistic budget.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 13 min read

Organizing a concert or rally comes down to five jobs done in order: lock a venue that fits your realistic crowd, file permits and buy insurance early, build a stage and sound plan the space can support, staff security and volunteers, and promote hard in the final two weeks. A small outdoor rally or benefit concert for 300 to 1,500 people usually costs $5,000 to $40,000 in market rates once you add stage, sound, permits, insurance, security and restrooms, and most cities want permit applications 30 to 90 days out. Campaigns add one more layer: every ticket dollar, donated performance and printed flyer has a campaign finance rule attached.

How are concerts organized? The basic order of operations

Well-run events follow the same sequence, even when the organizers never write it down.

  1. Set the goal and the number. Is this a fundraiser, a show of strength for the press, a volunteer recruitment night or a thank-you party? The goal decides the venue size, the ticket price and if you need a stage at all.
  2. Pick a date and a venue together. Check the city's event calendar, school schedules, big games and religious holidays before you sign anything.
  3. File permits and buy insurance. These have the longest lead times and the least flexibility.
  4. Book talent and production. Performers, sound, lighting, stage, power, tents.
  5. Build the staffing plan. Security, crowd managers, volunteers, a stage manager, a medic.
  6. Promote. Most attendance decisions happen late. Eventbrite's 2024 music data found that 57% of tickets were sold a week or less before the show.
  7. Write the run of show and walk the site. Then run it, and debrief within 48 hours.

The most common failure is promoting a date before the permit is approved. If the city moves you, every flyer is wrong.

Choosing a venue and sizing it to your real crowd

Size the venue to the crowd you can actually produce, not the one you hope for. A half-empty park photographs badly and reads as weakness in a news story. A packed room of 250 looks like momentum. Use the turnout math in our guide on how to get people to show up to your event before you pick a space.

What to check on every site visit:

  • Legal capacity. Indoor rooms have a posted occupant load set by the fire marshal. Outdoor sites get a capacity in the permit.
  • Power. Where is it, how many circuits, and will you need a generator? A stage with a real PA and lights will trip a single 20-amp outlet.
  • Sightlines and backdrop. For a rally, the backdrop is the photograph. Put the crowd behind the speaker, or a recognizable landmark, not a parking garage.
  • Restrooms, shade and water. The Portable Sanitation Association's planning chart lists 12 units for 2,000 people over four hours, with 10 to 20% more if alcohol is served.

Permits: special event, sound and street closure

Permit rules are local, and the deadlines are earlier than most first-time organizers expect. A sampling of 2025 and 2026 city rules shows the range:

  • New York City's permit office lists 10 days for press conferences and rallies, 60 days for block parties, and 14 to 45 days for street events depending on size.
  • Flint, Michigan wants special event and street closure applications at least 60 days ahead and adds a late fee inside 60 days.
  • San Francisco suggests at least three months for a new event and six for a large one.
  • Nashville lists a $785 special event application fee plus a $160 obstruction permit, and amplified sound must end by 11 p.m.

These are published figures at the time of writing (2026). Confirm current fees, deadlines and officer rates with the agency itself before you budget or set a date.

Expect some combination of special event, amplified sound, street closure, tent, food and temporary alcohol permits, plus a fire marshal inspection. Rallies in traditional public forums have First Amendment protection, but cities can still regulate time, place and manner, including stages and amplification. Call the special events office before you apply. A ten-minute conversation saves weeks.

Insurance and certificates of insurance

Almost every venue and city permit will require a certificate of insurance (COI) naming them as additional insured, usually at $1 million per occurrence. One-day special event liability is cheaper than people assume: Insureon reports its customers average about $182 for one day of coverage, though concerts, larger crowds and alcohol sales push premiums higher. Collect COIs from every vendor too, especially the stage, tent, sound and security companies. If a vendor cannot produce one within 48 hours, find another vendor.

Security and crowd management

Security is a plan for who decides to stop the show, where people exit and how a medic reaches the middle of the crowd, not just guards at the gate.

  • Crowd managers. NFPA 101, adopted by many fire marshals, requires at least one trained crowd manager for assembly occupancies and roughly one per 250 people above that, with local variations. Many states offer online crowd manager training. Volunteers can take it.
  • Private guards versus police. Off-duty officer rates vary by city: Columbus, Ohio lists $71 per officer hour with a three-hour minimum, and Bentonville, Arkansas lists $90. Some cities require police for street closures or alcohol.
  • High-profile speakers. A statewide or federal official's advance team will have its own requirements (hold room, secure arrival). Ask early.
  • Counter-protesters. Designate a marshal team trained to de-escalate, keep a buffer, and never engage. Brief the police liaison before doors.

Stage, sound and lighting basics

For a rally of a few hundred people, a portable PA with two powered speakers, a mixer and two wireless handhelds is enough. As a market range, a basic system rents for about $200 to $500 for a half day, and concert-scale setups run $800 to $2,000 or more per day. Rates move with the city, so get at least two quotes. For live bands, hire a company that provides an engineer, monitors and a backline, and send them every performer's tech rider.

Outdoor stages and risers run roughly $500 to $5,000 per day depending on size, roof and wind rating. If the event runs past sunset, light the speaker's face from the front, not just the stage from above, or every photo and TV shot will be a silhouette.

Booking performers, riders and comps

Booking starts with a written offer: date, set length, fee or donated performance, load-in time, what you provide (sound, hospitality, parking) and cancellation terms. The performer answers with a contract and a rider. The technical rider tells your sound company what to bring; the hospitality rider sets food and green room needs. Negotiate anything you cannot meet before you sign, not at load-in.

Comps (complimentary tickets) are a real budget line. Write a comp list with a cap per category: performers' guests, volunteers, press, sponsors and VIPs.

Music licensing: ASCAP, BMI and recorded music at rallies

Playing recorded music in public, or having a band perform covers, is a public performance that needs a license from the performing rights organizations (ASCAP, BMI, plus SESAC and GMR for their catalogs). Venues usually hold these licenses, but campaigns cannot rely on them. ASCAP says its venue licenses for convention centers, arenas and hotels exclude campaign events, and BMI sells a separate Political Entities license that covers campaign events wherever they occur.

Two warnings. Songwriters can ask ASCAP or BMI to exclude their songs from a campaign's license, and once you are notified, playing the song is outside the license. And the license does not stop an artist from objecting publicly or claiming false endorsement. ASCAP recommends getting permission from the artist's management and songwriter in advance, especially for a walk-on or theme song.

Video is a separate rights question. Putting a song in a campaign ad or recap video needs a synchronization license from the music publisher for the composition and, if you use an existing recording, a master use license from whoever owns that recording, usually the label (U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and the Music Marketplace). A public performance license for the event covers neither. Filming or livestreaming the concert itself also needs permissions the event license does not supply: the performers' written consent to be recorded and streamed, and clearance for the songs they perform if the footage is posted or streamed. Put those terms in each performer's contract before the show.

Accessibility and weather plans

Plan accessibility from the site map, not the week of. Local guidance based on ADA standards commonly calls for at least 5% of portable toilets to be accessible, with at least one in each cluster, on an accessible route that is firm and stable (grass and sand do not qualify). Add an accessible viewing area with a clear sightline, an ASL interpreter where cameras can see them, and seating for people who cannot stand for two hours.

Weather plans need written triggers. The National Weather Service says to stay inside a safe building or vehicle at least 30 minutes after the last thunder. Name the person who calls a lightning delay, where the crowd shelters, and the announcement script. Heat is the more common danger in summer: water stations, shade, and a medic with ice.

Campaign rallies: FEC rules on performers, tickets and disclaimers

These are federal rules for federal campaigns. State and local races follow state law, which often mirrors them but not always, so check with your state ethics or elections office.

  • Performers who donate their services. Under 11 CFR 100.74, the value of services an individual volunteers without compensation is not a contribution. The FEC has applied this to entertainers performing at fundraisers. The catch: if anyone else pays the volunteer, that payment becomes an in-kind contribution the campaign must report. The campaign should pay the production costs (sound, travel, hotel) itself. If a venue, label or promoter covers them, you may be receiving an in-kind contribution, and if that entity is a corporation, a prohibited one.
  • Goods are not services. A performer who donates a signed guitar for auction is giving a thing of value, which counts as a contribution.
  • Ticket revenue. The entire ticket price is a contribution, even if the event cost the campaign part of it. A $100 ticket is $100 against the buyer's limit, which is $3,500 per election to a federal candidate in the 2025 to 2026 cycle. Collect donor information (name, address, employer, occupation) at checkout, and keep ticket sales separate from free general-admission RSVPs.
  • Disclaimers. Flyers, posters, yard signs, ads and mass emails paid for by the campaign need a "Paid for by" disclaimer under 11 CFR 110.11. Printed versions must be in a box, readable, with reasonable color contrast.
  • Venues. A corporation donating a venue for free is an in-kind corporate contribution to a federal campaign. Pay the normal rental rate.

Media and photo or video coverage

A rally is partly a production for people who are not there. Build a press riser with a straight-on angle to the podium and a mult box for audio, send a media advisory two to three days out and again that morning, and plan to post a 30 to 60 second recap within hours, not days. That takes a crew that shoots for the edit: one camera locked on the podium, one roaming for crowd reaction and B-roll, and an editor ready before the candidate walks off. These are market ranges drawn from the sources linked above and typical vendor quotes, not Leo Operations rates. Big metros run higher. The $5,000 to $40,000 total in this guide reflects the combinations a smaller production actually buys (a park with no rental fee, a donated set, no lighting, a modest security detail). It is not the sum of every line's maximum, which would describe a much larger show. Confirm each line with the actual vendor, agency or ticketing platform for your date.

Writing the run of show

A run of show is a minute-by-minute script shared with everyone who touches the stage. Include load-in times, sound check order, doors, each speaker or set with exact start and end times, walk-on music, who introduces whom, who holds the mic between acts, the photo moment, the close and the strike. Build in five minutes of slack per hour. Speakers run long. Always.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to organize a concert?

A small community or benefit concert for a few hundred people typically costs $5,000 to $40,000 at market rates once you count venue, permits, insurance, stage, sound, security, restrooms and promotion. That band reflects the line items a smaller production usually needs, not every item at its highest price. Donated performances lower the talent line but not the production costs. Large ticketed concerts with headline acts can reach six figures.

How are concerts organized?

Organizers set a goal and budget, secure a date and venue, file permits and insurance, book performers and production vendors, staff security and volunteers, then promote and execute a written run of show. Permits and venue come first because they have the longest lead times.

How do I get a permit for a rally or concert?

Call your city's special events office, then file a special event application along with any amplified sound, street closure, tent, food or alcohol permits. Deadlines range from about 10 days for small rallies in New York City to 60 to 90 days or more in many cities. Expect to submit a site map, security plan and certificate of insurance.

Do I need a music license for a campaign rally?

Usually yes. Venue licenses often exclude campaign events, so campaigns typically buy their own political licenses from ASCAP and BMI. Artists can still ask to have songs excluded and can object publicly, so get permission for any theme or walk-on song.

Can a musician perform for free at a campaign event?

Yes. Under FEC rules, an individual volunteering their services without compensation is not making a contribution. If anyone else pays the performer, or if a company covers production costs, that becomes an in-kind contribution the campaign must report, and corporate contributions to federal campaigns are prohibited.

How far in advance should I plan a rally?

Give yourself 8 to 12 weeks for an outdoor rally with a stage and street closure, and 12 to 16 weeks for a ticketed concert. A small indoor rally in a rented hall can be pulled together in three to four weeks if the venue already has permits and insurance in place.
