What to do after an election, win or lose: the first 72 hours to day 90

Concession and victory statements, recounts, campaign debt, leftover funds, FEC reports, your data and what comes next, for candidates and the people who run their campaigns.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated November 2026 · 12 min read

The week after an election has four jobs, win or lose: say the right thing publicly, protect the count until it is certified, settle the money, and keep the list and the coalition you built. Federal campaigns that ran in the 2026 general election owe the FEC a post-general report covering activity through November 23, 2026, due December 3, 2026 (FEC). Leftover money can never go to personal use, but it can go to charity, a party, refunds or a future campaign. Everything else on this page is about doing those four jobs in the right order.

Election night is not the finish line. The next 90 days leave you with either debts and a dead email list, or a clean committee and a running start on the next race.

The first 72 hours after an election

Do not concede or declare before the numbers support it

Election night totals are unofficial. Counties keep counting mail and provisional ballots for days, then canvass and certify, and media projections are not results. Before the candidate says anything final, the campaign manager and the data lead should agree on three numbers: the current margin, the number of ballots still outstanding, and the margin that triggers or allows a recount in your state.

Know your state's recount and certification rules

Every state sets its own recount threshold, its own deadline to request one, and its own rule on who pays. Two examples show how different the rules are:

  • Florida. If unofficial returns show a candidate lost by one-half of a percent or less, a machine recount is ordered, unless the losing candidate asks in writing that it not be done (Fla. Stat. 102.141).
  • Pennsylvania. A statewide race inside a 0.5% margin triggers an automatic statewide recount. In the 2024 U.S. Senate race, counties had to start the recount by November 20 and finish by noon on November 26 (WHYY).

For local and legislative races, the county election office and the secretary of state's site have the rules. If you are within range, keep your lawyer and poll watchers on standby for the canvass, and keep your public language calm.

What to say after losing an election

A good concession is short and gracious. It thanks people by role, congratulates the winner, and gives supporters somewhere to put their energy. Sample language:

"A few minutes ago I called [Name] to congratulate her on her win. I asked her to take good care of this district, and I meant it. To every volunteer who knocked a door in the rain, every donor who gave twenty dollars they had to think about, and every staffer who worked nights for this: thank you. We did not get the result we wanted. The issues that brought us together did not go anywhere, and neither am I."

Skip blame and any hint about running again.

What to say after winning an election

Victory remarks thank the team, acknowledge the opponent, and speak to voters who did not pick you:

"I want to thank [Opponent] for a hard-fought race and for calling me tonight. To the people who voted for someone else: I work for you too, starting tomorrow. And to the volunteers and staff in this room, this win belongs to you."

Thank-you messages for election support

Send a thank-you email and text to your full supporter list within 24 hours. Then the candidate calls every major donor, endorser and precinct captain within a week, and staff get a handwritten note. People remember who thanked them after a loss.

What happens to campaign money after an election

Who pays campaign debt after an election

The campaign committee owes its debts, not the vendors and not the voters. Unpaid invoices for mail, printing, ads, field and staff are the committee's obligation until they are paid, settled or forgiven under the rules. A federal committee can raise post-election money to retire its debts as long as each contribution is designated for that election, stays within the donor's limit for that election, and the committee actually has net debts outstanding for that election on the day the check arrives (FEC). An undesignated contribution received after the election counts toward the candidate's next election unless the campaign gets it redesignated. The individual limit for the 2025 to 2026 cycle is $3,500 per election (FEC).

Campaign debt retirement, done right

Build the debt list in the first week: every vendor, amount and due date. Pay staff and small vendors first; a print shop carrying your invoice into December is a reputation problem that follows you into the next race. Then run a short retirement push to your donor list with a clear dollar goal.

Only a terminating committee can settle a debt for less than the full amount. The vendor has to have extended credit in the ordinary course of business, the committee has to have made all reasonable efforts to pay, and the treasurer files the settlement plan on FEC Form 8 and holds payment until the Commission finishes its review (FEC). No vendor is required to forgive or settle anything.

If the candidate loaned the campaign money, those loans can be repaid from contributions raised after the election. The old $250,000 cap on that repayment fell after the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in FEC v. Ted Cruz for Senate, and the FEC removed the regulation that same year (FEC).

Can politicians keep unused campaign funds?

No. Federal law bars converting campaign money to personal use, which means anything that would exist without the campaign or the office: mortgage or rent, clothing, non-campaign car costs, country club dues, vacations, household food and tuition (52 U.S.C. 30114). The same statute lists what leftover federal funds can do:

  • Pay campaign expenses, including for a future federal race.
  • Pay ordinary and necessary expenses of holding federal office.
  • Go to charities described in 26 U.S.C. 170(c).
  • Transfer without limit to national, state or local party committees.
  • Go to state and local candidates where state law allows.
  • Any other lawful purpose that is not personal use.

The FEC also lists refunds to contributors as a use of remaining funds (FEC). If you plan to run again for federal office, the money can transfer from your old principal campaign committee to your new one, provided the funds came from contributions that complied with the law and you are not running for two federal offices at once (11 CFR 110.3).

State and local committees follow state law. Most states have their own list of permitted uses and their own rules for dissolving a committee, so pull your state's guidance from the campaign finance agency before you write a single post-election check.

The FEC post-general report for 2026

Every House and Senate committee whose candidate ran in the November 3, 2026 general files the post-general report on Form 3. The reporting period runs from October 15 through November 23, 2026, and the report is due December 3, 2026 (electronic filings by 11:59 p.m. Eastern). All PACs and party committees file a post-general report on Form 3X regardless of activity (FEC). December 3 is the Thursday after Thanksgiving, so file early.

How to close a campaign: FEC termination vs. staying open

A federal committee can terminate once it no longer receives contributions or makes expenditures, has no outstanding debts, and is not in an audit, enforcement matter or litigation. The termination report covers all unreported activity, including debt retirement, and says how remaining funds are being disposed of. You check the termination box on your regular report and keep filing until the FEC confirms termination in writing (FEC).

Keep the committee open if you plan to run again or are still retiring debt, and budget for the treasurer, because an open committee keeps filing. A dormant committee with sloppy filings is a gift to your next opponent's research team.

Keep the data and campaign assets

The most valuable things a campaign owns after election day are not furniture or signs. They are lists.

  • Voter contact data. Export every canvass result, ID, phone bank disposition and text response. Note who you persuaded, who was a soft no and who moved. A future campaign that starts with that history saves months.
  • Volunteer list. Tag everyone by what they did and how reliable they were. Your best 50 are the core of any next campaign.
  • Donor list. Clean it, note giving history, and send a final update. Donors who hear nothing after a loss give to someone else next time.
  • Website and domain. Renew the domain for several years even if you are not running again. An expired candidate domain can be bought by anyone.
  • Social media accounts. Change the bio, pin a thank-you post, and keep the accounts. Do not delete followers you spent a year earning.
  • Photos and video. Archive every raw file. Footage of a candidate with voters is useful for years.

Asset sales count too. If the committee sells equipment or a list to anyone, including the candidate, it must be at fair market value to avoid personal use (FEC).

If you won the election

Transition and staffing

Campaign staff and official staff are different teams with different rules and payrolls. Pick a transition lead within days, decide who moves into government roles, and read the new office's ethics rules before you promise anyone a job.

Separate official and campaign accounts

Winners need a clean wall between official communication and campaign communication. In the House, official websites and social media accounts may not be used for campaign purposes, members can keep campaign and personal accounts, and those accounts may not use official resources (Congressional Research Service). State legislatures and city governments have their own versions of this rule. Keep the campaign account for politics and a clearly labeled official account for constituent service.

The next cycle starts now

House members run every two years, and every potential challenger reads your first report after swearing-in. Keep a modest fundraising rhythm, keep the volunteer list warm, and keep shooting content while you govern. Our guide to political social media for 2028 covers how to build that content machine.

If you lost the election

What happens when you lose an election

The committee stays open until it is terminated, and the debts and deadlines still apply. Personally, most candidates need a week before making any decision. Take it.

Run an honest debrief with precinct results

When the canvass is final, pull precinct-level results and compare them with your targets: where turnout fell short, where persuasion failed, where you over-performed and what you did there. Match the results against your field data, mail drops and ad flights, and write it down while memories are fresh. Most losing campaigns skip this and repeat the same mistakes.

What do politicians do after losing an election?

They go back to their careers, into advocacy, appointed roles or party leadership, or straight into the next race. The ones who come back strong kept their list active and stayed visible in the community.

Deciding whether to run again

Base the decision on the debrief, not on the mood of election night. Local races come quickly. Nashville, for example, elects its mayor, vice mayor and Metro Council on August 5, 2027 (our 2027 Metro election guide). Federal candidates should read our guides to the 2028 congressional elections and the 2028 presidential primary calendar, and our breakdown of how much it costs to run for office. If you are rebuilding a team, read how to hire a political consultant before you sign anyone.

What the 2026 results mean for 2027 and 2028 candidates

As of November 4, 2026, major news organizations, including NBC News and CNBC's Election Desk, projected that Democrats would control both the House and the Senate starting in January (NBC News, CNBC). Several races were still too close to call, and none of these results are official until states certify them.

For candidates of either party, a change in congressional control reshapes 2028 target lists, party recruitment and where outside money goes. Campaigns that plan to compete in 2027 or 2028 should start building now, while donor and volunteer energy from 2026 is still fresh.

Frequently asked questions

What to do after losing an election?

Wait for the canvass before conceding in a close race, then thank your volunteers, donors and staff by name and congratulate the winner. Build a full list of debts, pay small vendors first, and file every required report. Export your voter, volunteer and donor data, then run a precinct-level debrief before deciding about another run.

What to say after losing an election?

Thank the people who worked for you, congratulate your opponent, and tell supporters the issues still matter. Keep it short and avoid blame or talk of the next race.

What to say after winning an election?

Thank your team first, acknowledge your opponent, and tell the voters who backed someone else that you will represent them too.

Who pays campaign debt after an election?

The campaign committee owes its debts. Federal committees can raise post-election contributions to retire them, designated for that election and within the donor's limit, as long as net debts remain. Only a terminating committee can settle debts for less than the full amount, through the FEC's settlement process.

What happens to campaign funds after an election?

They can pay remaining campaign costs, go to qualifying charities, transfer to party committees, be refunded to donors, or move to the candidate's future federal campaign. State campaigns follow state rules.

Can politicians keep unused campaign funds?

No. Federal law prohibits using campaign money for anything that would exist without the campaign or office, such as mortgage payments, clothing, vacations or tuition.

When is the FEC post-general report due in 2026?

The 2026 post-general report is due December 3, 2026, covering activity from October 15 through November 23, 2026. House and Senate committees file on Form 3, and PACs and party committees file on Form 3X.

How do you close a campaign committee with the FEC?

Pay or settle every debt, dispose of remaining funds through permitted uses, and check the termination box on your regular disclosure report. Keep filing until the FEC confirms termination in writing. A committee in an audit, enforcement matter or litigation cannot terminate.
