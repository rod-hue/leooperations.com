How Much Does a Wedding Videographer Cost, and Is It Worth It?

National averages, the deliverables that move the price, and what the regret data actually says about skipping video.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 10 min read

The average wedding videographer costs about $2,300, according to The Knot 2026 Real Weddings Study, with a median near $1,900, among couples married in 2025. Zola's data puts the average higher, at $3,993, with most couples spending $3,200 to $4,800, drawn from its Wedding Cost Index and a 2026 survey of 720 recently married couples. Only about 37% of couples hire a videographer at all, according to The Knot, which is why video is the vendor people most often debate. For a full day with a cinematic highlight film from an experienced team, $2,500 to $5,000 is the realistic 2026 market range.

The spread is wide because "wedding video" covers everything from one person with a camera filming the ceremony to a two- or three-person crew delivering a color-graded short film with licensed music. Each source samples a different population, so the averages will not match. A likely reason The Knot and WeddingWire read lower is that they include a lot of shorter, simpler coverage; Zola's number sits closer to what an established full-day videographer quotes today.

Zola also points out a city effect: about $6,091 in San Francisco versus about $3,005 in Salt Lake City for comparable 150-guest coverage.

How much does a wedding videographer cost per hour?

Like photographers, most videographers sell packages rather than hourly time. If you divide The Knot's $2,300 average over eight hours, you get under $300 an hour, but that hides the real cost structure. For video, editing is often more time than filming. A five-minute highlight film can take several full days to cut, color and mix.

Zola puts overtime at $200 to $400 per hour depending on the market and the original package. Treat that as the price of added time on the day, not a measure of what the base package costs per hour, since the package price also carries editing, gear and delivery. Zola's package tiers run four to six hours for basic coverage, six to eight for standard, and eight to twelve for premium.

The deliverables that drive the price

The biggest price driver is not hours. It is what you get back. Here is how the common deliverables affect a quote. The dollar ranges are 2026 market ranges drawn from published vendor pricing, not survey averages and not Leo Operations rates.

Highlight film

This is where your money goes. A good highlight film is built around real audio: the vows, a line from a toast, a father's voice. That requires lapel microphones on the couple and officiant, a feed from the DJ's board, and an editor who knows how to cut a story. Ask every videographer you interview how they capture audio. If the answer is vague, keep looking.

Full ceremony edit

For many families, especially those with long religious or cultural ceremonies, the complete ceremony matters more than the cinematic film. Grandparents who could not travel will watch every minute. If this matters to you, make sure it is in writing.

Drone

Drone footage is beautiful for estates, waterfronts and rural venues. Commercial drone pilots in the U.S. need an FAA Part 107 certificate, and many venues and urban areas restrict flights, so ask whether your videographer is certified and whether your venue allows it. Weather and wind can cancel a flight on the day.

Same-day edit

A same-day edit is a short film cut during the wedding and shown at the reception. It is a showstopper, but it is labor intensive. It usually means an additional editor working on site, which is why it is priced as a premium add-on.

Raw footage

Some couples want every clip. Raw files are large, unedited and mostly unwatchable, but they are a good insurance policy if you want someone to re-edit later. Ask how long the videographer keeps archives.

Is it worth it to get a wedding videographer?

Yes. Photos show what the day looked like. Video is the only thing that keeps what it sounded like: your vows in your own voices, the laugh after a toast, a grandparent who is no longer here.

The regret data points the same way. In its 2023 Real Weddings Study, The Knot reported that 39% of couples hired a videographer and 19% said not booking one was among their top wedding regrets. That is a survey of how couples felt about their own weddings, not a prediction for yours, and it is still a striking number for a single vendor. You can add more photos later from guests' phones. You can never go back and record your vows again.

Video earns its place even at small weddings:

  • A short ceremony is still the one moment you will want to hear again, and a professional records the vows with clean, separate audio.
  • Grandparents and relatives who could not travel get to see the day as it happened, not in a few cropped phone clips.
  • Couples who think they will never rewatch it are usually the ones who end up playing it on every anniversary.

A middle path that has grown fast is the "wedding content creator," someone who shoots phone footage for social media. Zola reports these typically cost $500 to $999 with about 24-hour turnaround. That is great for Instagram. It is not a replacement for a professionally recorded ceremony with clean audio.

What is a good budget for a wedding videographer?

Zola suggests about 8% of your total wedding budget for video, roughly $2,400 on a $30,000 wedding. The Knot's budget breakdown puts photographer and videographer combined at about 10% of the budget in its data-based sample breakdown. If you are hiring both, our recommendation is to plan for about 15% of the total for the pair, since The Knot's two vendor averages ($3,000 and $2,300) add up to about 15.5% of its $34,200 average wedding.

Our practical guidance (recommended ranges, not survey figures):

If you want both photo and video and the budget is tight, it is usually smarter to book a coordinated team than to stretch for a top-tier photographer and a bargain videographer. We compare the options in our photographer and videographer guide.

How to compare quotes

  1. Watch two or three full highlight films, plus one full ceremony edit. Listen with headphones. Bad audio is the most common failure in wedding video.
  2. Ask how many shooters, how many cameras on the ceremony, and how they mic the couple and officiant.
  3. Get the deliverables in writing: length of each film, what is included, delivery timeline, music licensing.
  4. Ask about their style. Cinematic, documentary and traditional videography produce very different results. Our wedding videography styles guide explains the difference.
  5. Ask about backups: simultaneous recording to two cards, file storage and what happens if someone is sick.
  6. Check the turnaround. Zola notes traditional videographers can take two to six months. Ask for a date, not "a few months."

Ways to save on wedding video

  • Book a shorter block that starts at the ceremony and ends after toasts and first dance. That captures most of what you will rewatch.
  • Skip the same-day edit and the documentary feature. The highlight plus full ceremony covers most families.
  • Choose a Friday, Sunday or off-season date.
  • Hire a photo and video team that already works together, which can reduce duplicated travel and planning.
  • Ask whether raw footage is included before paying for it as an add-on.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average cost of a wedding video?

The Knot's 2026 Real Weddings Study puts the average at $2,300, with a median of about $1,900. Zola's 2026 data, from its cost index and a survey of 720 recently married couples, puts it at $3,993. Most couples hiring an experienced full-day videographer in 2026 should expect quotes between $2,000 and $5,000.

Is it worth it to get a wedding videographer?

Yes. Video preserves vows, toasts and voices in a way photos cannot. In The Knot's 2023 study, 19% of couples said not booking a videographer was one of their top regrets.

What is a good budget for a wedding videographer?

Plan on about 8% of your total wedding budget, per Zola, or roughly $1,800 to $3,500 for a mid-sized wedding. Larger weddings with two shooters, drone and a longer film commonly run $3,500 to $7,000.

What percentage of weddings have a videographer?

About 37% of couples hired a videographer, according to The Knot's real weddings data. By comparison, the large majority of couples hire a photographer.

How much does a wedding videographer cost per hour?

Most videographers sell packages, not hours. Added time (overtime) typically runs $200 to $400 per hour, according to Zola, which is not the same as the base package's hourly cost. Remember that editing often takes more hours than filming.

Will I regret not having a wedding videographer?

Many do. The Knot reported that 19% of couples in its 2023 study named skipping a videographer as a top regret. If voices, vows and toasts matter to you, or you have elderly relatives, the case for video is strong.

How much does wedding drone footage cost?

As an add-on, drone footage commonly runs $300 to $800. The pilot should hold an FAA Part 107 certificate, and your venue must allow flights. Weather can cancel drone coverage on the day.

Is a content creator a substitute for a videographer?

No. Zola reports content creators typically cost $500 to $999 and deliver phone footage within about a day, which is great for social posts. They do not usually provide multi-camera ceremony coverage, professional audio or a graded film.
