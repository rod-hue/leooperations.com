How much does it cost to run for office? Real numbers from city council to president

What candidates actually spend at every level of American politics, where the money goes, and how self-funded and grassroots campaigns differ.

By Rodriquez Wright II, Founder, Leo Operations · Updated October 2026 · 13 min read

Running for office can cost anywhere from a few hundred dollars to more than a billion. A small-town council or school board race is often won for under $10,000. A competitive state legislative seat now commonly runs $100,000 to $1 million or more. According to OpenSecrets, the average winning U.S. House candidate in 2022 spent about $2.79 million and the average winning Senate candidate about $26.5 million. Those are candidate-committee figures for one cycle; outside spending comes on top.

The price is driven by three things: how many voters you need to reach, how contested the race is, and how much of your communication has to be paid for rather than earned. The rest of this guide breaks the cost down by office, shows where the money goes, and explains the rules that kick in once you start raising and spending.

How much does it cost to run for local office?

Most local races are cheaper than people assume, and most first-time local candidates underspend in the wrong places rather than overall.

City council and school board

In a small city or a single council district, the electorate that actually shows up for a municipal election can be a few thousand people. That is a race you can win with a candidate who knocks doors every weekend, a few hundred yard signs, two or three mail pieces, and a small digital budget. Budgets of $5,000 to $25,000 are normal. School board races are often cheaper still, frequently under a few thousand dollars, unless outside groups decide the board is a battleground, which has happened more often since 2021.

Big-city council seats are different. A district with 150,000 residents, a crowded primary and expensive mail costs real money. New York City's public funds program gives a useful benchmark: the base spending limit for participating council candidates was $228,000 per election in 2025. That limit rises by 50% if a non-participating opponent raises or spends more than half of it, and is lifted entirely above three times it, once the CFB confirms relief.

Mayor

A mayor's race in a town of 20,000 can be run for $15,000 to $50,000. In a mid-sized city of 100,000 to 300,000, $150,000 to $750,000 is common for a competitive open seat. Large-city races are a different sport. In Nashville's 2023 open mayoral race, the third-place finisher, Matt Wiltshire, raised nearly $2 million and loaned his campaign more than $800,000, according to Tennessee Lookout. In New York City's 2025 general election, super PACs supporting Andrew Cuomo spent about $28.4 million, the most ever spent by super PACs for a single NYC candidate, per Citizens Union. He lost.

Our guide on how to run for mayor or city council walks through the full process, including the math for how many votes you need.

How much does it cost to run for state representative or state senate?

State legislative costs have climbed fast. In Wisconsin, a Marquette Law School analysis published in July 2026 found the median Assembly campaign has historically spent roughly $20,000 to $50,000, yet nine of the ten most expensive Assembly races since 2010 happened in 2024, topped by a district where total spending hit about $3.97 million.

That gap is the story everywhere. A safe-seat state representative might spend $30,000. A seat that decides chamber control can cost both sides seven figures. Spotlight PA found competitive Pennsylvania state House races averaged $1.1 million in campaign spending in 2024, and state Senate races $3.5 million, and that excludes outside groups. In North Carolina, legislative candidates raised more than $80 million in 2024 and one Senate seat became the state's first $4 million legislative race, according to the John Locke Foundation.

A reasonable planning range for a contested, non-targeted race: $50,000 to $250,000 for state house and $100,000 to $500,000 for state senate. If your seat is on either party's target list, plan for more and expect outside money on both sides.

How much does it cost to run for Congress?

The most-cited benchmark is OpenSecrets' average for winners: about $2.79 million for a House seat in 2022, roughly three and a half times what the average winner spent in 2000. That average hides a huge spread.

  • A safe incumbent can win cheaply. Rep. Robert Aderholt raised about $1.5 million for his 2024 re-election in a deep-red Alabama seat (KNSI/Stacker).
  • A competitive open or swing seat costs far more. Eugene Vindman raised nearly $18 million to win Virginia's 7th District in 2024 (same source).
  • The most expensive House race ever, New York's 19th District in 2024, topped $50 million in ad spending alone, per AdImpact.

In our planning experience, donors, press and party gatekeepers look closely at a first-time primary challenger's early fundraising, and raising $250,000 to $500,000 in the first reports is a strong signal. A general election in a competitive district commonly runs $3 million to $8 million in candidate money before outside spending. Those are planning benchmarks, not entry requirements; districts and media markets vary enormously. The FEC reports that 1,886 House candidates raised about $2.3 billion in the 2023 to 2024 cycle (FEC).

Average Senate campaign cost

The average winning Senate candidate spent about $26.5 million in 2022 (OpenSecrets). In 2024, Bernie Moreno raised about $26.6 million to win Ohio's seat, while outside groups spent roughly $296 million supporting or opposing the candidates in that one race, according to OpenSecrets data cited by KNSI/Stacker. The FEC counted 266 Senate candidates raising about $1.5 billion in the 2023 to 2024 cycle.

In small, cheap media markets, a Senate race can still be run for under $10 million. In a battleground with expensive TV, $50 million to $100 million in candidate money is no longer unusual. In June 2026, AdImpact projected a record $11.6 billion in total political ad spending for the 2025 to 2026 cycle, above the roughly $11.1 billion to $11.2 billion it has reported for 2024 in different updates, with Senate and governor races driving the increase. That is a projection of total advertising by all sponsors, not candidate budgets (Reuters via GV Wire).

How much does it cost to run for governor?

Governor races swing more than any other office because state size and self-funding vary so much. A small-state governor's race can be won for $3 million to $10 million. Large states run into nine figures. J.B. Pritzker spent about $323 million of his own money across his 2018 and 2022 Illinois campaigns (Illinois Policy). In California's 2026 race, ad spending supporting Tom Steyer alone reached $201.4 million by late May 2026, per AdImpact data reported by MediaPost.

How much does it cost to run for president?

Getting on a ballot is cheap. New Hampshire's presidential primary filing fee in the 2024 cycle was $1,000 (NH Secretary of State). Being competitive is not. The FEC reports presidential candidates raised about $2 billion and spent about $1.8 billion in the 2023 to 2024 cycle, and OpenSecrets estimated the full presidential race, including outside groups, at about $5.5 billion. A minor candidate can file with the FEC and appear on a handful of ballots for under $100,000, but a viable primary campaign needs tens of millions just to survive to the early states.

Filing fees and the FEC $5,000 threshold

Before you spend a dollar on voters, you pay to get on the ballot, either with a fee, signatures, or both. Ballotpedia's 2026 ballot access overview shows the range:

U.S. House$10,440 (Florida)$50 (New Hampshire)
U.S. Senate$10,440 (Delaware, Florida)$100 (Alaska, New Hampshire)
Governor$3,750 (Texas)$100 (Alaska, New Hampshire)
State senate$1,250 (Texas)$10 (New Hampshire)
State house$750 (Texas)$2 (New Hampshire)

Signature routes can be just as demanding. Utah requires 7,000 signatures for a U.S. House candidate using that path, and Michigan and New York require 15,000 for governor. Local offices are set by city charter and county election offices, often 25 to a few hundred signatures.

For federal office, the key trigger is $5,000. Under FEC rules, once you raise or spend more than $5,000 you become a candidate and must file a Statement of Candidacy within 15 days, and your principal campaign committee must register within 10 days after that. Money spent only "testing the waters" does not count toward the threshold until you actually decide to run. Individual donors can give up to $3,500 per election to a federal candidate in the 2025 to 2026 cycle (FEC). State and local races follow their own state rules, and many states require you to appoint a treasurer before you take in or spend any money at all.

Where does campaign money go?

Strip away the specifics and nearly every campaign spends on the same buckets:

  • Paid media. Broadcast and cable TV, streaming, radio and digital ads. In congressional, statewide and big-city races this is usually the largest line. See our breakdown of political TV ad costs.
  • Field. Canvassers, organizers, voter file access, walk lists and GOTV. In local races this should be your biggest investment, often through a paid canvassing program.
  • Mail and texting. Persuasion and turnout mail, peer-to-peer texts.
  • Staff and consultants. Campaign manager, finance director, field director, plus general, media, polling and digital consultants.
  • Fundraising costs. Call time tools, events, digital list building, and payment processing fees.
  • Overhead and compliance. Office, phones, software, legal and treasurer services.
  • Research and polling. A benchmark poll, opposition and self research.

A long-standing rule of thumb from campaign trainer Jim Arnold, published by Aristotle, is to aim for about 65% of spending on direct voter contact and treat anything above 35% on everything else as a warning sign. We agree with the spirit of that. For the percentage splits we recommend by race type and sample budgets, read our political campaign budget guide.

Self-funded vs. grassroots campaigns

Federal law puts no limit on how much candidates can spend from their own personal funds, though every dollar must be reported (FEC). Since the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in FEC v. Ted Cruz for Senate, candidates can also be repaid for personal loans above $250,000 using money raised after the election. Many states have their own rules, so check before you write a check.

A self-funded run buys speed: staff, early TV and vendors locked in before opponents. It does not buy a donor base, volunteers or the signal that people believe in you, and big self-funders lose all the time.

A grassroots run looks different. The candidate spends 15 to 25 hours a week on call time early, builds an email and text list, and leans on volunteers and door-to-door contact to stretch every dollar. It is slower, but the list and the network carry into the next race. In local and state legislative contests, a disciplined grassroots campaign beats a lazy rich one more often than people expect. In congressional and statewide races, grassroots energy still needs to turn into enough money for paid media in the final six to eight weeks.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to run for local office?

Most local races cost between $1,000 and $25,000 for school board or small-city council, and $10,000 to $150,000 for mayor of a smaller city. Big-city council and mayoral races can run from six figures into the millions. The biggest variables are how many voters turn out and whether outside groups get involved.

How much does it cost to run for Congress?

The average winning House candidate spent about $2.79 million in 2022, according to OpenSecrets. Safe incumbents often spend $1 million to $2 million, while competitive swing-seat races can top $15 million in candidate money alone. Filing fees range from $50 in New Hampshire to $10,440 in Florida.

How much does it cost to run for state representative?

A contested state house race usually costs $25,000 to $250,000, with safe seats on the low end. Races that decide control of a chamber can exceed $1 million. Competitive Pennsylvania House races averaged $1.1 million in 2024.

How much does it cost to run for state Senate?

State senate districts are bigger, so plan for roughly $75,000 to $500,000 in a contested race. Battleground seats cost much more: competitive Pennsylvania Senate races averaged $3.5 million in 2024, before outside spending.

What is the average Senate campaign cost?

The average winning U.S. Senate candidate spent about $26.5 million in 2022. Costs depend heavily on state size and TV markets, and outside groups often outspend the candidates in battleground states.

How much does it cost to run for governor?

A small-state governor's race can be run for $3 million to $10 million. Large-state races commonly cost $50 million to more than $200 million when outside money and self-funding are included.

How much does it cost to run for president?

Filing for a single primary ballot can cost as little as $1,000, as in New Hampshire. A competitive presidential campaign needs hundreds of millions of dollars for the primary and more than $1 billion as a nominee. Presidential candidates spent about $1.8 billion in the 2023 to 2024 cycle.

When do I have to register with the FEC?

Once you raise or spend more than $5,000 for a federal office, you are a candidate and must file a Statement of Candidacy within 15 days. Your committee then has 10 days to register. Testing-the-waters money does not count toward the threshold until you decide to run.

Can I run for office with no money?

At the smallest local level, yes. Many school board and small-council candidates win on a few hundred dollars, a disciplined walk plan built from the voter file, and relentless door knocking. You still need to cover the filing fee or signatures, and every office above that level requires a real fundraising plan from day one.
